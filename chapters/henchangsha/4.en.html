Chapter Four: Tao

“Tao” — Of the Four Fiends of antiquity, Taowu was the untamable one: proud, vicious, scornful of virtue; even after running headlong into the south wall, it would not turn back.

Not long after losing his job at the overseas internet company, on the day of the Dragon Boat Festival, Old Zheng picked up a basketball and arranged a game with a few former colleagues. On the court they ran into some high schoolers, and a few men in their thirties ended up taking on the high schoolers. Words were exchanged; Old Zheng went up for a shot, came down wrong, and by some freak twist managed to break his own right leg.

Zhang Jingyu got the child settled and rushed to the hospital, where she found Old Zheng half-sitting up in bed, one leg in a fresh cast.

Zhang Jingyu frowned. “How did this happen? You broke your leg playing ball?”

Old Zheng said with a grin, “Well, now I can relax. I haven’t found a job anyway, so I’ll just have to take a vacation like a good boy. Babe, can you keep me?”

Zhang Jingyu shot him a reproachful glare, went off to pay the bill, rented a wheelchair and wheeled Old Zheng home.

Several months later, as it happened, a cousin of Zhang Jingyu’s who was a leader in the Hefei Economic and Technological Development Zone heard that her husband was out of work and phoned her. “Your Northwestern Polytechnical has set up a startup-investment industrial park incubator here in Hefei, and I know the guy running it really well. You and Old Zheng are both Northwestern Polytechnical people, and he’s worked in the system all these years. Why not have him come be a business lead at the incubator?”

Zhang Jingyu said, “I know you mean well, but I don’t want Old Zheng leaving Beijing.”

Her cousin said, “It’s a pretty good opportunity. There’s no permanent establishment post, but on the plus side the salary’s market-rate, a good deal higher than in the system, and there are bonuses from investment returns. And whoever runs this incubator has to understand technology — it suits your Old Zheng.”

Zhang Jingyu said, “Oh, the child’s too young. I can’t even cook. I don’t want him going to another city.”

But in the end Zhang Jingyu couldn’t help herself. Old Zheng had been recovering for months — he could walk but not run — and spent his days at home on the internet; he got on well with his in-laws and often cooked while waiting for his wife to come home. That day Old Zheng carried the pork rib soup to the table, wiped his hands on his apron, and the moment he sat down, Zhang Jingyu couldn’t help telling him about her cousin’s call.

Zheng Zhiyong asked, “Did your cousin say what the name is of the person who went over to run that Northwestern Polytechnical incubator?”

Zhang Jingyu said, “It didn’t even occur to me to ask.”

Zheng Zhiyong: “Ask him now?”

Zhang Jingyu messaged her cousin on WeChat on the spot, and the reply came back: two words, “Yu Hong,” shining clean and clear in the middle of the screen. Zhang Jingyu held up the phone for Old Zheng to see.

The couple laughed together. “Isn’t that our advisor’s top senior disciple?”

After dinner Zheng Zhiyong called his senior, Yu Hong, and before long the Hefei job was settled. The senior was already a college-level leader at Northwestern Polytechnical. The incubator was one of those the government guides universities to build in local industrial parks: the government puts up the money, the university supplies technology and people to turn research results into industry. Zheng Zhiyong would go with Yu Hong to negotiate funding from local governments in various places, and also find suitable projects to bring into the incubator — all things that seemed right up Zheng Zhiyong’s alley.

After Zheng Zhiyong went to work in Hefei, he lived a two-city life: leaving by train every Sunday, coming back to Beijing by train every Friday. But compared with the internet company his salary had dropped by more than half, to about twenty-some thousand, and once you added round-trip fares and rent in Hefei, Old Zheng felt his income just couldn’t get anywhere.

Two-plus years in, the incubator really had raised a lot of money from the government, but it was also supporting dozens of technical staff, with application areas including servo motor control systems, robotics, simulation, even the underlying operating systems for robot controllers. There was a grand office, a high-end exhibition hall displaying robotics products and half-products one after another, and an empty factory floor stacked with imposing heavy equipment. Being in the middle of it all, Zheng Zhiyong gradually discovered that although these technical people had plenty of skill, almost none of them had any business sense. R&D projects came one after another, but it was very hard to actually pin down a concrete commercial use case, and harder still to find anyone with real business thinking to take these technologies and use cases to market. Every project team in the incubator was made up of people with a scientist’s mindset and no feel for the market, and one company name after another soon turned into a gray logo, dull and lusterless on the display wall.

Old Zheng went to Yu Hong and put forward his idea: the big equipment hall downstairs had complete infrastructure and every facility — could the incubator itself lead a company, built around robot chassis technology, go after some market deals and try to commercialize? To his surprise, the moment Yu Hong heard it, he said he’d fully back Old Zheng in the attempt, let him build his own team from the existing staff, and put up the first round of start-up funds from the incubator’s fund along with the premises. Meanwhile Old Zheng could still draw a base salary from his old unit, with his social insurance kept there too. With support this strong from Yu Hong, Old Zheng decided to go all in.

The company was named Qingzhi Guanglun — Zheng Zhiyong came up with the name — with a registered capital of one million yuan. The incubator held fifty-one percent, Old Zheng twenty-five percent, and the four other core members twenty-four percent. As everyone had agreed, the registered capital had to actually be paid in. Old Zheng normally handed his salary over at home, so when it came to discussing his share of the capital with Zhang Jingyu he felt uneasy, afraid she wouldn’t agree.

Zhang Jingyu asked, “After this investment, roughly when will there be revenue, and roughly how long until there’s profit?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “A year. It’ll definitely be profitable within a year.”

Zhang Jingyu shot him a look and transferred two hundred fifty thousand to his account, and Zheng Zhiyong had a company of his own. Just as he’d said, in the company’s first year, through the connections of a few old classmates, they did two or three million in revenue; since the core members were still drawing salaries from the incubator, that year’s dividends basically paid back everyone’s investment.

In the second year, a component delivery from Zheng Zhiyong’s team had quality problems and all of it had to be redone; not only did they make no money, that year’s profit was basically wiped out. At this point Zheng Zhiyong felt this venture wasn’t reliable either and started looking at opportunities elsewhere. Especially after he got the offer from Hanhai, his mind was basically made up. He went to find another brother who’d started the company with him, Qi Jin. Qi Jin had his bachelor’s and master’s from USTC, had held twelve percent of the company, came from a family with some background in the system, and was a capable right-hand man with ability on both the technical and business sides. The two met in Hefei at a stinky mandarin fish restaurant and drank a few liang of baijiu.

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Old Qi, after all this time I finally get it — you’re the real backbone of this company.”

Old Qi: “Brother Zheng, don’t say that. The guys all came to work for you.”

Old Zheng said, “Look — whatever happens at the company, when we’re chasing a deal, you’re the one cracking the technical problems; when we’re rushing a delivery, you’re the one leading the team on the front line, grinding it out; when something goes wrong, you’re the one who stays calm. I truly respect you, and I truly think you’re better suited to be CEO of this company than I am.”

Old Qi: “Brother Zheng, you can’t say that. When the project started, the hardest technical point was you leading the guys through it, one coil at a time. I truly admire your technical skill, and I want to work with you.”

Old Zheng: “I’m not like you. You’re anchored at the company like the pillar that calms the sea. I’m always going back to Beijing — the kid’s little, there’s a lot to worry about at home. I didn’t invite you out for drinks to set a trap. I’m sincerely making way for someone more capable. If you take the CEO job, you’ll be doing me a big favor.”

Old Qi: “That’s not right. Besides, how would we explain it to Boss Yu?”

Old Zheng said, “I’ll handle Boss Yu. Wait to hear from me. It’s not a big problem.”

Old Zheng went on, “I’m thinking of transferring eighteen percent of my shares to you at the registered capital value — a hundred and eighty thousand. That way I keep seven percent and you’ll have thirty percent. You be CEO and take full charge of everything at the company. I’ll keep handling outside business; when I get orders I’ll steer them into the company, and when there are technical problems we’ll solve them together like before. It’s just that I really, really have to go back to Beijing. I’m not making much money, I’m never home, and my wife’s resentment is huge. If I don’t go back, my little family won’t survive.” With that he let out a long sigh, picked up his glass first and looked at Old Qi. “Come on, brother.” So Old Qi raised his glass as well and clinked it against his.

A few days later Zheng Zhiyong joined Hanhai Chenxing.

That morning Zheng Zhiyong first drove his wife to her work. As Zhang Jingyu got out of the car she said to him, “Honey, actually I don’t need you to make all that much money. I just hope you can stay in Beijing and find a steady job. Go a bit slower, stay a bit longer. I don’t think it’s good to keep changing jobs. With this Hanhai job, try to stay as long as you possibly can.” Zheng Zhiyong nodded and waved, and Zhang Jingyu walked off.

Zheng Zhiyong drove to this classified defense company in the Western Hills and walked into the office. It was a huge control room; an electronic screen showed satellite orbits, and the twinkling little stars marked the company’s first successes over the past few years.

A receptionist in a business suit led Zheng Zhiyong into the office of the sales director, Hu Yibo, who held out his hand and said with a smile, “Welcome to Hanhai Chenxing.”

A month later, Old Zheng got a call from Lu You. Lu You said, “Engineer Zheng, have you already gone to Hanhai Chenxing?”

Old Zheng: “I’m already there, Mr. Lu.”

Lu You: “I was just saying we’ve got a big machined motor part we want to have custom-made at your old startup. Are they still doing that kind of work?”

Old Zheng: “Mr. Lu, I’m completely out of that now. My old partner Qi Jin took it over. You two have met, haven’t you? If you want to work together, just talk to him directly.”

Lu You said, “Fine, fine, I’ve got Engineer Qi’s contact. Engineer Zheng, how’s your new company?”

Old Zheng: “Even better than I expected. It’s like a door to a new world just opened. The way they do things is so methodical. I’m finally getting a close-up look at a good company, and I can finally see it clearly — they’re so clear about their core value proposition.”

Lu You said, “Great, great, Engineer Zheng, I’m happy for you. Let’s get together sometime and you can tell me more.”

Old Zheng hung up and kept driving along the narrow wooded road. After a while his phone rang again. Old Zheng picked up. “Engineer He, long time no talk.” Engineer He was a good friend from Old Zheng’s days in the system; they’d joined the academy in the same intake of graduates. He said to Old Zheng, “There’s a pretty big SAR remote sensing company in Hunan called Yuanwang. Their head of technology wanted to ask me some technical questions. I looked, and what he’s asking about is exactly the direction you worked on at the academy a few years back — I figure you’d have it in the bag. If you’re interested, see whether you’d give them some technical guidance. Paid. I’ll put you in a group chat and you can deal with each other directly from there.”

Old Zheng said, “I’m interested. Set up the group.”

Old Zheng drove on. Winter was coming; the tall trees on both sides were bare and bleak, yet they still stood ramrod straight, with a commanding air. After a while he left the stretch of road lined with tall trees and turned onto the busy West Fifth Ring Road. There were plenty of cars but no jam, and traffic moved fast; his white car ran freely down the lane like an antelope. The sky slowly darkened, and in the distance the Western Hills, set off by a bank of red evening clouds, looked especially bewitching.
