Fuzhu

Chapter Twenty: Fuzhu

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Fuzhu” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Central Mountains, Book Three”), on Mount Ao’an there is a beast shaped like a white deer with four antlers, called the fuzhu; when it appears, its town is flooded. As the saying goes: a white deer with four antlers — the flood is already on its way before anyone sees it.

Zheng Zhiyong met Zhang Hailu the very next day. Shasha introduced them, and they arranged to spend an afternoon over tea at an out-of-the-way teahouse in Xiangtan. On that winter afternoon, without a breath of wind, Zhang Hailu told it from where he had stood, and little by little the early days of Yuanwang — the founding of the business, and the tangled feelings between Gao Fei, Ms. Yan’er and the rest of them — came together, clear and whole, in Old Zheng’s head.

When Yuanwang was first founded, this was how it started. Sister Yan’er had been Gao Fei’s junior in the same school, four years below him. Because the two were a couple at the time, after graduating they both stayed on at the university as teachers, and soon they married and became a husband-and-wife pair of professors. While he was at the university, Gao Fei had orders for some outside technical projects that brought in a few million a year — good profit, and not hard to deliver. So Gao Fei had Sister Yan’er resign from the university and set up this technical-solutions company in her name. The first technical employee it hired was Zhang Hailu. He had in fact been a master’s student in the lab Sister Yan’er ran when she taught at Xiangnan, so even back in school he’d been helping Gao Fei and Sister Yan’er with projects and earning a bit on the side. When Gao Fei proposed setting up the technical-solutions company, Sister Yan’er’s and Zhang Hailu’s technical abilities were in fact the cornerstone of the company’s technical strength. Zhang Hailu was the student Sister Yan’er relied on most, so it was only natural that he became the company’s first employee, and its one and only head of technology. After that they had two or three orders a year, a few million a year that kept the business running, and the company stayed at a dozen-odd employees — Ms. Yan’er, her younger brother and her brother’s wife all worked there. In the company’s fourth year, one of Gao Fei’s old university classmates — also Sister Yan’er’s senior in the school — was promoted to deputy director of a client unit, and in the course of one conversation they got wind of an important project. Zhang Hailu took on the job at a critical moment. With Sister Yan’er he led a few of the company’s key people, and they also rallied the other teachers and students from Gao Fei’s lab at Xiangnan University as technical backbone. More than ten technical staff worked for half a year, hard and without letup, and at last they landed an order worth 20 million. And this order was a long-term one: since the product was successfully commissioned into service, there would be follow-up procurement on the project for several years to come. That order completely changed the company’s fundamentals. It grew from a dozen-odd people to more than fifty, and in a year or two the business was taking in more than a hundred million in revenue, with a very respectable profit margin.

By then the company’s headcount was close to a hundred. Business was growing at a furious pace, they had turned one core product into a real product line, and that product, which had started out in defense work, could gradually move into the commercial market too. And Gao Fei, as a professor at Xiangnan, faced a choice. A government-led industrial fund was going to invest 200 million yuan at a valuation of 1.5 billion, but it required Gao Fei to run the company as its actual controller and give up his post as a university professor — in other words, quit his golden rice bowl.

At the time Ms. Yan’er and Gao Fei talked it over. With the business booming, his resigning wasn’t actually a hard decision. What was truly hard to decide was that the 200 million investment came with a valuation-adjustment clause, a performance bet: the company had to IPO within seven years, or else the founder, Gao Fei, as actual controller, would be on the hook for the bet on the capital, meaning that after eight years he would have to buy back the investment at 12 percent a year.

Ms. Yan’er felt the money carried too much risk — and the company not going public was itself an outcome they would have to chase — so for the time being she talked her husband out of it. Not long afterward, Diao Kexin joined the company.

That year the chairman’s son, Gao Ju, had just started third grade. Because the company his parents had founded was going like a house on fire in those years, and both parents were buried in work, Gao Fei and Ms. Yan’er agreed between them to send their son Gao Ju to an international school in Changsha. He moved from the public primary school by their front door to a boarding school out in the suburbs.

When Diao Kexin joined the company, she knew its business was in the middle of a remarkable surge. Because it was then at the stage of technical breakthroughs, and her husband at the time, Li Fulang, was still in the university lab taking part in some of the R&D, the moment word came that the project had landed and the pre-research had succeeded, Diao Kexin, as if answering the call of fate, begged her husband to get her into Yuanwang as an ordinary sales specialist.

That evening Diao Kexin and her husband met Gao Fei and his wife for the first time. Diao Kexin was twenty-eight then, and Chairman Gao Fei was forty-seven. He was slender, tall and pale, with a refined pair of gold-rimmed glasses. Because Gao Fei had taught at the university for years and was always absorbed in technology and research, and apart from a bit of running had no other hobbies, he looked much younger than his real age, and standing beside Diao Kexin’s short husband, he looked especially tall and handsome. Sister Yan’er was four years younger than Gao Fei, a few years below him in school; the two had met while Gao Fei was a graduate student and married a few years after graduating, and had been married ever since. Sister Yan’er was of medium height, fair-skinned, just a little plump, with features that had something of a well-bred young lady about them. She wore no makeup and also wore glasses, and when the two of them stood together they had, unmistakably, the air and look of a professor couple, a pair of intellectuals. Besides that, Sister Yan’er was mild-tempered and spoke clearly and precisely, every point fitting neatly into the next, with the steadiness of the lady of the house. Both couples came away from that evening with a very good impression of each other, and not long after, Diao Kexin joined the company.

Diao Kexin often had to travel with the chairman and Zhang Hailu to see clients in Beijing. Since she had no technical background and no industry knowledge, when she went with the company’s bosses to meetings at the clients’ she would always earnestly take out her notebook and write everything down meticulously, and whenever she had the chance she’d learn from the chairman and Zhang Hailu about business details that needed preparing in advance, and prepare for them very thoroughly.

A girl like this, willing to work, eager to learn, twenty years younger than Gao Fei — through those days she followed quietly behind the chairman, plain and unremarkable, without a sound. When Gao Fei strode forward, Diao Kexin’s burning gaze followed him close.

One night they all got drunk at a client’s. Diao Kexin helped the unsteady Gao Fei back to his hotel room. After he threw up he lay flat on his back on the bed. Diao Kexin carefully took off his shoes and socks, straightened his arms and legs, pulled the covers over him, and even thoughtfully opened a bottle of mineral water and set it on the chairman’s nightstand. When she’d done all this, she didn’t leave. Instead she lay down quietly on the sofa by the bed, fully dressed, and quietly fell asleep.

In the night Gao Fei woke up thirsty, saw the water set out by the bed, took it and gulped it down, and then saw the little girl asleep on the sofa on the far side of the bed.

By now he was more than half sober. He got out of bed softly and went over to Diao Kexin. She was asleep on her side on the sofa, still in her shoes, so her feet weren’t up on the sofa but hanging over its edge. Her sleeping face — fair, baby-fat cheeks full of collagen, eyes tightly closed, long lashes in the yellow lamplight — looked like a sweet little doll.

Gao Fei patted Diao Kexin lightly. She woke with a start and sat bolt upright. Gao Fei asked, “What are you doing sleeping here?” Diao Kexin said, “Chairman Gao, I was afraid you’d drunk too much and might feel sick later, so I thought I’d stay with you a while. If you’re all right now, I’ll go back.”

Gao Fei felt a warmth in his heart. “Hurry back and get some sleep.”

And Diao Kexin left Gao Fei’s room.

After that, the next time they traveled together, going into the airport, Gao Fei would also pay attention to this little girl behind him, watch her pace, slow his stride and walk at her side. Zhang Hailu was with them that day. Walking toward the gate, he was behind Gao Fei and Diao Kexin, and he could see that the two of them were a good thirty centimeters apart in height. Their real ages were nearly twenty years apart, but because Gao Fei had kept himself in such good shape, from the outside they looked only seven or eight years apart. That day Gao Fei even offered to put Diao Kexin’s bag up in the overhead bin for her. In Zhang Hailu’s eyes, a chairman looking out for a junior employee was a gentlemanly and decent thing to do.

The three of them landed in Beijing and ran into a blizzard, high winds and a sharp drop in temperature. So when they got to the hotel, Gao Fei suggested the three of them go to a mall nearby and buy something thick and warm to wear. Zhang Hailu had thought ahead when he packed and happened to have a spare down jacket in his bag, so he begged off. And so that day Gao Fei and Diao Kexin set off for the mall together, just the two of them. Gao Fei found a Nike store, tried on a pure black down jacket and bought it straightaway. Diao Kexin kept him company, and when he’d paid he said to her, “You’re not wearing much either. Pick something for yourself.” Diao Kexin nodded and asked the clerk, “Is there a women’s version of this jacket?” The clerk said, “No.” Diao Kexin said, “Then could you get me the same men’s jacket in a small to try on?”

Because she was so tiny, even the smallest men’s size hung loose on her, far too big. But she took one look in the mirror and said, “It’s great, I’ll take this one.” And Gao Fei paid for hers too.

The two of them went out into the street together and had dinner, just the two of them. Diao Kexin took the chance to get the chairman to tell her about the first half of his life. The chairman’s studies and career ran in very clean lines: a top degree, a teaching post, a junior from his school, a wife, a son, a first venture, one big business opportunity, a small company grown into a mid-sized firm of more than a hundred people taking shape. And now a government industrial fund had taken a shine to them; an agreement for a 200-million-yuan capital increase was lying right there on his desk. To the young girl in front of him, the figure was without a doubt astronomical. She couldn’t stop herself from worshipping the man in front of her. With infatuated eyes she praised his career and praised his good looks. She said, “You’re like an eagle. You’re going to soar — fly high, just like your name.” It had been a long time since Gao Fei had felt such a fiery, scorching look from a woman, and this sudden adoration stirred him up no end.

In short, after that dinner, Gao Fei’s heart was rippling all over, and this woman had sown every ripple. Not long after, the two of them went to bed together on a business trip, and from then on, whenever they traveled together, they stayed together. Apart from when they absolutely had to go out and work, the two of them made love madly in bed from morning till night, and from then on the chairman arranged a business trip whenever he had the chance. Gao Fei fell madly for Diao Kexin’s body, and Diao Kexin, with her own peculiar, captivating sincerity — her worship of this man, her abandon and frenzy in bed — step by step conquered his heart completely.

You could say that before long, Gao Fei had fallen for her entirely.

As CTO, Zhang Hailu often traveled with Gao Fei and Diao Kexin, the three of them. In time he too could sense that the looks between the two had become a little delicate. But as a tech guy, it wasn’t his place to speculate about things that had nothing to do with him — all the more so since the other man was not only his chairman but his teacher’s husband, a famous professor in the school, as close to him as family. So even with doubts in his heart, he never tried to confirm his suspicions; out of a scholar’s sense of propriety, he even went out of his way to avoid the moments when he might find something out. Gradually, as Gao Fei and Diao Kexin grew ever closer, more and more often Gao Fei stopped traveling with Zhang Hailu and took only Diao Kexin with him to business occasions around the country.

The affair had gone on for three or four months when Diao Kexin found out she was pregnant.

She went to Gao Fei and told him the news. Gao Fei panicked. It wasn’t that he didn’t love Diao Kexin. Right then, he and Diao Kexin were at the most inseparable point in their lives; the two of them grabbed every chance to be together, wildly, away from home. He’d been a good student, a good teacher, a good husband — when had he ever known a life in bed this utterly unrestrained? And with a woman twenty years younger than him, no less.

But he couldn’t just not think about anything. He had a wife whom he loved and who loved him deeply — and what’s more, this wife had built their business empire together with him from the ground up. She had real technical grounding and was a fine hand at running a company. Before he came on full-time, the company’s business was already in perfect order, and after he resigned and joined Yuanwang, the things Sister Yan’er went on handling — the middle and back office, delivery, production — never cost him half a thought.

Besides, Sister Yan’er was an emotionally steady and understanding partner. She almost never meddled in Gao Fei’s arrangements; about his frequent trips lately and his not coming home, she never asked a thing. She trusted her husband deeply and had never doubted him in the slightest.

Diao Kexin said, “Will you marry me?”

Gao Fei: “Right now, I can’t.”

Diao Kexin said, “Gao Fei, I’ve fallen in love with you. I don’t want to be apart from you. I want to have your child and be with you for the rest of my life.”

Gao Fei: “I love you too, I really do. I want our child too.”

Diao Kexin: “But if you won’t get divorced, how am I supposed to keep this child?”

Gao Fei said, “So are you planning to get divorced?”

Diao Kexin said, “I’m carrying your child. If I’m going to have it, divorce is the only road I’ve got.”

Gao Fei said, “How would you get divorced — tell him?”

Diao Kexin said, “Never mind how I get divorced. I’ll be divorced very soon. Just tell me whether you’ll get divorced for me, so I can decide what to do about this child.”

Gao Fei said, “I owe Ms. Yan’er a proper settling-up. I can’t drop everything at this stage and be with you. If I lost the business, I couldn’t take care of you and the child either. But I’ll take a stretch of time to sort all this out, make arrangements with Ms. Yan’er, and then bring the company with me and marry you.”

Diao Kexin said, “What arrangements are you planning?”

Gao Fei said, “I’ll give her 8 percent of the equity, and all the cash in what the two of us own jointly right now. I’m thinking of taking that 200-million capital increase from the industrial investors. That would put the company’s valuation at 1.5 billion yuan, and Ms. Yan’er would get equity worth 120 million — enough for her and our son for the rest of their lives. But I have to keep this company. Without the business I’d have no way to settle you properly, and no way to settle my own conscience either. That side is my first wife and my child, too. If I don’t get these arrangements right, I can’t be with you.”

“But I will — I’ll sort it all out as fast as I can.”

Diao Kexin said, “You have to promise me you’ll marry me. Otherwise I’m giving up this child.”

Gao Fei: “I promise you, I will marry you. I love you, I love our child, and I’ll give him a home.”

With Gao Fei’s promise in hand, Diao Kexin, in a very short time, sold the young couple’s flat in Changsha. That flat had been bought for her and her husband by her mother-in-law, who had sold the family factory back in her hometown so the couple would have a home to wait for the next generation in. She sold the flat and parked the money for the time being on her younger brother’s bank card, which she kept in her own hands. At the same time she moved all the savings in her and her husband’s names onto that card as well. Diao Kexin’s husband didn’t manage money; long after she’d made all these financial arrangements, he still never noticed a thing. Then she took the occasion of a meal her husband was having with a visiting overseas scholar at the university to accuse him of cheating on her. In front of everyone, her husband, who’d had autism since childhood, didn’t argue back. He only explained in English to the other visiting scholar, a woman, that the person who had come was his wife, who might have misunderstood their relationship. When the female professor from Britain heard Professor Li’s quiet explanation, she got up and left the restaurant, leaving behind a room taut with tension and a crowd of people who had seen the whole show with their own eyes.

The Western restaurant was right next to the university, so very soon the whole university knew.

Diao Kexin felt that, since she was pregnant, heaven itself had given her this chance. She wanted to gamble — gamble that with this child she’d get Gao Fei to marry her. Gao Fei’s company was already worth 1.5 billion, and by Gao Fei’s own account he would bring the rest of the shares with him and marry her. He said he wanted her child. But she wasn’t sure of it either. Sister Yan’er was too deeply bound up with the company; the company’s whole public image was the story of a professor couple starting a business together. But right now, if she wanted to get Gao Fei to marry her, she needed to have this child. She decided to have it. Best of all if it were a boy, she thought.

But what if — just what if — he didn’t get divorced? Having this child would mean she’d need more money, more resources to survive. So all the property from her previous marriage, every cent in that marriage, the property of her previous husband’s family — she wanted all of it in her own hands too.

Her desperately poor childhood still lived inside her body. Memories of hunger, of poverty, of hard labor often washed over her thoughts in her dreams. She wanted that glossy life too, the life of the people on top. She was already the lover of one of those people, and that person had promised to marry her, to love her, to be with her. She could already see the ladder that climbed up into that world above, every rung of it so clear. She knew this child was a stake she had to win, and that her first husband was the first rung of her life’s ladder.

The moment she planted her foot on her husband’s body and climbed upward, she gave no thought to whether that blind, reckless step might kill him. And yes, Li Fulang very nearly died. He lost all his money, his reputation, his standing, his beautiful wife, his mother’s health — but he was lucky, too. He didn’t die. Pine Billows Grove saved his life; he only limped from then on, that was all. He never again had a single word of news from his wife.

He didn’t even know that his wife would soon become a mother.

Diao Kexin decided to have the child. This child would be her second rung. She needed to push Gao Fei, firmly, to get that money — that 200-million investment — and drive Yuanwang’s valuation up to 1.5 billion. And this was about to run into firm obstruction from Sister Yan’er. But she, too, would never give up.
