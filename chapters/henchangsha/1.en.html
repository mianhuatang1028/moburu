Chapter One: Xiao

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Xiao” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas, birds are mostly named for their shapes. This one hides by day and comes out at night, and whoever hears its cry takes it as an ill omen. As the saying goes: the night bird gives its first cry, and the long night has only begun.

Old Zheng’s name was Zheng Zhiyong, and he felt that for the whole first half of his life, Heaven had been playing a joke on him.

He was sitting at an outdoor table of a café. Not far off, the leaves of the birch trees rustled in the wind. He was savagely smoking one cigarette after another as he waited for his friend Lu You. He had already ordered a bottle of sake and a few cold dishes to go with it. The dishes were on the table; the tables around him were all empty, not another customer in sight. Old Zheng smoked one after another, burying each butt in the ashtray in front of him. The ashtray was filled with spent coffee grounds, and the butts stood upside down in it one by one, like stubborn seeds pushing up out of the soil. Old Zheng thought about it; he had a great deal to sigh over.

Old Zheng was from Xiangyang, Hubei, born in 1986. The year he was born, his grandfather, who had been a cadre in the state system, took over the contract to run a small state-owned factory in their little city that was on the verge of bankruptcy, and after the restructuring he went into business for himself. Year after year the results got better and better. By the time Zheng was in primary school, his grandfather’s company was netting several million yuan in profit a year, so Zheng Zhiyong’s father, mother and aunt each quit their state jobs and joined the company full time. As the eldest son of the eldest son, the whole family considered him a child born under a lucky star — the grandson’s birth had brought the grandfather good fortune — and he was doted on above all the others. Recalling a childhood of fine clothes and fine food, of having whatever he asked for, and setting it against a middle age now sunk in confusion, Old Zheng tipped his head back and looked at the sky; in the clear, crisp sky, clouds were drifting slowly past. Old Zheng lowered his head and let out a helpless sigh.

Lu You had picked this café, called “Xiao” — the Owl — for their meeting. It was in a shopping park on Beijing’s central axis. Lu You’s child took fencing lessons there from a retired former national-team athlete, so he had searched “Nearby” on Dianping for a little bar and chosen this place. At the very moment Old Zheng sighed, Lu You walked up and, sitting down, said with a laugh, “Well, Engineer Zheng, contemplating life?”

Old Zheng shook his head slightly and smiled bitterly. “I was just thinking — I’m Jia Baoyu.”

Lu You said, “Jia Baoyu doesn’t quite fit, does it? Didn’t Jia Baoyu’s family go to ruin in the end, and he became a monk?”

Lu You was four years older than Zheng Zhiyong, thin, tall and pale. If anything he looked five or six years younger than Zheng Zhiyong. He was from Shenyang, and his parents were also from the state system — now retired, they had been bureau-level officials in local government. After graduating he had worked for a few years at a research and design institute in Beijing, and for the past ten years he had been running, with a few university classmates, a defense-industry machining plant in Xi’an. The company had originally made only simple machined parts, but after several years of technical iteration it finally developed an industry-leading communications component dedicated to BeiDou satellites, for receiving and transmitting ground signals. After several rounds of pre-research, bidding and deal-chasing, it had ended up, together with two other friendly companies, being fielded across multiple navy and army units, and his company was the supplier with the largest share of procurement for that model. The defense industry is different from others: even if a technology or patent belongs exclusively to one supplier, all the technical secrets must be shared, so that each piece of equipment generally has at least a second and third supplier, to guarantee stable supply at delivery time and competitive pricing. So landing the largest supply share for a fielded product was in itself a very high bar.

Lu You’s company had passed fifty million yuan in revenue last year and was expected to keep growing steadily for several more years. A few trusted classmates kept watch over technology and production at the plant, so Lu You spent more and more time in Beijing. He had married a Beijing woman and they had boy-girl twins; a few years into the business he had bought a flat of more than two hundred square meters near Wanliu, and both little ones now attended Zhongguancun No. 3 Primary School. Apart from going to Xi’an once or twice a month, whenever he was in Beijing, when he wasn’t entertaining clients or out running, his fixed daily routine was taking the kids to school and picking them up, driving his wife to and from work, and cooking dinner for the three of them at home. They had a live-in housekeeper who could cook too, but he said he enjoyed cooking, so the housekeeper mostly cooked when he was away on business.

Lu You was one of the few friends in Beijing Zheng Zhiyong was willing to confide in. Both had graduated in mechatronics from Northwestern Polytechnical, though they hadn’t known each other as students. Many years after graduation, both working at research institutes in the state system, they had met through work. Zheng Zhiyong’s technical chops were formidable. When Lu You started his company and hit a technical sticking point in product development, he asked Old Zheng, who thought for a moment and said try a Fourier function, then tossed a few papers over WeChat and drew a few diagrams for Lu You’s technicians — and sure enough, the problem was solved very quickly. Lu You took Old Zheng to dinner and tried to give him ten thousand yuan as thanks; Old Zheng thought he hadn’t gone to any trouble at all and refused. Lu You decided this junior schoolmate was a decent guy, and from then on the two gradually became close friends, meeting now and then for drinks and talk when they had the time.

Old Zheng had called Lu You this time because, he said, he had a new job opportunity and wanted to hear what Lu You thought. But bringing up his childhood unprompted — that was a first for Old Zheng. After Lu You’s laughing line about Jia Baoyu and a slight pause, Old Zheng took a fierce drag. He looked up at Lu You. “Old Lu, my family went to ruin too. My grandfather got sick and died suddenly the year I was in ninth grade. If I don’t make some money now, I’ll have to become a monk as well.”

After his grandfather’s sudden death, everything went up in smoke. Because of one man’s passing, a machining enterprise famous throughout the little city suddenly went bankrupt. Suddenly one day the factory gates were sealed with official notices; suddenly one day PAY YOUR DEBTS was splashed in red paint across the factory’s outer wall. In the setting sun, a boy in a school uniform rode past on his bicycle, head tilted, staring in disbelief at all of it. That same still-boyish youth, through the half-height glass partition above the desk in his bedroom, which looked onto the living room, watched his parents — both now jobless at home — quarrel over not having enough money; his mother, after one sharp word too many, overturned the dinner table, and leftovers and plates crashed to the floor. The boy watched all of it, coolly twisted the blinds shut on the glass partition, and sat down at his desk. These images he could not shake still turned up in the dreams of the now middle-aged Zheng Zhiyong.

Lu You gave a regretful, awkward smile. It was early summer; the weather wasn’t hot yet, just right for sitting outside in the breeze, and the smell of Old Zheng’s tobacco still curled faintly in the air. The theme of today’s meeting was supposed to be whether Zheng Zhiyong needed to change jobs. The company in question was a small-satellite firm called Hanhai Chenxing, whose revenue had already reached two hundred million. It developed supporting satellite products for state institutes and handled R&D, production and operations for small and medium satellites. Its core team had all come out of the state system; business was advancing rapidly and revenue booming, exactly the moment they needed people. Because Old Zheng knew Lu You knew something about this company in his own field, he had arranged on WeChat to meet and talk over whether he should join it. Unexpectedly, the meeting had opened with Old Zheng’s childhood. Lu You said, “I’ve known you all this time, and this is the first I’ve heard of any of it.”

Zheng Zhiyong: “So I really do have a fixation on starting a business. My dilemma is — if I’m going to start a business again someday, what does the Hanhai opportunity mean for me?”

Lu You: “Engineer Zheng, isn’t the director of your institute pretty supportive of you starting a business? He even lets you take unpaid leave and keeps your post for you. Last time you said what revenue you did last year? Wasn’t it eight million? Gross margin over seventy percent, and your plant’s only got a handful of people — aren’t things just starting to take off? You’ve already started a business, so why look at other companies?”

Old Zheng said, “What eight million in revenue? No, no,” waving his hand, and went on: “The payment terms in defense are too long, the company’s cash flow is tight, and there’s a lot of pressure at home. I’ve had a few rounds with Hanhai this time. Do you know who the founder’s father is?” Old Zheng paused on purpose, building suspense.

“The vice president of the Fifth Academy.” Old Zheng took a drag and went on:

“The company’s second-largest shareholder, one of the partners, also came out of the Fifth Academy, and he’s still serving as a reviewer for research equipment every year. The way these people run a company is completely different from what I’d imagined. I need to get in there and learn for a few years. I’d be doing sales, which is also a perfect chance to build up client resources for when I start a business again.”

Lu You: “What position are they offering? What’s the pay?”

Old Zheng said, “Sales. The position’s not high, but I’d have access to core client resources. Forty thousand a month plus commission.”

Lu You asked, “Any options?”

Old Zheng said, “The chairman’s younger than me, and bold. He told me that after the three-month probation I can name my own number for options, and he’ll do his best to meet it.”

Lu You said, “Brother, from what I know this company’s good, and the pay’s not bad. The position’s a bit low, but the opportunity’s real. Actually you’ve already made up your mind whether to go, haven’t you? Did you really come to ask my opinion?” With that he raised his sake cup and said to Zheng Zhiyong with a smile, “Brother, here’s to a brilliant future!”

Zheng Zhiyong smiled and raised his cup, and the two clinked cleanly, like generals of old sealing a pact.

Lu You shelled an edamame and, popping it into his mouth, asked, “Your kid must be in primary school by now?”

Zheng Zhiyong nodded. “He’ll be in second grade this fall. Last year my wife fought with me no end over his schooling. She wants to trade our place in Fengtai for one in Xicheng. She thinks the Financial Street school district in Xicheng isn’t just a simple difference in school districts — it gives the kid a completely different environment and starting line. Think about it: who are the parents of those Financial Street kids, and who will those kids grow up to be? But you know me — I haven’t made much money these past years. Even if we sold our place and moved to Xicheng, the monthly mortgage would be too much.”

Lu You nodded and asked, “I seem to remember your wife’s from Northwestern Polytechnical too?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “That’s right. Jingyu was my first love, my university classmate — the campus beauty back then.” And with a hint of showing off he held up his phone. His lock screen was a photo of his wife, smiling radiantly, her arms around a wide-eyed, innocent little boy.

“Jingyu’s over a meter seventy, a tall girl. Loads of guys were after her back then. I’m only a meter seventy-five myself. No idea how she ever picked me.”

Lu You laughed. “Not bad, you rascal, landing such a pretty wife. So just work hard from here on, put in a few solid years, and leave the startup business for later! Starting a company really isn’t as easy as you imagine — don’t do it lightly. If you can hang on at Hanhai until it lists, then trading up to a place in Xicheng will be easy, won’t it?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “I’m still going to start a business. But I don’t have money. I need money first, sort out putting food on the table, and then start a business. If I want my wife to respect me from the bottom of her heart, I still have to make money.”

What Zheng Zhiyong didn’t tell Lu You was this. Ever since last year, when Zhang Jingyu started pushing to buy a school-district apartment for their son and Zheng Zhiyong said they couldn’t afford it, she — cool-natured to begin with — had sometimes let her coldness and disdain for him show plainly over the past year or two, which wounded him deeply, and he couldn’t summon any enthusiasm for married life either. Especially in the last couple of years: in the three years Zheng Zhiyong had been on unpaid leave from the system to take part in this little startup, he had basically brought no money home. Along the way the company had a quality problem with a machined part and lost a large sum, which more or less wiped out the profits they’d actually had in hand before last year, leaving only receivables that might never arrive. Meanwhile Zhang Jingyu’s own career kept getting better; she was an associate researcher at a research institute of the Fifth Academy, and in a few more years she’d have a shot at full researcher. For a middle-aged man, the ache in the heart often weighs more than the ache of the work. At that thought, Zheng Zhiyong proposed another toast himself, saying to Lu You, “Come on, Mr. Lu! I’ll drink first!” — and downed it in one.
