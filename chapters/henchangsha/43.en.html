Chapter Forty-Three: Heyu

“Heyu” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Eastern Mountains”), on Mount Shan there is a beast shaped like a pig with a human face, a yellow body and a red tail, called the heyu; when it appears, all the world is flooded. As the saying goes: a pig’s body and a human face, yellow body and red tail — it lies hidden in the wilds, and when it appears, the great flood comes.

Qi Jin’s factory was in Hefei, in the same old place.

Old Zheng’s history with Qi Jin went back a good few years, to when the two of them were both in the Northwestern Polytechnical incubator. Old Zheng had taken the lead on Qingzhi Guanglun, and Qi Jin was a technical backbone with a bachelor’s and master’s from USTC, who held the core of the servo motors and chassis control in his hands. Later, when Old Zheng went back to Beijing, he transferred his shares to Qi Jin at a set price, kept seven percent for himself, and Qi Jin took over as CEO. That parting dinner had been a decent one. The two of them clinked glasses at a stinky-mandarin-fish restaurant in Hefei; Old Zheng said, “You’re the real pillar of this company,” and Qi Jin said, “Brother Zheng, you’re better than me technically.” The words were polite, but Old Zheng knew very well that Qi Jin was more solid than he was in both technology and management — it was just that he hadn’t had the chance back then.

These past two years Qi Jin had made Qingzhi Guanglun steadier and steadier. Old Zheng had long since heard from mutual friends that the factory had expanded by more than a few lines and had even won a provincial “Little Giant” award for specialized, innovative firms. Last year, he’d heard, it had done more than thirty million in revenue at a gross margin of just over fifty percent. Qi Jin had found his way in the servo motor trade. On his own WeChat Moments he’d posted, “The little lotus has only just shown its pointed tip” — but truly, as the next line goes, “already a dragonfly stands upon it.”

Old Zheng called Qi Jin from Dongguan, and two days later came to see him. The day he arrived in Hefei, Qi Jin drove to the high-speed rail station himself to pick him up. After barely a few pleasantries, Qi Jin pulled him straight into the factory’s technical room. On the antistatic workbench a Dyson fan had already been taken apart into its components — even the motor had been dismantled into impeller, coils and other parts. On the whiteboard beside it were sketches, and several more versions of sketches were spread on the desk.

“Dyson’s patents are no big deal. There’s plenty of this being made in China now — we’ve done something similar ourselves before,” Qi Jin said to Old Zheng. “That ring-shaped narrow-slit outlet, the Coandă surface — Dyson’s locked down the core path. But the hard part now is making it cheaper, so we can’t go that way.”

Old Zheng said, “What I’m thinking is, don’t change the ring-shaped outlet structure, but change the airflow path. Don’t use a centrifugal fan in the base — use mixed flow. Mixed flow has lower static pressure than centrifugal, but higher flow and lower cost. The key thing is, the air coming out of a mixed-flow fan is axial, so it needs to turn once before it goes into the ring cavity. That turning section is where we can do something.”

Qi Jin: “We’ve done a preliminary assessment and considered that direction too. Put flow guides in the turning section, make them injection-molded parts — the mold cost isn’t high. But mixed flow has a different efficiency curve from centrifugal. If the back pressure of your ring slit isn’t matched well, the airflow will drop. Have you worked out the slit width?”

Old Zheng said, “Dyson’s is 1.3 millimeters. I’m not planning to go that narrow. The narrow slit is there to keep the Coandă effect stable, but a narrow slit needs high back pressure, and mixed flow can’t take it. I’d like to open it to 1.8 to 2.0 — give up a bit of air speed in exchange for flow. Ordinary people using it want a lot of air, not fast air.”

Qi Jin stubbed out his cigarette, stood up, went to the whiteboard and drew a few lines. “With a 1.8 slit, the Reynolds number changes. At 1.3 millimeters the flow in the slit is laminar, and when it comes out it attaches along the Coandă surface. Open it to 1.8, and if the surface curvature isn’t enough, the flow will separate. The air comes out, but it won’t carry — it’ll scatter.”

Old Zheng went over, took the pen and changed the arc on the curved surface he’d drawn. “Increase the curvature. When the slit gets wider, increase the curvature with it, so the flow stays attached before it separates. The price is that the ring part’s outer diameter gets bigger and the mold’s a size bigger. But it’s an injection-molded part — a size bigger doesn’t add much money.”

Qi Jin looked at the arc and did a calculation. “This curvature of yours — what does it correspond to in Coandă angle, roughly?”

“Twelve to fourteen degrees. I’ve tried it. Over fifteen degrees, the separation point moves forward and the air scatters.”

Qi Jin didn’t answer. He went back to the desk, picked up the motor and turned it over and over for a while. “And the motor? Your FOB is fourteen dollars. Take out the molds and exterior parts, and what’s left for us on the motor is under five dollars. Brushless motors at that price on the market either skimp on the iron core or use aluminum wire for the windings. Use aluminum wire and efficiency drops, temperature rise goes up, and there goes the service life.”

Old Zheng said, “Aluminum wire. But use an iron core with high permeability and low core loss. Aluminum wire has higher resistance, so you have to make up for it with flux density. A slightly better core material at the same slot fill factor gets the flux up, and you can pull back part of what you lose in copper losses.”

Qi Jin said, “Aluminum wire is three to five yuan a kilo cheaper than copper. One motor doesn’t use much, but at volume, what you save is real gold and silver. The iron’s a bit more expensive, but the iron’s a one-time thing, and the aluminum saves on every single unit. And aluminum wire is light, so the rotor’s dynamic balance is easier, and the noise comes down.”

The two of them looked at each other and smiled. This was the understanding between two partners: they’d long since arrived at the same place on the design.

Qi Jin said, “We can take this job. The equipment’s already here — swap a set of fixtures on the winding machine, open a new injection mold, adjust the rotor balancing machine. The upfront investment isn’t big; the bulk of it is molds and fixtures. How much can the initial R&D fee be? On my side, I’ll have motor samples out in forty-five days.”

Old Zheng said, “Five hundred thousand.”

Qi Jin said, “Done.”

Old Zheng and Wang Renhou had agreed long before that the outsourced factory would get an additional technical development fee as an advance. When the product went into mass production, with the unit price within the range agreed in the contract, it would be deducted from the purchase amount of the first batch of orders. If the product couldn’t be made to work within three months, or couldn’t be used, the fee would have to be refunded after a year.

Qi Jin asked again, “And what about you personally?”

Old Zheng said, “On this project, of your net profit, give me personally seven percent. You take care of all the tax handling for me, and pay it into my personal account.”

Qi Jin gave a knowing smile. The figure was exactly the share arrangement Old Zheng had had with him back then. Because of this project, the two of them had become partners again. They agreed to start work the very next day, and Old Zheng would stay on that week to work with the team on getting a product prototype done as fast as possible.

That evening he went back to the hotel Qi Jin had arranged for him. Old Zheng called Zhang Hailu.

“Hailu, I’ll have to stay out here in Guangzhou and Shenzhen a while longer. The supply-chain business isn’t done — I’m talking to several factories, and capacity and cost both have to be ground out. You keep an eye on things in Beijing, and if there’s any news, tell me right away.”

Zhang Hailu was silent on the other end for a moment, then said, “Mr. Zheng, you’ve been on the road so long — can your health take it? How about I come down and do the running for you for a few days?”

Old Zheng said, “No need. You keep an eye on the technical side. How’s the mood with Little Chen and the others?”

Zhang Hailu said, “Not bad. I told them that even though the volumes as secondary or tertiary supplier are small, at least we’re in the commissioning lineup. Hold on to the qualification first and fight for more later. Little Chen hasn’t mentioned going back to Xiangcheng again.”

Old Zheng said, “Good. Hang on a while longer. Once I’ve got results out here, I’ll come back.”

Zhang Hailu said, “Okay. Mr. Zheng, make sure you get some rest.”

When he hung up, Old Zheng leaned back in the chair at the desk and looked out the window at the fields slipping past. Zhang Hailu thought he was in Guangzhou and Shenzhen running down the supply chain, thought he was killing himself for the company. He didn’t explain. This trip to Hefei, this business with Qi Jin — he had no intention of letting anyone know. His phone screen flickered and WeChat lit up. It was from Xiaoya: “Brother Yong, I want to come to Xi’an to see you.”

Zheng Zhiyong called her. The two of them hadn’t seen each other since that last time in Xi’an, but Xiaoya was the clingy type, and these past few months she’d been chattering away to Old Zheng on WeChat every day. Sometimes when Xiaoya said she hadn’t eaten, Old Zheng would order her takeout or something. Her birthday had come along in the meantime, and Old Zheng had ordered her a cake and flowers too. With a tacit understanding between them, things had grown sweet as honey mixed with oil. They weren’t really a couple, and their conversations didn’t stray in that direction, but the emotional back-and-forth Xiaoya and Old Zheng gave each other every day was about the same as a couple’s.

Old Zheng explained that he wasn’t in Xi’an but in Hefei. Xiaoya said then she’d come to Hefei. Old Zheng said he’d be very busy for the next few days; if she really had to come, she should wait until this Saturday, so that at the weekend there’d be a bit of time and he could take her to the Yellow Mountains for a day.

Zeng Xiaoya arrived early, and Old Zheng picked her up at Hefei’s high-speed rail station. She was pulling a twenty-inch Xiaomi suitcase and wearing a light, thin jacket, and when she saw him she ran over. The two of them got into the BYD Old Zheng was driving — a company car Qi Jin had borrowed for him — and drove off together to the Yellow Mountains, where they spent the whole day. In the evening they came down the mountain and booked a hotel at the foot of it.

That evening the two of them had dinner near the hotel. Xiaoya ate very slowly and didn’t say much — probably worn out from climbing. Both of them had lost their excitement from up on the mountain, and Xiaoya urged Old Zheng to go back to the hotel and rest. At the hotel they checked in; their rooms were next door to each other. The moment Zeng Xiaoya had put her suitcase inside her own door, she made the excuse of wanting to see what Old Zheng’s room was like, and in the blink of an eye had followed Old Zheng into his room.

Old Zheng said, “You’ve seen it. Now hurry on back.”

Xiaoya said, “There’s something I still want to talk to you about.”

Old Zheng said, “Then say it quick, and once you’ve said it, go back to bed.”

Xiaoya said, “I still want to tell you I want to work for you. I really do like you as a person. I know you’re very focused on your career right now, and I don’t think we can be boyfriend and girlfriend, but I can work for you, and I can learn things, and I can earn money too.”

Old Zheng looked at her. Her eyes were shining, and it wasn’t a put-on shine. He said, “Xiaoya, I’ve told you many times on WeChat these past months: Xinghuan isn’t my company. I’m a hired hand. I don’t have money.”

Xiaoya said, “You won’t always be a hired hand. You’ve got ability — you’ll definitely do it for yourself. Put me in your company, let me learn the work first, and when you’re ready and you go out on your own, I’ll do it with you.”

Who would have thought that this little Chengdu girl, who seemed to have no guile, no scheming, nothing but simplicity, would with one sentence strike right into Zheng Zhiyong’s heart? He hadn’t told anyone that he’d already started his own business with Wang Renhou. He hadn’t told, and didn’t plan to tell, anyone at all. But this girl, with a single sentence, had pried his heart wide open.

Old Zheng said, “Do you want some tea? I’ll pour you a cup.”

Xiaoya said, “No need, mineral water’s fine.” Old Zheng handed her a bottle of Nongfu Spring. Xiaoya took it and drank. “I don’t mean right now, this minute. I just want you to know how determined I am. I’ve sorted things out at home too. I won’t pester you. Whenever you’re ready, I’ll come and work with you.”

Old Zheng looked at her. The light in the room was pale yellow and fell gently on her face. She wore no makeup, her lips were a little dry, but her eyes were soft.

Diao Kexin’s face, the faint dimple on Diao Kexin’s left cheek — and Zeng Xiaoya’s eyes, a pair of eyes with no guile, no calculation.

Old Zheng was sitting on the sofa by the window and Xiaoya on the edge of the bed, so the two of them were face to face. Old Zheng said, “Come sit over here.”

Xiaoya said, “What for?”

Old Zheng said, “I’m telling you to come sit over here.”

Xiaoya came over without any fuss and sat down on the sofa Old Zheng was sitting on, the distance between them only thirty centimeters. Grinning, she asked again: “Big brother! What for?”

Old Zheng said, “What you said — I’ll remember it. If the chance comes, I’ll take you with me when I start my own business. You know finance, don’t you? Later on you can manage the finances and admin for me.”

Xiaoya said, “Seriously?”

Old Zheng’s sudden hundred-and-eighty-degree turn really did take Xiaoya by surprise. She’d been going on and on about this for months — knock the tree with a pole whether or not there are any dates on it; even if Old Zheng didn’t agree, she’d have lost nothing. She never thought he’d suddenly agree. It was completely beyond anything she’d expected.

Oh, how come you just agreed? Xiaoya pressed her right hand to her chest to calm her excitement, and Old Zheng’s eyes went to the long, fair neck beneath her palm, the collarbone showing, the cleavage half hidden and half revealed.

Old Zheng said, “Before, I didn’t want to get close to you because you’re too much like my ex-wife. I hated her very much, so I didn’t want someone who looked so much like her developing into a romantic relationship with me again. But all this time, the deeper we’ve got to know each other, the more I’ve finally understood that apart from looking alike, you’re nothing like her at all.”

Xiaoya: “I said so ages ago — she’s her and I’m me, aren’t I!” And with that she reached out and took Zheng Zhiyong’s hand.

Zheng Zhiyong’s eyes grew tender. He gazed at her with feeling and held her hand back.

Xiaoya said, “Big brother, you told me that after you broke up, you never had another girlfriend. Is that true?”

Old Zheng said, “I told you — I haven’t even got through the survival stage. What right would I have to find another girlfriend?”

Xiaoya said, “So these two years, you’ve been on your own.”

Old Zheng nodded.

She moved off the sofa a little and crouched down at his feet, crouching between Old Zheng’s legs, her face tipped up, her hair sliding off her shoulders and hanging down over her arms. He reached out and tucked a strand of hair from her forehead behind her ear. Their hands were still holding each other’s. Xiaoya didn’t move; she just looked at him like that.

He bent his head and kissed her. The kiss wasn’t hard, and it wasn’t long. Xiaoya’s lips were warm.

Xiaoya kept her eyes closed for two seconds before she opened them. She said, “Big brother.”

Old Zheng said, “Mm.”

Xiaoya said, “Shall I not leave tonight?”

Old Zheng said, “No. You have to go.”

Xiaoya said, “Then why’d you kiss me?”

Old Zheng looked at her. She was crouched there, face tipped up, and what was in her eyes was very straight, without any going around. He reached out and pulled her up, sat her back on the edge of the sofa, leaned his body over toward her and went on kissing her. Xiaoya had no time to speak, and didn’t push him away either. Old Zheng wrapped his arms around her body and kissed her hard, then slid from her lips to her neck. Just as the two of them were lost in it, Old Zheng suddenly pushed Xiaoya away and said, “You’d better go, quick. I can’t hold myself back — I’m going to make a mistake.”

Xiaoya said, “What mistake? We’re both single. I want to.”

Old Zheng said, “Then you’ll have to choose one. Work for me, or be my woman?”

Xiaoya’s brows drew together. This man pushing forty seemed much more complicated than everyone else in her world. “I can’t figure it out,” Xiaoya answered honestly.

Old Zheng helped Xiaoya to her feet and gently pushed her out through his door. How far things would go with Xiaoya, he hadn’t figured out yet either. But that kiss was the most exhilarating, most wildly animal kiss he’d had in two years. It seemed neither Cong Shasha nor Xiao Jing had that kind of sexiness and desire. It was just that things were already very complicated now, and he didn’t know whether he could keep control of a life with yet another variable added to it.
