Jiuwei

Chapter Twenty-Eight: Jiuwei

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Jiuwei” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Southern Mountains”), on Mount Qingqiu there is a beast shaped like a fox with nine tails; its cry is like a baby’s, and whoever eats it cannot be bewitched. As the saying goes: the more tails, the more paths; the more paths, the harder to tell which is which.

The supplementary materials for the Sixth Academy’s notice period had been handed in and the matter still wasn’t settled, and Zheng Zhiyong was already on his third round trip between Xi’an and Beijing.

Zhang Hailu had pulled out every original record — the third-party testing agency’s qualification documents, the record of the change of legal representative, the company roster, employees’ résumés, the defense-qualification reports, project contracts and so on — and put them together into a thick explanatory volume, which he handed in to the project office. Supervisor Zhou took the materials and said it would go through procedure; they should wait. On the hidden front, Cong Shasha had picked up a bit of news too: Deputy Director Li’s exact words were “it’s being pushed down from above.” When Zheng Zhiyong heard that, the string inside him stayed pulled tight.

Right at this critical juncture, the Sixth Academy’s project office at last agreed to a dinner.

It was the same old four-star hotel. The same twelfth floor, the same room. Cong Shasha was on the tenth floor, same as last time.

The project office didn’t want to switch companies now. If Old Zheng’s side couldn’t do it, Yuanwang would struggle too, and now that the bidding process was finished, nobody wanted to overturn everything. At the dinner table the other side accepted the gifts, and in the end told him the leader’s name — his surname was Wu — and said to Old Zheng earnestly, “On this one you still need to let the leader see what you can do. We’re definitely behind you.”

They saw the guests off. At ten that night he went down to the hotel bar alone and ordered a whisky. He was halfway through it when Cong Shasha walked in and said, “Mr. Zheng, drinking on your own and not calling me?” She sat down, ordered herself a tequila shot, poured it, and kept him company drinking.

The two of them started on the project office’s attitude, went on to the relationships inside the Sixth Academy, and in the end both reached the same conclusion: this Director Wu was the big backer Diao Kexin had once told Old Zheng about. He’d already put away half a bottle of baijiu at dinner, and with a mixed foreign liquor on top of it at the bar, Zheng Zhiyong was clearly drunk and starting to ramble, talking more and more. He talked about last winter in that office of his in Xi’an, the dead fish floating on the surface of the tank, sitting alone across from Lu You drinking tea, feeling his life was over. He talked about Diao Kexin, about the day he walked out of his marriage with nothing, seven or eight cardboard boxes holding twenty years of his life. Cong Shasha listened, putting in a word now and then, never too light and never too heavy, just right.

After midnight Zheng Zhiyong said they should go up. The two of them got into the elevator, leaning on each other, close. Twelfth floor; the corridor was very quiet. Cong Shasha helped him to his door, felt in his pocket for the key card and swiped it open. The main light in the room wasn’t on; a little light from the corridor leaked in through the gap in the curtains.

Zheng Zhiyong sat down on the edge of the bed and fell back flat. Cong Shasha stood by the bed and looked at him, looked at him a long time.

The room was very quiet. The lamp wasn’t on; there was only that thin strip of light from the curtain gap lying across the covers. Cong Shasha took off his shoes, lifted his legs onto the bed, went into the bathroom and wrung out a hot towel, and came back to wipe his face. As the towel passed over his forehead, his eyes opened a slit; he gave her a muddled look and mumbled something.

Cong Shasha put the towel on the nightstand and sat down on the edge of the bed.

She knew exactly what she was doing. She’d followed Diao Kexin for seven years; she understood the woman, and she knew that Diao Kexin’s secret weapons all worked in settings just like this — this after-drinks, late-night, two-people-alone-in-a-room kind of ambiguity. She’d watched how Diao Kexin held her words back at dinner and let her eyes go soft, how in the car taking someone home she’d lean back just a little and let the man lean in by himself. What came after, she’d never seen, but today a lot of things suddenly became clear. She understood why Diao Kexin’s income was several times hers, when she was younger and prettier than her and had a better figure. That whole set of feminine weapons — she had them too. She’d just never met anyone worth using them on.

Now that someone was lying right in front of her.

She bent down over him.

Zheng Zhiyong’s consciousness came and went.

He remembered soft hair brushing his chin. He remembered someone undoing the buttons of his shirt, one, then another, fingertips warm, moving very slowly — so slowly it was as if she were leaving him time to push her away. He remembered a tongue brushing across his belly and reaching his thigh. He remembered reaching out to push her away, pushing very feebly, his hand resting on her wrist, saying “Shasha, don’t” in a voice so hoarse he didn’t recognize it himself. He remembered that Cong Shasha didn’t stop, and didn’t speak either, only slowed down even more and looked down at him, with no pressing in her eyes and no retreat — just looking at him like that, as if waiting for the breath he was holding to let go by itself.

He remembered that after that he stopped pushing.

He remembered that strip of light from the curtain gap wavering on the wall, and wavering again. He remembered the corner of the covers sliding onto the floor, and no one picking it up. He remembered Cong Shasha’s hair coming loose and sweeping across his collarbone, ticklish. He remembered opening his eyes for an instant and seeing her face in profile — the light was very dim and he couldn’t make out her expression, only that her eyes were closed. He remembered turning his face aside onto the pillow, which had a faint smell of hotel laundry detergent, clean and unfamiliar.

He remembered a very soft sound escaping his throat, half a sigh and half surrender.

He remembered that at the end he fell asleep, very deeply, so deeply he dreamed of nothing at all.

The next morning Zheng Zhiyong was woken by the glare through the gap in the curtains.

He opened his eyes. The first thing he saw was the hollow in the other pillow where someone had slept; then the ceiling; then the beige lamp on the nightstand; and then Cong Shasha. She was already sitting in the chair by the window, wearing one of Zheng Zhiyong’s shirts, a big light-gray one, looking down at her phone. Hearing movement on the bed, she looked up and smiled.

“Mr. Zheng, you’re awake?”

Zheng Zhiyong propped himself up. His head was splitting and his throat was dry. He looked down at himself: his shirt had been taken off and hung over the back of a chair, and the covers were over him. His head buzzed.

“Shasha—”

“You had too much to drink last night,” Cong Shasha said, putting down her phone. She got up, went to the nightstand, poured him a glass of water and handed it to him. “I helped you back here.”

When she said this her tone was perfectly flat, as if she were talking about something entirely ordinary. There was no embarrassment on her face, no evasion, no hint at all of “something happened.” She just stood there, all smiles, handing him the water.

Zheng Zhiyong took the water and drank a couple of mouthfuls, and his throat felt a little better. He looked at Cong Shasha and wanted to say something, but when it got to his lips he didn’t know how to begin.

Cong Shasha acted as if she hadn’t noticed the state he was in. She turned and drew the curtains halfway open, and said, “There’s a short meeting with the project office this morning, set for ten. You lie down a while longer — I’ll come up and get you at half past nine. I’ve had the restaurant save you breakfast; they’ll bring it up soon.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Shasha.”

Cong Shasha turned her head.

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Last night—”

Cong Shasha cut him off, still smiling the same smile. “Mr. Zheng, last night you had too much to drink. I brought you back and then went to my own room. If your head hurts, I’ve got some hangover pills — I’ll bring them up in a bit.”

She said it lightly, cleanly and crisply, without the slightest unnecessary pause.

Zheng Zhiyong looked at her for a few seconds and nodded. “…All right. Thank you.”

Cong Shasha said, “Then you take a shower first. I’ll go change and come up at half past nine.” With that she took off Old Zheng’s shirt as easily as anything, put on the clothes she’d worn the day before, opened the door and went out, her footsteps fading down the corridor.

Zheng Zhiyong sat on the bed holding the glass of water, his head in a tangle. He remembered last night — in bits and pieces, but everything he ought to remember, he remembered. He knew what had happened, and he knew Cong Shasha was covering it up for both of them. She covered it so naturally, without leaving a trace, and that only made him feel worse.

He got out of bed and took a shower. Hot water poured over him; he stood under the showerhead with his eyes closed, turning it over and over in his head. He remembered saying “Shasha, don’t” last night, remembered that hand pushing so feebly. He knew he hadn’t pushed firmly enough. He knew there was a place in his heart that, in that very, very quiet room, had let go for a moment.

He hated himself.

He’d only just crawled out of Diao Kexin’s pit and was still covered in mud. His debts weren’t paid off, he still hadn’t found his son’s school, the Sixth Academy project was still stuck on the supplementary verification — what right did he have to let go like that?

He came out of the shower, got dressed, and sat on the edge of the bed smoking. He was halfway through the cigarette when someone knocked. He pinched it out and went to open the door. Cong Shasha was standing there with a box of hangover pills and a glass of warm water. She’d already changed into a business outfit, her hair freshly pinned up, every trace of that smile wiped clean from her face, looking every bit someone heading out to get things done.

“Mr. Zheng, your pills. It’s nine-forty — we should go down.”

Zheng Zhiyong took the pills and said, “Shasha, about last night—”

Cong Shasha looked at him, waiting for him to finish.

Zheng Zhiyong said, “It was my fault. I’d had too much, I shouldn’t have—”

Cong Shasha smiled and cut him off. “Mr. Zheng, nothing happened last night. You had too much to drink, I took you back to your room, that’s all. Don’t overthink it, and don’t take it to heart. We’ve got real work today — that meeting with the project office is pretty important.”

When she said this, her eyes were on Zheng Zhiyong, very candid, very calm — so calm that Zheng Zhiyong felt he hardly knew her.

Zheng Zhiyong opened his mouth, and in the end said nothing, only nodded.

The meeting that day went fairly smoothly. In the meeting Cong Shasha spoke when she should and took notes when she should, passed over materials and picked up the thread of the conversation, no different from usual. Zheng Zhiyong sat beside her and glanced at her now and then. She didn’t avoid it either — met his eyes when she should and smiled when she should, without a single crack showing.

At lunch the two of them sat in a noodle shop near the Sixth Academy, a bowl of beef noodles each. Zheng Zhiyong ate a few mouthfuls in silence, then suddenly said, “Shasha, why did you…”

He didn’t finish.

Cong Shasha looked up at him, swallowed her mouthful of noodles and said, “Mr. Zheng, what do you want to ask?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Last night. Why did you.”

Cong Shasha put down her chopsticks, thought for a moment, and said, “Mr. Zheng, I followed Ms. Diao for seven years. In those seven years I watched how she went from junior salesperson to sales director, how she went from a divorced woman to the chairman’s wife. I watched her do everything she did, say everything she said, how she judged the line, how she advanced and retreated in front of men. I learned a lot.”

She paused, then went on, “I’m not Ms. Diao. I don’t have her luck, and I don’t have her ruthlessness. But I want to move up too. I’m twenty-eight this year. I worked at Yuanwang for seven years, my salary barely went up and my position never moved. I quit and came out with you because I think you’re someone who can make things happen. I want to follow someone who can make things happen, and move up a bit.”

Zheng Zhiyong said nothing.

Cong Shasha said, “About last night — don’t feel you owe me anything. I’m not the kind of person who’d use something like that to ask you for anything. If you feel it isn’t right, we’ll just say it never happened. If you feel—” She stopped and smiled. “If you feel there might be another chance down the line, I won’t run from it either. But for now, the Sixth Academy project matters most. Everything else goes on the back burner.”

She said it very directly, very plainly, without any beating around the bush.

As Zheng Zhiyong listened, the regret in him slowly turned into something he couldn’t name. He thought of Diao Kexin. Diao Kexin would never have said things this directly. Diao Kexin’s words were always soft, always roundabout, always leaving you to guess and wonder and take the bait yourself. Cong Shasha was different. Cong Shasha laid her cards on the table and let you choose.

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Shasha, the state I’m in right now, it’s not a good time to talk about this.”

Cong Shasha said, “I know. Right now you’ve still got someone else in your heart, and you’re still holding your breath to bring Yuanwang down. I know all that. I’m not asking you to talk about anything now. I’m just telling you plainly what I’m thinking. As long as you know, that’s enough.”

She lowered her head and went on eating her noodles.

Zheng Zhiyong looked at her for a good while, then lowered his head too and went on eating his.

When the noodles were finished, the two of them came out of the shop, and the March wind in Beijing blew in their faces. Cong Shasha walked half a step from his side, same as always, not too close and not too far.

Zheng Zhiyong suddenly said, “Shasha, this Sixth Academy project — we’re going to win it.”

Cong Shasha said, “I know.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Once we’ve won, Xinghuan’s going to expand — hire people, build out sales. When that happens, I’ll hand sales over to you.”

Cong Shasha tilted her head, glanced at him and smiled. “Mr. Zheng, I’ll hold you to that.”

Zheng Zhiyong nodded and said nothing more.

The two of them walked toward the Sixth Academy. The wind lifted Cong Shasha’s hair a little, and she raised a hand to smooth it. Zheng Zhiyong looked at the drab gray office building ahead, and the messy jumble in his heart slowly settled.

He knew that what happened last night wouldn’t just pass like that. It was like a seed buried between the two of them; when it would sprout, and what it would grow into, he couldn’t see clearly yet.

He only thought: what mattered most right now was the Sixth Academy order.

Only once that order was won would he have the right to think about anything else.

And Cong Shasha, walking beside him at that moment, wore that just-right smile on her face, not too much and not too little. What she was thinking about was something else. She was thinking about last night — the things Zheng Zhiyong had said on the hotel bed, half drunk and half awake, mumbled so she couldn’t quite make them out. But one name she had heard clearly.

That name, she knew all too well.

At the time her hands hadn’t stopped, but in her heart she’d noted it down very clearly.

She thought: so that’s how it is.

She thought: what Ms. Diao can do, I’m not necessarily unable to do.

She thought: follow Mr. Zheng, get this project done first, get Xinghuan off the ground. What comes after can wait until after.

The wind blew between the two of them, making Beijing in March a little chilly. Cong Shasha pulled the collar of her cardigan closer and followed Zheng Zhiyong through the gate of the Sixth Academy.
