Bifang

Chapter Twenty-Six: Bifang

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Bifang” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Western Mountains, Book Three”), on Mount Zhang’e there is a bird shaped like a crane, with one foot, red markings on a blue-green body and a white beak; it cries out its own name, and when it appears, strange fires break out in its town. As the saying goes: the one-footed bird lands without a sound.

Zhu Yiwen’s answer came on the evening of the third day.

No phone call, no WeChat voice message — just an email, its subject line a clean four characters: Intent to Cooperate. The body wasn’t long either. The gist was that after internal evaluation, Gaoda was willing to take part as subcontractor in Xinghuan Technology’s overall solution for the Sixth Academy project, with the specific terms to be worked out between the two sides’ technical and commercial teams. It was signed with Zhu Yiwen’s name, without even the company seal — a gentleman’s-agreement kind of statement, to begin with.

Zheng Zhiyong read the email over and over, seven or eight times, forwarded it to Wu You and Zhang Hailu, then stood up and walked two laps of his office, and finally went to the window and lit a cigarette.

He’d assumed a phone call would come, that Zhu Yiwen would say a few of the usual polite things over the phone — “Mr. Zheng, we were fated to meet,” say, or “I’m doing it for Teacher Song’s sake.” But there was nothing. One email, clean and crisp, without a single word of explanation more than needed. The more it was like that, the more clearly Zheng Zhiyong understood: Zhu Yiwen didn’t need to explain this decision to him — still less to anyone else.

Zhang Hailu came over from the office next door, glanced at the email, and said just one thing: “We’re in.”

Zheng Zhiyong asked, “Why do you think he picked us?”

Zhang Hailu thought for a moment and said, “From the business side, our solution really is a cut above Yuanwang’s. He’s got no reason to bet on a company whose technology is already a hollow shell. From the personal side—” Zhang Hailu paused. “Zhu Yiwen — my classmate Song Li’s said he’s a man who’s loyal to old ties. And what a man like that hates most is someone trampling on old feelings.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “At bottom it’s still that I offered him a price he couldn’t possibly refuse. The split I gave him is better than Yuanwang’s by a mile — sky-high better.”

Zhang Hailu nodded along. “True, true.”

For all he said, Zheng Zhiyong wasn’t completely sure either; he kept feeling there might be something more behind it. He remembered the jolt on Zhu Yiwen’s face at that dinner, almost impossible to hide, when he heard that Wang Yan had divorced; remembered the faint something, there and not there, when Zhu Yiwen said, “Wang Yan was my classmate at university.”

He hadn’t thought about it closely at the time. Now, standing at the window in the gray of a Xi’an winter, he suddenly put a few things together. When Zhu Yiwen talked about Wang Yan, there’d been something in his voice, very light and very faint, that he couldn’t quite keep down. When a man speaks of a woman from his university class in that tone, it’s more than the word “classmate” can hold.

Zheng Zhiyong stubbed out his cigarette in the drink can on the windowsill and didn’t say it out loud to Zhang Hailu.

He only thought: Gao Fei has probably let down a great many people.

Zhu Yiwen’s side moved even faster than Zheng Zhiyong had expected. On the third day, Gaoda’s technical team was added to Xinghuan’s group chat, and the engineers on both sides started working out the interface parameters for the remote-sensing module. On the fifth day Zhu Yiwen called himself. His tone was strictly business: the subcontracting share would go by the forty percent Mr. Zheng had proposed last time, but the cost-sharing and payment schedule would have to be worked out again; they couldn’t go by what Mr. Zheng had said at the dinner table that day.

On the phone, Zheng Zhiyong agreed at once. “Mr. Zhu, have your side draft the terms and we’ll look them over.”

When he hung up, he gave a wry smile to himself. What Lu You had said that night in the hotel lobby in Xi’an was still in his ears, every word of it. Forty percent to the subcontractor, ninety percent of the cost, a 60 percent gross margin — when you did the math, his end wouldn’t have much money left. But he had to take this deal, even at no profit he had to take it, because what this deal was fighting for wasn’t just an order: it was Yuanwang’s foundations, it was Gao Fei’s face.

He called Wu You and reported Zhu Yiwen’s conditions to him exactly as they were, and finished by saying, “Mr. Wu, I’ve done the math. The margin on this one is thin — we might even just break even — but its strategic value outweighs its financial value. I recommend we take it.”

Wu You was silent for a few seconds on his end, then said, “Old Zheng, coming to me after you’ve worked out the numbers yourself — that’s the right way. But do the up-front funding budget in finer detail before you come to me with it.”

With just that one sentence, the string that had been pulled tight in Zheng Zhiyong’s heart all this time loosened a little.

The Sixth Academy’s bid opening was set for late March.

The weather in Beijing those few days was unusually good — strong wind, blue sky, sunshine on your skin with the warmth of early spring. Xinghuan sent five people: Zheng Zhiyong, Zhang Hailu, Cong Shasha, and two technical consultants Zhang Hailu had brought in at short notice from among his Xiangnan University classmates to add some weight. Yuanwang, it was said, had also sent five: Gao Fei leading the team in person, Diao Kexin, Zhao Bin, and two old hands from sales.

The bids were opened in the third-floor meeting room of the Sixth Academy’s main building. The corridor floor was laid with old-fashioned beige vinyl, a row of honor photos from years past hung on the wall, and the air had that dry smell peculiar to government buildings, baked out by the radiators. Zheng Zhiyong’s group got there early and sat for ten minutes or so on the long bench in the anteroom. Cong Shasha kept her head down, leafing through the bid; Zhang Hailu sat with his eyes closed, resting; and Zheng Zhiyong went over the first three minutes of his presentation in his head again and again.

The presentation order was drawn by lot. Xinghuan drew second, Yuanwang third.

The first to present was a small company, very possibly brought in by Yuanwang’s connections to make up the numbers as a dummy bidder. It talked for twenty-odd minutes and wrapped up in a hurry. Then it was Xinghuan’s turn. Zhang Hailu did the main technical presentation, and Zheng Zhiyong presented the overall solution and the delivery guarantees. Zhang Hailu was very steady. When he got to the section on the upfront simulation module, several of the expert evaluators below clearly raised their heads, and one even leaned forward and jotted a few lines on paper with his pen. When Zheng Zhiyong presented the overall solution, he leaned hard on “productization,” “iterability,” “modular supply chain” and “consistent quality in rapid mass production” — precisely what Yuanwang had lacked most these past two years.

As he spoke, his eyes swept the room, and when they reached the far end of the evaluators’ table, they stopped for a moment.

The door was open. People were moving in the corridor.

Yuanwang’s people had arrived.

Zheng Zhiyong didn’t look up. He drew his gaze back and kept talking. But he knew that in that instant, out in the corridor beyond the door, someone’s eyes had fallen on him — fallen very briefly, and been pulled back very quickly.

When Xinghuan’s presentation ended, the evaluators asked a few questions, and Zhang Hailu answered them one by one. As Zheng Zhiyong gathered his materials and went out, the five from Yuanwang were coming down the corridor from the far end, and the two groups met face to face in the not-very-wide passage by the meeting-room door.

Gao Fei walked in front. He’d lost a bit of weight, his hair combed without a strand out of place, and the eyes behind the gold-rimmed glasses were the same as ever: when he looked at someone, he swept over them first, then fixed on them, as if weighing how heavy something was. When he saw Zheng Zhiyong, his step slowed by half a beat, and then he put out his hand, with that very proper business smile on his face. “Mr. Zheng, long time no see.”

Zheng Zhiyong put out his hand too, and shook it once. Gao Fei’s hand was very dry and cool, his grip firm and measured. “Chairman Gao,” Zheng Zhiyong said.

The two hands held for less than two seconds before letting go.

Behind Gao Fei was Zhao Bin. Zhao Bin nodded to Zheng Zhiyong, his look a little complicated, and said nothing. Further back were Yuanwang’s two salespeople, whom Zheng Zhiyong didn’t know.

Last came Diao Kexin.

She was at the very back of the group, in a dark gray business suit, her hair pinned up, light makeup, her lipstick a very restrained dusty rose. She’d slimmed back down a good deal since he’d seen her outside the Sixth Academy four months before, but there was still something about her, hard to put into words, a heavy kind of stillness. She held a stack of bid documents in her arms, her eyes on the way ahead, as if she hadn’t seen Zheng Zhiyong at all.

The two groups turned sideways to pass. The passage was too narrow, and Zheng Zhiyong’s arm brushed Diao Kexin’s. Just once. The cuff of her suit was wool, and as it brushed past there was the faintest little rasp of friction.

Zheng Zhiyong didn’t look back.

He heard Yuanwang’s people go into the meeting room behind him, heard Gao Fei say something in a low voice, heard chairs scraping. Then Cong Shasha, beside him, said softly, “Mr. Zheng, let’s go.”

He said “Mm” and walked on.

When he came out of the main building’s doors, the March wind rushed at his face and blew the hair over his forehead into a mess for a moment. He stood on the steps, took out his cigarette pack, shook one out, lit it and took a deep drag.

Cong Shasha followed him out and stood beside him, about to speak and then holding back.

Zheng Zhiyong said, “If you want to say something, say it.”

Cong Shasha said, “I saw just now. Ms. Diao.”

Zheng Zhiyong didn’t answer.

Cong Shasha said, “She looked at you. Just once, in the passage. When she looked at you, that look — did your heart skip?” She looked into Old Zheng’s eyes and said it teasingly.

Zheng Zhiyong held the cigarette between his fingers, looking at the few trees in the courtyard just turning green again, and said, “Shasha, will you quit messing with me? I turned that page a long time ago. Right now every fucking ounce of me is on one thing only: we have to win.”

Cong Shasha said, “I know.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Not just winning for Xinghuan. I want Yuanwang, in this business, from today on, to look up and see that the rival in front of them is us.”

When he said this his voice was very flat, with no teeth-gritting about it at all. But the flatter it was, the more Cong Shasha, listening, felt a subtle change of feeling — something like admiration for this man, with a touch of worship in it. She’d followed Diao Kexin for seven years, and followed Zheng Zhiyong these past few months, and suddenly she felt a little jealous of Diao Kexin. For a second she was stunned that jealousy had actually sprung up in her, and with a jolt she quickly pulled herself back to work and covered it up: “Mr. Zheng, I’ll go over the bid once more. I’m worried something’s been missed.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Go ahead.”

Cong Shasha went back into the building. Zheng Zhiyong stood alone on the steps, finished his cigarette and stubbed it out in the ashtray on top of the trash can by the door.

He took out his phone and scrolled through it. No new messages.

He didn’t actually know what message he was waiting for.

Four months ago, at the door of this same building, Diao Kexin, filled out all over, bare-faced, had said to him, “You’ve got to keep working hard on your career, okay? You’ve got to make it bigger than Yuanwang.” He remembered that sentence to this day, every word of it. Standing in front of her then, he’d had only one thought churning over and over in his head: is she pregnant, and is the child mine?

He’d never told that thought to anyone. Not Lu You, not Wu You, not Wu Peng. He’d pressed it down at the very bottom of his heart, like weighing it down with a stone. But a stone can’t hold a thing down. It just sits down there and digs in.

Just now in the passage, in that one moment as they brushed past each other, he’d caught the very faint, familiar scent of her hand cream. Just that once, and the stone in his heart gave a jolt.

The gray suit she wore today was cinched tight at the waist. She didn’t look pregnant. Ah — she’d never been pregnant at all.

Zheng Zhiyong stood on the steps and turned the thought over and over, and suddenly felt a bit ridiculous. Four months ago he’d stood in the same place, staring at her filled-out figure, and in his heart had earnestly claimed a child as his own. For several nights after that he couldn’t sleep, thinking again and again: if the child really is mine, what do I do? He’d even thought that if she really was carrying his child, he would risk everything to get the child back.

Thinking about it now — ah, I, Zheng Zhiyong, am still a total fucking idiot.

How would someone like her ever carry his child? Someone like her worked out every step in advance. The only child she could be carrying was Gao Fei’s, could only be the last, and most important, piece on that billion-yuan board of hers. She’d filled out — maybe she just hadn’t watched what she ate, maybe she’d just been living comfortably for a while, maybe it was for no reason at all. That foolish, wishful longing of his, Zheng Zhiyong’s — to her it probably didn’t even count as a joke.

The ridiculous part was that, even now, he still hadn’t managed to think this thing all the way through. He hated her. Hated her for using him as a stepping stone, hated her for stripping him clean and then kicking him away, hated how crisply she’d said that “Yes” on the phone, without a trace of hesitation. But at the very deepest point of his hatred he’d think of her crouching at his feet to light his cigarette; of her turning back on the Zhangbei grassland to say to him, “If one day you’re not by my side, when I see the sunset, I’ll miss you terribly”; of the sound of her voice in the car as she straddled his lap, buried her face in the crook of his neck and said, “I just can’t take it anymore.”

The hating and the missing churned together, churned into something he couldn’t name. Sometimes he’d wake in the middle of the night and suddenly think: is she doing all right now? Is Gao Fei good to her? That child — if there really was that child — is it a boy or a girl, and who does it look like? And then he’d curse himself: Zheng Zhiyong, for fuck’s sake, wake up.

He stubbed out the cigarette and straightened up. Far off in the Sixth Academy’s grounds, a black car was driving in through the gate and pulled up slowly in front of the building. Several people got out — other bidders, by their clothes. Zheng Zhiyong took one look and turned to go back into the building.

By the time he got back to the meeting-room door, Yuanwang’s presentation had already begun. The door was ajar, and Gao Fei’s voice came from inside — not loud, very steady, with that methodical rhythm, laying everything out point by point, peculiar to someone who’d been a professor for more than a decade. Zheng Zhiyong stood at the door for an instant without pushing it open, then turned and went down the stairs beside it.

He thought: Gao Fei, however well you speak, you’re not taking this one.

The results would take fifteen days; every company had submitted hundreds or thousands of pages.

For those two weeks Zheng Zhiyong hardly left the office. Zhang Hailu and Cong Shasha went back to their own places to wait for news, leaving only him and a few technicians in the office. By day he dealt with odds and ends of business; at night he sat alone in the dormitory room on the fourth floor, staring blankly at the man-made water features outside the floor-to-ceiling window.

On the third evening Wu Peng knocked on his door and came in carrying two bowls of noodles. “Mr. Zheng, I saw your light was still on. The canteen auntie saved some noodles, so I heated them up for you.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Have you eaten?”

Wu Peng said, “I’ll eat with you.”

The two of them sat at the little coffee table in front of the sofa and ate noodles. Wu Peng didn’t talk much, but when there was something to say he never beat around the bush. Halfway through the bowl he suddenly said, “Mr. Zheng, today I heard from some of Mr. Wu’s people that Yuanwang’s funding bet comes due this year.”

Zheng Zhiyong looked up. “Who’d you hear that from?”

Wu Peng said, “Mr. Wu’s quant team in Shanghai deals with a few family offices. Some of the investors in that 200-million capital increase Yuanwang took back then have a bit of a connection to one of those family offices. The news isn’t necessarily accurate, but it’s pretty close.”

Zheng Zhiyong put down his chopsticks. “How much is the buyback debt?”

Wu Peng said, “At 12 percent a year, rolled over seven or eight years, two hundred million turns into somewhere around four hundred million.”

Zheng Zhiyong said nothing. He picked up his bowl, drank a couple of mouthfuls of the remaining broth, put the bowl down and said, “Wu Peng, thank Mr. Wu’s people for me on this. But don’t let this news get out for now.”

Wu Peng said, “I understand.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “On the surface, Yuanwang is still the same old Yuanwang now — the park’s there, the tower’s there, the five hundred-odd people are there. But if the books really have come to this, it can’t hold out much longer. Gao Fei — he’s headstrong, cares about face, and the worse things get, the harder he’ll try to hold it all up. Him leading the team himself to bid at the Sixth Academy this time shows he can’t afford to lose this one.”

Wu Peng said, “Then we’ve got to win all the more.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Right. All the more.”

Two weeks later, in the afternoon, the Sixth Academy called Xinghuan’s front desk.

Cong Shasha took the call. A couple of sentences in, she covered the mouthpiece and shouted into the office, “Mr. Zheng! The Sixth Academy!”

Zheng Zhiyong came out of his office and took the phone. On the other end was the head of a project office at the Sixth Academy. His tone was very polite; he said a few of the usual things first, then said, “Mr. Zheng, the evaluation results are out. We’ve finished our internal procedures, and I’m formally notifying you that Xinghuan Technology has the highest overall score and is entering the public notice period.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Thank you, thank you, sir. Thank you to the Sixth Academy.”

The other end said a few more things — the notice period was two weeks, and once it was over they’d issue the award notice, so they should get ready for the contract work to follow.

When he hung up, Zheng Zhiyong stood there at the front desk, still holding the receiver, for two or three seconds before he put it back.

Cong Shasha watched him from beside him, eyes shining, and asked in a small voice, “We won?”

Zheng Zhiyong nodded.

Cong Shasha let out a “Wow!” and nearly jumped, then quickly covered her mouth. A few technicians in the office heard the commotion and crowded around, all asking at once. Zhang Hailu came down from the second floor, heard the result and, for once, smiled. “All right. Then all those sleepless nights these two weeks weren’t for nothing.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Tonight’s on me. Extra dishes at the canteen, and the drinks are on me.”

Everyone cheered.

After the excitement died down a while later, everyone went their separate ways. Zheng Zhiyong went back to his own office, shut the door, sat down and took out his phone. In WeChat he scrolled to “Lu You” and typed a line: “Old Lu, the Sixth Academy project — we won. Thanks for what you said that night.” Sent.

Then he scrolled to “Wu You” and typed a line: “Mr. Wu, we won the Sixth Academy bid. Two-week notice period. First order, in the bag.” Sent.

Wu You replied very quickly, just a few words: “Good. Keep going.”

Lu You replied more slowly, with a long paragraph — congratulations, mainly, and also that winning the first order was only the beginning; the more things went your way, the more you had to keep steady and not let it go to your head.

Zheng Zhiyong answered each of them.

Then he put the phone on the desk, leaned back in his chair and looked up at the ceiling.

Outside the window was Xi’an in March. The sky wasn’t fully dark yet, and a trace of pale orange still hung on the outlines of the distant buildings. He suddenly remembered the night last winter when he first walked into this building — Wu You taking him up to the fourth floor to see the dormitory room, pushing open the hidden door behind the bookshelf, and him lying stiff as a board on that two-meter bed, feeling none of it was real.

Back then he had nothing at all. He was a hundred thousand in debt, kicked by a woman from the clouds down into the mud, unable even to find his own son’s school.

Now he had a company, a project he’d just won, a group of people working with him, and contract money about to come in.

He should have been happy.

But sitting there, what floated up in his head was the thing Cong Shasha had passed on that afternoon — a two-week notice period. That meant that half a month from now at the most, Yuanwang would be completely out. Would Gao Fei feel the pain? Would Diao Kexin?

He imagined the scene: Gao Fei sitting in that office of his by the Xiang River, getting the call, listening, putting the phone down — what would the look on his face be? He imagined Diao Kexin standing beside him, or sitting across from him, hearing Gao Fei out — and what would her face look like?

At that thought, the corner of Zheng Zhiyong’s mouth moved. Not a smile. Something else.

He remembered last winter, sitting in that tiny office with dead fish floating in the tank, drinking cup after cup of tea across from Lu You, telling his whole rotten story from beginning to end, over and over. Back then he didn’t even know where next month’s rent would come from. Back then Gao Fei was in his tower by the Xiang River with Diao Kexin at his side, and they had everything.

Now he sat in this building in Xi’an, gripping an award notice. Gao Fei and Diao Kexin — by now they must know they’d lost this one.

Did it feel good?

Damn right it felt good.

But it was the kind of good feeling that had been pressed down a long time and had finally pushed a little bit of its head up out of the ground — with the smell of earth on it, with a bitter, astringent taste. He remembered the day he signed the divorce agreement, Zhang Jingyu pushing the paper across to him, his hand shaking as he signed. He remembered moving out of that home — a few cardboard boxes, a two-cubic-meter storage unit, holding twenty years of his life. He remembered Diao Kexin’s flat, final “Yes” on the phone, remembered the night after she blocked him, going through one app after another, and not a single message would send.

All of those things, one by one, were still there. Winning the bid couldn’t cure them. Winning the bid only meant that, from today on, he had a tiny bit of strength to hit back.

He picked up his phone, went to his contacts, scrolled to the three characters of “Diao Kexin.” The number was still there; he hadn’t deleted it or blocked it. He stared at those three characters for a long time, then put the phone down.

What could he say? “I won”? “Did you see”? “You chose wrong”?

And what if he did say it? Someone like her, hearing that, would probably just give a little smile, reply “Congratulations, Brother Yong,” put the phone down and turn to talk with Gao Fei about something else.

Zheng Zhiyong picked the phone up, then put it down again. In the end he stood up, went to the window and lit a cigarette.

He was halfway through it when he suddenly remembered something: today was Zheng Ruixi’s birthday.

He sent Zheng Ruixi a message on WeChat: “Happy birthday, son. Want your old man to give you a call?” No reply.

Then he sent Zheng Ruixi a 1,000-yuan red packet. A few minutes later, the red packet was collected.

He breathed out, feeling a little bit of connection. These days his head had been full of nothing but bid documents, bid openings, winning the bid; he’d nearly forgotten his son’s birthday.

He stubbed out the cigarette, sat back down at the desk, opened WeChat and typed a line: “It’s our son’s birthday. Can I see him? How would I see him?”

When he’d typed it, he stared at the screen for a while. No one replied. Then he turned the phone’s screen off, opened his computer, and started writing up the work plan for after the award: contract follow-up, the advance payment, scheduling production in the supply chain, expanding the team, building up a reserve of sales leads.

Halfway through, the phone lit up.

It was a WeChat from Wu Peng: “Mr. Zheng, news from Yuanwang — they got the notice this afternoon too. I hear Chairman Gao sat in his office a long time and didn’t come out.”

Zheng Zhiyong looked at the message for a dozen seconds or so and replied: “Got it. Nine tomorrow morning, a short meeting to divide up the post-award work.”

Wu Peng replied: “Okay.”

Zheng Zhiyong put down his phone and went on writing his work plan.

Outside the window, the Xi’an night was very still.
