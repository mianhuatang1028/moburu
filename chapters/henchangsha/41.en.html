“Xiqu” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Central Mountains”), on Mount Li there is a beast shaped like an ox, with a dark blue-green body; its cry is like a baby’s, it eats people, and it is called the xiqu. As the saying goes: blue-green, ox-shaped, crying like a baby — it builds no den of its own, but moves into other beasts’ burrows.

That month, the R&D review had only just been passed. At the academy, Dr. Xiao signed off on the final version of the technical proposal. The questions several experts asked at the review were basically all covered by the materials Engineer Wang had prepared in advance by email. The review’s conclusion came down: the R&D stage was passed, and the commissioning tender could move forward. How the primary and secondary supplier shares would be split was a battle still to be fought, outcome unknown.

This time Old Zheng came alone. Cong Shasha was in Xi’an keeping an eye on sales, and Zhang Hailu was leading the team on the next stage’s prototype. He landed at Daxing and took a taxi to Fengtai. The night before, Dr. Xiao had sent him her address and said, “Come to my place for dinner tomorrow night?” Old Zheng replied, “Wouldn’t going to your home be awkward? How about that Chongqing hotpot from last time?” She replied, “Tomorrow’s my birthday. Come try my cooking.”

When he stood at her door, there were a bouquet of flowers and a cream cake there for him. He’d ordered both before his plane took off, to be delivered to Xiao Jing’s door at a set time, and by the time Old Zheng arrived, the gifts were already sitting quietly by the door. The door opened, and here Dr. Xiao turned back into Xiao Jing — in a dress, an apron tied on, her hair a good deal longer than a few months ago, falling to her shoulders now. She said, “Slippers.”

Old Zheng changed his shoes and put the flowers and the cake he’d brought on the dining table in the living room. Xiao Jing said, “I didn’t expect presents. That’s thoughtful of you. Sit down first — I’ll go back to the kitchen.” Old Zheng followed her into the kitchen. The range hood was still roaring; on the counter were green peppers washed but not yet cut, shredded pork, and a clay pot simmering. Old Zheng said, “Actually, I really love cooking. You’re the birthday girl today — how about I do it?”

“Then you do it.” Xiao Jing untied her apron and slipped it over Old Zheng’s head, then took him by the shoulders, turned him around and tied the strings of the apron for him, like an old married couple. In these past six months the two of them had met privately more times than they could probably remember themselves. Every time he came to Beijing, apart from academy business, there were always private dinners, singing, walks. When she’d drunk too much, he took her home — and how could Xiao Jing not have known about that gaze of his, fixed on her as she slept? What was more, Xiao Jing knew nothing about Old Zheng and Cong Shasha. What she knew was limited to Old Zheng’s divorce from his ex-wife and the story of how he’d been hurt by Diao Kexin. All this while, Old Zheng had kept up a close, gentle manner toward Xiao Jing, now near, now distant, and Xiao Jing couldn’t make it out either. She felt the man in front of her seemed to be interested in her, but was too shy herself to pierce that last thin paper window between them.

Old Zheng first looked at what was in the fridge, picked out a few things, then put vermicelli in to soak — washing, trimming, chopping, all in one smooth run. You could tell at a glance he’d done this kind of work for years. Back before his divorce, the cooking at home had been almost entirely his job. He liked to eat and knew how to cook, and he’d cooked for his wife and child gladly. Old Zheng was good at braised meat and stir-fries and liked his food spicy, and he’d brought the whole family, old and young, around to loving spice. Later, when he traveled a lot, Zhang Jingyu mostly just made a bit of something with noodles or ordered takeout.

Old Zheng asked Xiao Jing, “Can you eat spicy?” Xiao Jing said, “I love spicy.” Old Zheng answered, “Good. Then I’ll put some heaven-facing chilies in the stir-fried pork. You just go sit down and wait.”

Xiao Jing went to wash her hands and came back to stand in the kitchen doorway, watching Old Zheng cook. Old Zheng’s chopping and stir-frying were brisk: high flame, plenty of oil, he could even toss the wok. How much salt, how much heat — it all came from his hands, without thinking. Xiao Jing watched for a while and said, “You really can cook.”

Old Zheng made Hunan-style stir-fried pork, pork tenderloin wrapped in egg with shrimp, fish-fragrant eggplant, and king oyster mushrooms with diced beef. He added spices and vermicelli to the meatball soup simmering in the clay pot on the stove, and baby bok choy at the end, and that was the lot, ready for the table.

The two of them sat on either side of Xiao Jing’s little round dining table, glasses raised. Then they lit the candles, Xiao Jing made a wish, they each had a little piece of cake, and they started on dinner. At first they chatted on and off about supply guarantees and contract manufacturing; then both of them said less and less, and the air between them grew delicate.

Xiao Jing poured him another glass of wine, and one for herself. She still couldn’t hold her drink; two glasses and her face was red. She looked at him and said, “Zheng Zhiyong, do you remember me telling you about having my fortune told?”

Old Zheng said, “What fortune? I don’t remember.” He meant it, too. The time he and Diao Kexin had gone to have their fortunes told was still vivid in his mind, but the time Xiao Jing had mentioned, half joking over dinner a few months back, that she’d had her marriage prospects read — he’d long since forgotten all about it.

Xiao Jing said, “I had my fortune told. At thirty-five I’d meet my true match. And now — thirty-five is here.”

Old Zheng tried to remember, and yes, there had been a conversation like that. And now it was as clear to Old Zheng as a mirror: the woman PhD in front of him had feelings for him.

This thirty-five-year-old woman usually looked so stern, so haughty, so hard to deal with. But in private, with no one around, she liked singing, liked soft colors, liked clothes in pure wool and fine knits. She kept her home spotless, raised rows of succulents, and even had a strawberry-bear plush toy on her sofa. And look at Xiao Jing’s looks: the delicate face under the glasses, the fair skin. Though she never wore makeup and never wore jewelry, her femininity was obvious — neither fat nor thin, neither tall nor short. Especially whenever she was working with them, discussing the technical proposal, and pushed up her sleeves to show a little bit of forearm, fair and rosy, so fine you couldn’t see the pores — many times, when she was writing on the whiteboard, Old Zheng had found himself gazing absently at that bit of bare arm for a while.

Compared with Xiao Jing, Cong Shasha didn’t like to express her feelings openly, but she had the edge in youth and looks, slim and slender. When he was with her, Old Zheng had a self-satisfied sense of still being attractive to a young girl, and a feeling of conquest. Their relationship wasn’t wild and passionate, but there was always an understanding between them. And this past year Shasha had grown steadier and steadier in temperament; when they were together she looked after him in every little way, and when they were apart she never demanded emotional attention. Being with her always felt comfortable and easy.

But Xiao Jing — this woman actually brought up feelings with him this directly. Old Zheng understood that she was dropping him a hint, and understood even better that now the initiative in this was in his own hands. After a slight pause Old Zheng said, “I remember it now. I just don’t know — this true match of yours, what’s his surname?”

Xiao Jing gave a shy, coquettish smile and shot Old Zheng a sidelong look, and that one look set Old Zheng’s heart racing and rippling. Who’d have thought that under the woman PhD’s severe, starchy exterior there could be such a coy, sexy look? The enormous contrast made Xiao Jing, just then, seem sexy and radiant.

Xiao Jing said, “Don’t you know what my true match’s surname is?”

Old Zheng raised his glass. “I don’t know. You’ll have to tell me. Is it Zheng?”

That night, Old Zheng didn’t leave. While they were washing the dishes their hands met and held, and then neither of them said anything; they both felt the dishes could wait. Hand in hand, the two of them walked together into Xiao Jing’s bedroom. Xiao Jing had Old Zheng take off his trousers, and Old Zheng sat on the edge of the bed in his shirt and underwear.

Xiao Jing said nothing. She bent down, took his face in both hands and kissed him. The kiss wasn’t light and it wasn’t testing; it came straight down on him, with a faint taste of wine and with that force she usually kept hidden. Old Zheng’s hands came up and settled on her waist.

He pulled her gently, and she sat down on the edge of the bed too. Their kiss went deeper, longer this time than before, heavier, and both of them were breathing hard. Her hands slid down from his shoulders and undid his shirt buttons, one, two, not fast, but without hesitating. Old Zheng’s hand moved up from the side of her waist, over the fine knit of her dress, warm underneath.

She drew back a little and said, “I’ll do it myself.”

She pulled the dress off over her head and threw it on the chair at the foot of the bed. Underneath she wore a matching set of lace underwear, and she didn’t cover herself; she looked at him. The two of them lay down on the bed, Old Zheng on his side, looking down at her, and he suddenly felt this woman was completely different from what he’d imagined. At the academy she wore a dark suit and white shirt, her hair short, her face showing nothing, her words sharp enough to choke you. Now her hair fell to her shoulders, her voice was gentle, her face was flushed, her eyes were on him — and the look of her was ardent, alive, urgent.

He said, “Xiao Jing.”

She said, “Mm.”

He said, “May I?”

She said, “Come here.”

So Old Zheng kissed her. What came after was more ardent than Old Zheng had imagined. That coldness and hardness she usually had all melted away at this point, turned into a response that was very direct and unhidden, and later on she was even taking the lead. Old Zheng let her lead him; at first he couldn’t quite keep up, and then he let go.

She talked in his ear, her voice low, her breath hot, saying things she never usually said. Listening, Old Zheng felt all the tangled mess in his head scatter, leaving only the person in front of him, this body, this warmth.

When it was over, the two of them lay side by side, catching their breath for a while. Only the bedside lamp was on, its light falling on the pale yellow flowered comforter.

She rolled over, buried her face in the hollow of his shoulder, rested her hand on his chest, her fingers pressing slowly, again and again. Old Zheng didn’t move, looking at the ceiling.

Xiao Jing said, “Are you my true match?”

Old Zheng said, “Oh, the dishes still aren’t done. I’ll go wash the dishes.”

Xiao Jing said, “Wash what? Do them tomorrow.” And with that she grabbed hold of him.

He reached out and drew her head a little closer, onto his shoulder.
