Chapter Fifty-Five: Qiyu

Translation first published: 2026-11-19 20:25 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-19 20:25 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Qiyu” — in the Classic of Mountains and Seas, the Northern Mountains, on Mount Dai there is a bird shaped like a crow, with five colours and red markings, named Qiyu; it is male and female in itself, and eating it cures abscesses. Truly, five colours and red markings, both sexes self-sufficient — needing not to lean on another in order to take form, needing not to prove one's worth through a relationship; only when a person can at last be whole alone has she truly walked out of the old life.

That evening Wu Peng called Wu You to report. “Uncle, to report — Zhang Hailu and the others have finished the formalities for their departure. Mr. Zheng spoke with him today, and the year-end bonus promised earlier has been confirmed; as we agreed, it will be paid out over fifteen months.”

Wu You nodded, and Wu Peng went on:

“Yes. As you reminded me before, non-compete compensation has to be paid monthly — you can't simply write a figure into the agreement and then fail to honour it in practice. We've arranged for Mr. Zheng to release the money month by month; nominally it's a year-end bonus, but in effect it has become monthly non-compete compensation. The relevant agreements and payment arrangements have already been handed over to HR.”

Wu You did not comment further on the matter:

“The truth is, we're still not certain whether Zhang Hailu and the others have gone to Yuanwang, are we?”

Wu Peng said yes.

“Zhang Hailu handled a good deal of procurement and project money at the company before, didn't he?”

“Yes. Project expenditure for the technical department, and some outsourced project management and procurement payments — a lot of that he followed in the past.”

“Then go over that part of the accounts again.”

Wu Peng did not answer at once.

Wu You went on: “One direction is expenditure. The money he handled — where it went, and whether the contracts, invoices and payment records can be matched up. The other direction is income. On the projects he was responsible for, whether the contract amounts, the actual deliveries, the receipts and the settlements are consistent. Don't look only at the figures on the financial books; check the business records alongside them.”

“I understand. I'll have finance and the relevant departments sort out the projects he handled.”

“Don't rush to conclusions, and don't prejudge. First gather all the material, then check it project by project, item by item. If you find any inconsistencies, dig deeper. If there's no problem, that's good for the company; and if there really is a problem, that's good for the company too — if Zhang Hailu and the others really have gone to Yuanwang, then we'll have a card to play.”

Wu Peng indicated to his uncle that he “understood”.

“One more thing. Oversee the audit yourself. Don't tell Zheng Zhiyong.”

A week later, Xiangcheng. Zhang Hailu arrived to report for duty with the other two. Lan, however, had not followed Zhang Hailu. After leaving Xinghuan he went to Shenzhen and joined an AI camera company called Firefly, entering its algorithm team. Shenzhen's industrial environment was closer to the technical direction he had known before, and the new team offered him a different choice. He did not return to Xiangcheng, nor did he take any further part in Zhang Hailu's arrangements.

Zhu Yiwen had fully taken over the Yuanwang building, and the sign on the top floor had been replaced with the two large characters for Gaoda. He gave Diao Kexin's technical team half a floor, and so Zhang Hailu — once CTO of Yuanwang's technical team — and his old subordinates came back to work here in a strange fashion. The world turns and circles, and often returns to its starting point.

When Diao Kexin met Zhang Hailu, she did not process the three of them directly into Yuanwang's employment, but adopted a more flexible approach. Through a company she controlled, she provided the capital to set up a separate technical-services company. The shareholding was arranged so that Zhang Hailu held ninety percent and Xiao Chen ten percent. This company then signed a technical-services agreement with Yuanwang, under which Yuanwang paid it a monthly technical-service fee. In legal terms, Zhang Hailu and Xiao Chen were shareholders of an outside company, and Yuanwang was buying a service — no employment relationship was directly established with the three. In practice, once the company received the fees from Yuanwang, it used them to pay the wages and related costs of Zhang Hailu, Xiao Chen and Xiao Li. In this way the three had in effect entered Yuanwang's technical system without appearing as formal employees of Yuanwang.

Diao Kexin had no need to add three conspicuous names to Yuanwang's organisational chart, nor to make Zhang Hailu confront Xinghuan's non-compete problem directly. Externally, they were simply the team of a technical-services company; internally, they formally began to take on Yuanwang's technical work.

On Wu Peng's side, he now set about checking the company's past payments and receipts, the largest of which was the eight-million-yuan sum paid during the night-vision device project with Gaoda. Wu Peng thought for a moment, picked up his phone and found Sister Liu, the project manager on Gaoda's side. It happened that back when Wu Peng was still Zheng Zhiyong's assistant, he had dealt with her on a good many matters of project coordination and financial liaison; she had even come to Xi'an during the project, and Wu Peng had been the one to host her.

Wu Peng said: “The New Year's nearly here, and I'd like to send Sister Liu and Mr. Zhu some of Xi'an's special cured beef — if it's convenient, could you give me a mailing address?” The other party demurred politely a few times.

But Wu Peng insisted, so she said: “Mr. Wu, I've heard you've been promoted to deputy general manager — and still you're so thoughtful, thank you so much. But I really mustn't. I'm pregnant, you see, an older mother-to-be; after the New Year I plan to take a long leave for a good while, to settle down and raise the child. You only need to send things to Mr. Zhu; as for the mailing, send it to Mr. Zhu or to me, either is fine — I'll pass it on to him.”

“But that's good news, Sister Liu — all the more reason to send some beef to build up your strength. Even if Mr. Zhu gets nothing from me, I'll still send some to you.”

Sister Liu could not talk him out of it, so she told Wu Peng their address had moved to the Yuanwang building in Xiangcheng, and explained: “Our Mr. Zhu bought the building when Yuanwang auctioned it off, but the sign has been changed and the old street names and so on haven't all been changed yet — probably after the New Year. For mailing right now, you'll still have to write Yuanwang Road, Yuanwang Building.”

“See how I call today and every piece of news I hear is good news. Mr. Zhu acquiring property, the joy of moving house, and your joy of expecting a child — my congratulations will be on their way soon!”

“Mr. Wu, you're far too kind. Thank you indeed.”

“Sister Liu, please don't stand on ceremony with me.”

“All right, all right,” Sister Liu said. Then she paused suddenly and, after hesitating a few seconds, went on: “Director Wu, what a coincidence — today at the company compound I saw Director Zhang Hailu and even said hello. Has he come to our company on business?”

Wu Peng's heart gave a start. He had meant to revive a long-dormant contact first, and only later learn gradually through Sister Liu whether any detail of their past financial dealings could still be traced. Instead he had stumbled quite by accident onto an enormous piece of news — could it be that Zhang Hailu and the others had not gone to Yuanwang at all, but to Gaoda? He replied: “You see, Sister Liu, Director Hailu reports to Mr. Zheng; I've had little to do with him lately, so I know nothing about it.”

After hanging up, Wu Peng immediately arranged for a trusted administrative colleague to fly to Xiangcheng that evening, telling him to go to the Yuanwang building — now called the Gaoda building — hire a car, and watch to see whether Zhang Hailu, Xiao Chen and Xiao Li came to work. “Wait by the stump for the rabbit,” he said. “If you see them, remember to take photos.”

He assigned no one else, nor did he widen the matter. For him, what mattered most now was not to reach a conclusion quickly, but to find pieces of information that could corroborate one another.

Two days later the man called him: “Mr. Wu, I've got them. The three of them — in early in the morning, out in the afternoon, two days running.”

After hanging up, Wu Peng opened his computer and began searching for information on Zhang Hailu. Soon he found a newly established technical-services company. It was registered in Xiangcheng, with Zhang Hailu holding ninety percent of the shares, and the other shareholder none other than Xiao Chen from the original team.

Wu Peng stared at the screen for a while. The matter seemed to be taking shape, but he still could not be sure what the company's relationship with Yuanwang actually was. He saved the registration details, and made separate notes of the times and places Zhang Hailu had appeared and of the circumstances of the other people involved.

He telephoned Wu You and Zheng Zhiyong separately to tell them what he had.

That evening, when he went to spend the night at Shasha's place, he told her about it too,

Shasha said: “Quite as expected. Only, we didn't know before — so they've gone to Gaoda?”

Wu Peng said: “A reasonable inference. Gaoda, Yuanwang, or Zhang Hailu providing them with technical outsourcing — all three possibilities exist.”

Shasha said: “Technical outsourcing — that's a clever move.”

Wu Peng said: “Not quite. The non-compete agreement stipulates that even this form triggers the clause, so long as the client is a party barred by the non-compete — and Gaoda and Yuanwang are even named explicitly in it.”

Shasha looked at Wu Peng admiringly. The twenty-six-year-old boy had matured a great deal before she knew it.

Wu Peng changed tack: “Shasha, at this moment, do you know what a great service you've done us by winning Xiao Jing over?”

Shasha smiled coyly. That day, back in Wu Peng's hotel room, she had told him so much of what had been exchanged — but she had not told him the actual conversation by which she had won Xiao Jing over. It had been a three-hour talk of crucial importance to Xiao Jing and to herself alike.

It was a bright, sunlit afternoon, the light transparent and candid and ardent, and Shasha talked with Xiao Jing for a long time that day.

At first they talked of Zheng Zhiyong and Diao Kexin. Shasha told Xiao Jing the whole of Diao Kexin's background, and the story of what had passed between her and Zheng Zhiyong — more legendary than any novel — exactly as it had happened.

“How does it make you feel, to know all that?” Shasha asked Xiao Jing.

“Truly, it doesn't. I feel I've been listening to a story with nothing to do with me. What I feel more is the standpoint of a third party: I find myself wondering what made Diao Kexin the woman she is, and what made Zheng Zhiyong become what he later became.”

Complex thoughts arose in Shasha. The two were fellow provincials, professional peers, women of much the same age; she had meant to use the gossip to draw them closer. Instead, Xiao Jing's words at that moment held up a mirror to Shasha's own heart. Having lived through her fleeting affair with Old Zheng, Shasha felt she was looking at the doings of a woman named “the past” — one with nothing to do with the self she was now — and of a man equally unrelated to her.

Shasha asked: “Sister Jing, so that experience of dating Zheng Zhiyong — it won't have any emotional effect on you now, will it?”

Xiao Jing smiled. “I don't think it will. In fact, I consider it a mistake. My relationship with him began as a working relationship, one about making money together, and yet I let a feminine emotional trait get the better of me. My admiration for him relaxed the cooperative rigour I should have maintained — the principle, for instance, of being more circumspect where one's own interests are concerned.”

Shasha said: “I've a rough idea of your contract with the company, and I do think you should have had more.”

“That doesn't necessarily mean I should have had more. My fault was in confusing the two — in putting the relationship above the work. That is a natural trait in women; I knew it perfectly well, yet on a human level I indulged it. Nor do I feel I was cheated. I think it is a road one must travel: only by going through it does one reflect, does one understand,” Xiao Jing replied.

Shasha said: “Do you mean that a business relationship shouldn't be mixed up with emotion?”

Xiao Jing said: “For me, yes. But for Diao Kexin it is the reverse. Among women's strategies for survival, hers may be seen as a shortcut.”

Shasha: “Diao Kexin always seems to know exactly what she wants. Every relationship she has had with a man has made a better version of herself, while every man who has been with her has been destroyed, or very nearly.”

Xiao Jing laughed. “For Diao Kexin to wield that strategy to such an extreme is, one might say, the fighter plane of the female-competition arena. Few could carry it that far. But I neither envy nor admire it. I live by my own ability — without gender — that is the me as I originally am.”

Shasha: “Sister Jing, simply for your being able to say that today, my admiration for you has reached its peak.”

“How does that differ from the me of the past?”

“The you of the past was strong in technology, but weak in emotion, and as a person I could not see you clearly. At this moment I feel you are someone candid and ardent, outward and inward the same; and you have shown me another road of possibility for women — to live by one's ability, without gender, to be an original self.”

“Yes, Shasha,” Xiao Jing smiled. “You understand me, and I believe you are the same sort of person.”

Shasha said nothing. Her relationship with Zheng Zhiyong was a secret kept in a black box — Xiao Jing did not know, Wu Peng did not know, Zhang Hailu did not know, no one knew. And the efforts and sacrifices she had made for this company were things no one in the world knew of either. Now Xiao Jing's question came straight at her, and Shasha needed to give herself an answer. After thinking a moment she replied solemnly: “Sister Jing, if I must choose between your road and Diao Kexin's, I choose yours. I hope, too, to become a person like you.”

Xiao Jing went on: “Diao Kexin is a clever woman. She knows where the resources lie, and she knows that relations between people can generate value. She probably understood earlier than most that the business world does not apportion rewards solely by ability — that connections, information, timing, even a person's emotions, may all become chips. There is nothing wrong in these perceptions in themselves. The question is, once a person is used to reading every relationship as a chip, can she still believe in one that requires no calculation? And when her world contains nothing but interest, can she still possess a truly pure feeling?”

Shasha nodded. “Yes. I don't envy her in the least — even though today, step by step, she has become a woman boss and acquired greater wealth.”

Xiao Jing said: “I don't know whether she sees it that way. But if in a relationship both sides are calculating who holds more of the initiative, it can hardly remain only a relationship. Today you gain resources through me, tomorrow I lose an opportunity through you; today I give ground because I have greater expectations, tomorrow I do not give ground because I feel I have already given enough. In the end, what the two are contending over may long since have ceased to be the relationship — it is that neither is willing to admit she has lost. So the best and purest feeling should not be mingled with interest.” These words of Xiao Jing's were a reckoning of the gains and losses from the last time she had fallen in love while collaborating with Zheng Zhiyong; yet to Shasha's ears they carried more to reflect upon.

Shasha gazed out of the window, and after a while said: “Sister Jing, so I feel that the business opportunity I've brought this time — you won't refuse me.”

“Of course not. Nothing on earth is going to stop Big Sister here from making money, darling.”

Shasha laughed. “And love?”

“Love is of course important. People aren't machines; just because one has a career doesn't mean one can pretend not to need feeling.”

“Then when career and love come into conflict, which should be placed first?”

Xiao Jing did not answer at once; she paused in thought. “I think the question itself is a little awry. Career and love are not two people standing in a queue for you to choose between. They are different parts of life. What truly needs ordering is your own set of values. You may adjust your work for love, you may give up a relationship for your career, but you must be clear why you do it — and you must know that the values at the root of your humanity matter more than either of those two. Only in the presence of sound values will you know whether the two can coexist, or whether you face a choice between them.”

These words left Shasha quiet for a long while. She thought of Wu Peng.

She thought of the rather awkward naturalness with which he had first called her his wife, of how he was willing to listen as she recounted the things that had happened in her day, and of how, when the two were together, neither needed to make a show of being cleverer, more charming, or more worthy of love. She suddenly realised that she cherished this relationship not because of anything Wu Peng could give her, but because in it she never had to be constantly considering how she ought to behave in order to get more.

She could simply be Cong Shasha, and she could be her original self again. She found that she had never wanted very much; in the ordering of her life, interest had never occupied so forward a place.

“Sister Jing, what do you think bad things a person has lived through signify?” she asked.

Xiao Jing smiled. “I count all my mistakes as wealth. If I had never known confusion, where would my clarity have come from?”

“Isn't that too late?”

“Darling, you're only twenty-nine, and I'm only thirty-five. You know Jung, whom I like — in the Red Book he wrote that the life before forty is all experiment.”

The sunlight outside the window shifted slowly, and the pool of light on the desk grew gradually smaller. They reflected one another, confirmed one another, encouraged one another, and no one brought the conversation to a final conclusion — yet it was a marvellous afternoon, descending upon the two of them unawares.

Whenever Shasha looked back on that afternoon, she realised that what she had truly come to see was not what kind of person Diao Kexin was. She had once regarded Diao Kexin as a specimen of success, envied her methods, even thought of copying the way she obtained resources. But that afternoon, for the first time, she realised that she did not in fact share that set of values. She was not opposed to women pursuing power, wealth and a career, nor did she think a woman must prove her worth by keeping her heart pure and her desires few. She was only growing clearer that some things may become abilities, while others become shackles.

She wanted a career of her own and the ability to choose her life independently; she wanted love as well, but was unwilling to exchange the former for it, still less to let the latter swallow her whole.

After that afternoon, Shasha began to understand that the so-called independence does not mean a woman needs no one at all; it means she no longer has to give up her original self in order to obtain anything.
