Mengji

Chapter Forty-Two: Mengji

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Mengji” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Northern Mountains”), on Mount Shizhe there is a beast shaped like a leopard, with a patterned forehead and a white body, called the mengji; it is good at lying hidden, and it cries out its own name. As the saying goes: however deep it lies hidden, when it opens its mouth, out comes its own name.

In the Army Equipment Department’s supply-assurance assessment of contractor units, the Seventh Academy, too, was only the vendor side.

Hailu and Zheng Zhiyong had known this before, but going by past experience, they’d been optimistic. The research academy was responsible for approving the design and ticking off the specs; supply assurance and risk control were, in the end, jointly vetted by the military’s supply-assurance assessment and the equipment-development system. The assessment system’s scoring mechanism looked instead at the hard strength of the entity — whether it had its own factory buildings, what the utilization rate of its production-line equipment was, how many industrial workers it had on the books, how many years its military-inspection quality system had been running. These rules were hard against hard.

Xinghuan’s materials for the preliminary assessment submitted its own company together with an outsourced factory in Guangdong that had supported the R&D. In the Seventh Academy’s eyes, that passed. But as the Equipment Development Department and the Army saw it, the office building wasn’t big enough; the Level-2 secrecy qualification and the contractor-qualification certificate were the bare minimum; and the existing factory floor space in Dongguan, its construction standards and the degree of automation of its production-line equipment were all on the weak side.

The day the preliminary scores came out, Zhang Hailu went to a meeting at the academy. The product’s technical design was to be used by all the suppliers in common. The project office people put each company’s introductory video up on the screen. The other two were both A-share-listed defense companies with annual revenue of five hundred million to a billion. Next to them, Xinghuan’s supply capability wasn’t just on the weak side — it was a little kid.

On the Army Equipment side, the night-vision device was equipment for commissioning into service, a key project. Over the next five years, annual procurement would be roughly a hundred to two hundred million, and in an emergency it could go up. What the military wanted was delivery at any moment, and units without enough supply capability, as a rule, didn’t get into the primary-supplier slot. The research academy, for its part, wanted to win Xinghuan a bigger share of the supply chain on the strength of its being the R&D team, but as things stood, even secondary supplier was out of reach; tertiary supplier they’d have to fight for. Going by the primary supplier taking forty to fifty percent, the secondary thirty to forty, and the tertiary ten to twenty, Xinghuan’s supply chain was the only one that had done the pilot-lead mass production, but constrained by capacity, it could only get twenty percent at the very most.

Zhang Hailu brought the results back to Xi’an and laid it all out for Old Zheng in his office. It was what they’d expected, and yet it went against all fairness: the listed companies suddenly charging in at the end to grab supply shares — that was what fit the grand plan. At the same time, the supply chain’s unit cost had been slashed by a big chunk; prices were knocked right down, so that even if they got twenty percent of the orders, the profit margin would drop too, to around thirty or forty percent.

When Old Zheng had heard him out, he sat in his chair without moving. He smoked a cigarette, and only then did he understand something: just as Yuanwang had been dragged to death by its funding bet and its payment terms, he was now up against being choked to death by “physical assets.” In this industry, a briefcase company could do early-stage R&D, but when it really came to commissioning, ramping up volume and fighting for a share of the supply chain, what mattered more were the infrastructure and factory buildings, the production-line capacity, the precision of the toolmaking, the efficiency and automation of the lines — far more than just your own personal technical skill and algorithms.

When the news reached the team, people’s spirits started to scatter.

The few key technical people under Zhang Hailu had taken pay cuts and slogged on for more than half a year, counting on winning the commissioning tender so the bonuses would let them get some blood back in one go. Now, hearing they might not even get primary supplier, Little Chen grumbled to Zhang Hailu, “Maybe I should just go back to Xiangcheng.” Being CTO like this was galling; it stuck in his chest.

Xiao Jing wasn’t happy either. Since she’d first gone to bed with Old Zheng, they’d slept together another two or three nights when Old Zheng was in town on business. Xiao Jing wanted to make it official that they were boyfriend and girlfriend, but Old Zheng turned her down on the grounds that their positions as client and vendor were too sensitive, and told Xiao Jing that if she cared so much about a title, then the two of them could just end things. Xiao Jing thought it over — titles or no titles, the agreement had been signed; at least on the supply side down the line she’d still get ten percent of the profit as income. So she stopped pressing the point. But now the Seventh Academy’s supply allocation was clear, and the Seventh Academy’s total share was only about twenty million. She worked it out: Old Zheng’s side would get fourteen million a year, she’d get ten percent of their gross margin, and that would put three to five hundred thousand a year in her hands — far below what she’d originally expected. To put it bluntly, winning the R&D and design work had been down to the core algorithm technology and R&D experience, and those were all Xiao Jing’s. The return she’d originally expected was at least three to five times what it was now.

Xiao Jing called Zheng Zhiyong and asked him to come to Beijing to meet and talk. She wanted to renegotiate the percentage: ten percent definitely wouldn’t do anymore; she hoped to raise it to twenty. Old Zheng didn’t know what Xiao Jing wanted to meet about, so he told her that his priority now was to nail down the supply-chain capacity in Guangzhou and Shenzhen, so they could fight for a bigger share as tertiary supplier.

On that point, Xiao Jing of course was supportive.

When he hung up, he booked a ticket to Guangzhou, to do a round of the Greater Bay Area first.

In fact, during the R&D stage, the reason Old Zheng’s team had been able to produce the prototype in an office building and pass the academy’s review was that the toolmakers of the Greater Bay Area and an electronics factory in Dongguan had worked closely with them. This factory, which made optoelectronic components in Shenzhen, belonged to a boss named Lin. Over those six months and more of R&D, the drawings Old Zheng and Zhang Hailu made and the algorithms “Engineer Wang” revised had all, in the end, been realized by Boss Lin’s factory, which helped with the samples, made the molds and did the small-batch trial production. The pilot production line was Boss Lin’s line. For the acceptance — those few days when the inspectors came to audit the factory — Boss Lin had even gone along with Xinghuan and swapped out the sign at his factory gate.

But in the R&D stage, with volumes of a few dozen or a hundred-odd sets, Boss Lin’s line could squeeze it in. Now it was the commissioning tender, orders for tens of thousands of units, and what the Army Equipment people wanted was guaranteed capacity. Boss Lin’s capacity wasn’t enough. Old Zheng went to talk to him, hoping he’d invest more in equipment so they could fight for twenty to twenty-five percent as tertiary supplier, which would still mean considerable revenue — but it would take several million in production-line costs up front.

Boss Lin heard him out in his office, then took a sheet of paper and scribbled on it, doing the math for a long time. “Sure, Mr. Zheng. It’s just — this investment, would Xinghuan be covering it? Or how much of it could you put in?”

Old Zheng drank some tea and said nothing. He understood Boss Lin’s math. This wasn’t about personal favors; it was a manufacturer doing life-or-death sums. Sink several million in just to take on an intent of his that wasn’t even in the bag yet — who would dare?

By the time he came out of Boss Lin’s, it was already dark. Old Zheng stood at the roadside, lit a cigarette and took two drags. He pulled out his phone and called Wang Renhou: “Mr. Wang, I’m in Shenzhen. Are you free tomorrow? How about I come over and take a look at your factory?” Naturally he was welcome.

The next morning he arrived at Wang Renhou’s factory.

Wang Renhou was making tea in his office. When he saw him come in he stood up, shook his hand and said, “Mr. Zheng, welcome, welcome! Long time no see — you’ve slimmed down and got even more handsome.”

Old Zheng said, “Hardly. I’m at my wits’ end, my head’s full of troubles.”

Wang Renhou poured him tea and said, “That’s the defense trade for you. Not easy.”

Old Zheng said, “Not easy. The technology’s too hard, the payment terms are too long, the cash flow’s no good. It’s not a good business.”

Wang Renhou said, “But you’re all contributing to the country, aren’t you? When we take Taiwan back someday, aren’t we counting on you lot! We definitely admire you.”

Old Zheng: “Boss Wang, you’re making fun of me again.”

Wang Renhou said, “Mr. Zheng, how have things been for you personally lately? Any good developments?”

The last time Zheng Zhiyong had stayed at Wang Renhou’s for a few days working on the motor, Wang Renhou had treated Old Zheng to drinks and dinner on several evenings, and had more or less got the whole story of Old Zheng’s past. Not only had he worked out that Old Zheng wasn’t actually any kind of boss or shareholder at Xinghuan, he also knew about Old Zheng’s love life and his mountain of debt. The man across from him was a fellow with a past.

Wang Renhou had a taste for gossip too and wanted to find out how things had moved along on that front. Old Zheng didn’t catch the meaning behind the words and answered, “My personal debts are basically all sorted out now, but money’s still tight. Once the monthly support payments go out, there’s not a penny left over. I won’t hide it from you — coming to see you on my own this time, I’ve got a bit of a personal idea too.”

The moment he heard this, Wang Renhou’s taste for gossip stopped dead. He’d admired Old Zheng’s technical skill for a long time, and this was a rare chance to win over a talent. So Wang Renhou’s eyes lit up and he asked, “What are you thinking, Mr. Zheng? Getting ready to strike out on your own?”

Old Zheng said, “Our last collaboration was very, very important to my company’s cash flow. But really, that collaboration was just me cashing in my own technical skill on my own — it didn’t have much to do with the company. To be honest, I’m living too hand-to-mouth right now. So I’ve been thinking: is there a chance of working with you again this time, and could our collaboration from now on be between me, Zheng Zhiyong, and you, rather than between my company and you?”

Wang Renhou laughed at once — wasn’t that just perfect! The two of them agreed on the spot that, starting this year, the commission portion agreed in their last collaboration would be paid directly to Zheng Zhiyong rather than to Xinghuan. Wang Renhou would open the customs-declaration data only to Zheng Zhiyong; Zheng Zhiyong would decide how much to settle with the company, and the rest would be his own.

When they’d finished agreeing on this part, Wang Renhou took an electric fan out of a drawer and put it on the desk. The fan had no blades. Its base was a cylinder, and on top was a ring-shaped outlet.

“A bladeless fan,” Wang Renhou said. “The Dyson kind. I’ve been doing fan lights for a few years, and this year customers have started asking for this product. You know I sell to the poorest, most backward countries in the world. There’s no getting around Dyson’s patents, and even where you can get around them, the designs that do it are too expensive, the cost’s too high. The few makers in China either pay the patent fees or skirt the edges, and they’re expensive too, so my customers can’t take them. I want to make one that avoids Dyson’s patented structure — enough airflow, low noise, and most important of all, cheap. Cheap enough that my customers in those poor countries think they can sell it. Have a look — can this be done?”

Old Zheng picked up the fan and turned it over and over. He asked Wang Renhou for pen and paper and sketched a few lines on the desk. He asked, “What’s the budget?”

Wang Renhou said that all told, the finished product’s FOB ex-factory price was fourteen US dollars. After the molds and the exterior parts, the lower the cost left for the motor, the higher their profit. With the motor solutions on the market now, he couldn’t make it for fourteen dollars, or there was basically no profit in it. If Old Zheng could crack it for him, he’d open the BOM for him to look at, and of the profit, he’d give him twenty percent. He could sign the contract with him personally, or with a company he named.

Old Zheng said, “Dyson’s core patents are in the airflow amplification and the Coandă surface at the outlet. If you want to get around them, you can’t go head-to-head with them on the ring-shaped outlet. You can work on the airflow path instead. Use a mixed-flow fan instead of a centrifugal fan, do one stage of pressurization in the base, and then send the airflow to the outlet through a separate guide structure. The shape of the outlet can be changed too — it doesn’t have to be their narrow ring-shaped slit.”

As Wang Renhou listened, his eyes lit up. He said, “Tell me in more detail.”

Old Zheng sketched a few more lines and said, “Whether anyone’s patented this structure before, I’ll have to check. But as a direction, I think it can work. The key is cost, and that depends on volume. If you get it going, how high can your volume go?”

Wang Renhou said, “The first year, one to two million’s possible. After that, it could be more.”

Old Zheng was startled. If there really could be a volume of one to two million units, this wasn’t just doable — there might be a lot of money in it. If Wang Renhou’s company built its own production for this module, sinking a few million into it, Wang Renhou’s long-term cost would be lowest. But if this thing were made by Qi Jin, the quality control would be more reliable, and the money saved on equipment could go into his own pocket up front, all in one go. Besides, as Old Zheng reckoned it, he wasn’t confident he could crack the technology alone. Qi Jin’s equipment was already there — it would only need some small modular modifications to the molds. And the team was familiar too; doing it together with him would actually give them a better chance.

Old Zheng thought for a moment and said, “This is much harder than our motor last time. Doing it the way we did last time won’t work. I’ll find a specialist company to make it for you — get around the patents and bring the cost down to where it needs to be. How about that?”

Wang Renhou said of course. Best of all would be a motor supplied with its external components included. The module price he could accept, he’d tell Old Zheng the next day after his technical people had worked it out. Anyway, by his reckoning, Old Zheng would get a cut of about two yuan per unit; if the customer price or the exchange rate changed too much, they’d talk about adjusting it. In short, he’d guarantee Old Zheng profit-sharing on the order of a million a year.

The two shook hands on the alliance.

After the ups and downs of these past year or two, Old Zheng had finally taken this step. Back when he’d signed the order over to Xinghuan, what Old Zheng had in mind was doing right by the brothers who followed him, doing right by Wu You, who’d propped him up at his hardest time. But at this moment, Old Zheng finally understood that he still hadn’t got clear of the survival stage. The most important thing was to think about survival first. Love, friendship — only once he’d grown bigger and stronger could he think about anything else.
