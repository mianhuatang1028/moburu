Jiuying

Chapter Seven: Jiuying

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Jiuying” — a nine-headed monster that spits water and fire at once; each head has its own mind, and when one calls, another will not answer. As the saying goes: nine heads facing nine ways — a sleepless night.

The next day was a Tuesday. When Old Zheng went to the Changsha office, there was no sign of Diao Kexin; it seemed she had already gone back to Beijing.

On Thursday Old Zheng arrived at the Beijing office a little after eight in the morning, sat at his desk, and kept glancing up at Diao Kexin’s empty office. But that woman didn’t come in until ten. All morning she sat steady in her own office and didn’t come out until noon, to ask her assistant to order her lunch; in the afternoon she came out again to talk work with other colleagues. All day long Diao Kexin’s gaze seemed not to land on Old Zheng for even a second.

When the workday finally ended and Old Zheng had packed up to drive away, a WeChat drifted in: “Want to have dinner together?” followed by a restaurant’s location pin.

Old Zheng, sitting in the driver’s seat, replied with a single “Okay,” started his white BYD and got ready to head to the restaurant. Just then he saw Diao Kexin, across the lot, walking gracefully toward a red Audi. He pulled out and saw the Audi following close behind; for several kilometers it kept pace with him, side by side. Through his side window he watched Diao Kexin in her driver’s seat; that woman’s eyes were fixed intently ahead, as if she didn’t see him at all.

They had Japanese food. As before, Diao Kexin listened while Old Zheng talked, chin in hand now and then, gazing at him. That day Old Zheng talked about one of the few relationships of his life — to be precise, the only one. He saw Zhang Jingyu at eighteen, became her boyfriend at nineteen, became her lover at twenty, married her at twenty-six, and now he was thirty-eight; their child was in second grade.

“Brother Yong, do you love her?” Diao Kexin asked.

Old Zheng didn’t answer right away; he took a bite of food. In his heart, how could he not love this woman? From meeting at eighteen, when both were young and clueless, to falling in love, staying together, building their little family together — twenty years now. Zhang Jingyu was his beloved and, more than that, his family, someone who had become part of his blood, bound up with Old Zheng. Zhang Jingyu was beautiful and capable, his son clever and adorable. But in recent years Old Zheng’s career had been a wandering, unsettled thing; every so often he’d be out of a job for a few months, and after all that thrashing about he wasn’t even earning as much as his wife in the system. The bigger home and better life he had promised her when they married still seemed far out of reach. Their home was old and leaked, every wall pressed in on cramped little spaces, and with his wife’s face growing gloomier these past few years, sometimes after work Old Zheng didn’t even want to go home; he’d sit in his car in the parking lot downstairs, smoking and playing on his phone, often not going up until his wife told him to.

When he’d finished eating, Old Zheng looked up and asked Diao Kexin in turn, “Little Ke, are you married?”

Diao Kexin lifted her face and looked straight at him. “Brother Yong, do you like me?”

Old Zheng’s face went visibly flustered through all four seasons at once. He waved his hands, laughing, saying no several times over; Diao Kexin kept staring straight at him. Old Zheng said, “Actually, tonight I wanted to talk to you about the Seventh Academy work. I’ve got an idea, and I wanted to see how you’d think about it from the client’s side.”

Diao Kexin laughed and began listening earnestly as Old Zheng talked about work, putting food on his plate as she did — still the same standard routine: chin in hand, gazing, serving him food, and whenever Old Zheng got excited, a round of praise.

When dinner was over, Diao Kexin said, “Brother Yong, I haven’t had enough time with you. How about this — I’ll drive you home, then go home myself.” Zheng Zhiyong politely declined, and as they talked it through it turned out they lived in residential compounds less than a kilometer apart. “Little Ke,” Zheng Zhiyong said, “maybe there really is some fate between us.”

Little Ke smiled.

When they reached the parking lot of Old Zheng’s compound, Little Ke said, “I’ll be going, then.”

Old Zheng said, “Let me see you home.”

So Old Zheng followed Diao Kexin’s red Audi to the parking lot below her building. Diao Kexin got out of her car and went straight into Old Zheng’s passenger seat. The moment she was in, she flipped down the vanity mirror above the seat, glanced at herself, flipped it back up, turned toward Old Zheng and said, “Old Zheng, I like you. Actually, from the first time I saw you at the Western Hills villa — the moment I bumped into you — I liked you.”

Old Zheng’s body and heart both gave a faint jolt. Diao Kexin leaned over and kissed him on the lips with her whole self, lingering for a few seconds; just as Old Zheng was about to part his lips slightly, Diao Kexin was already back in her own seat.

That retreat stirred Old Zheng’s possessiveness. He shifted over and kissed Diao Kexin hard, and this time she neither dodged nor refused. It was a long, passionate kiss.

After kissing for a long while Old Zheng’s back ached, and he went back to sitting upright in the driver’s seat. He took out a cigarette and asked Diao Kexin, “Do you mind?”

Diao Kexin said, “I don’t mind. I like the smell of your smoke. I can smoke with you.”

Old Zheng handed Diao Kexin a cigarette too, and they rolled the windows all the way down and flicked their ash out onto the parking lot floor. Old Zheng asked her, “Then why at the Seventh Academy did you say you didn’t know me?”

The scene of Diao Kexin standing beside Gao Fei, tugging at his sleeve and asking who this was, leapt suddenly into Old Zheng’s mind. Diao Kexin said, “Guilty conscience, I suppose. Seeing such a handsome man, I got flustered.” She tossed the butt out the window, then twisted around and sat on Old Zheng’s lap and kissed him again.

Old Zheng stopped caring and wrapped his arms around her waist. In the cramped driver’s seat, in the pitch-black night, the two kissed without stopping.

Just as things were heating up, Diao Kexin pushed Old Zheng away, ran out of the car and slipped nimbly through the security door of her building. By the time Old Zheng had sorted out his clothes and chased after her, all he saw was the entrance door slamming shut in front of him with a heavy clang, and it never opened again.

Old Zheng hurriedly called Diao Kexin. The phone rang a long time before she answered. “Hello,” she said, her voice thick with tears. “Brother Yong, didn’t you want to know if I’d been married? Actually, I was married once, but I divorced seven years ago. Do you want to hear my story?”

So Old Zheng went back to the driver’s seat of his car, lit another cigarette, and listened quietly as she told him over the phone, unhurried, about her life: her childhood, her growing up and her terrible marriage.

Diao Kexin was born into a poor ethnic-minority family. Her parents had never been to school and couldn’t read, and to this day they still made a living selling vegetables. She had a brother four years younger; he did poorly in school and didn’t go to college after high school.

When Diao Kexin was in middle school, the journey to and from school was very long, so in eighth grade her physics teacher — who was also the school’s principal — kindly took her in, letting her come to his home for dinner every day. The teacher had a wife with a hearty, open personality and a shy, grown boy, their son, in his second year of high school, who, on the occasions when she came to dinner, was especially kind to her too. The teacher’s wife was in the building-materials business, and the family was considered very well off locally. For the first time Diao Kexin saw a sofa, a television, a washing machine in someone’s home; she ate white rice steamed in an electric rice cooker. These were things unheard of in her world.

One ordinary evening, just before the summer vacation at the end of eighth grade, Diao Kexin was about to go home to her own family. Before stepping out the door, she looked around at everything about her: the physics teacher’s sofa, his refrigerator, his color TV, the refrigerator packed with food. With a thud she dropped to her knees in front of the teacher’s family and sobbed loudly to her physics teacher, “Teacher Li, I don’t want to go home.” The fourteen-year-old girl, weeping, poured out the story of her violent father and her mother who favored sons over daughters, who made her toil all day long and wouldn’t let her study, so that every time she left to go to school she was beaten. Though this was an utterly clichéd story, commonplace among mountain girls, the teacher’s whole family was moved by her suffering. She cried, “Teacher, I want to go to school. I’m afraid to go back to my family. Please, take me as your goddaughter,” and knocked her head against the floor again and again. The teacher’s wife rushed to help her up, pulled her into her arms with an aching heart, and shot her husband a hard look, as if sending out a signal and an order. That night, the eve of her return home, fourteen-year-old Diao Kexin made the school’s principal her godfather, the principal’s wife her godmother, and the seventeen-year-old boy her godbrother. Her utterly sincere tears had bought her, in a single night, three devoted family members.

The next day the teacher’s whole family walked more than two hours to her home to bring her family money and food. They told her birth family that although they had taken their child as a goddaughter, would pay her tuition from now on, and would support her lodging in their house while she studied in town — they stressed again and again: “The child will always be your child. We’re not replacing you as her parents. We just want to support her through school at this stage, and we expect nothing in return. When she grows up, you are her only parents. She’s clever and her grades are excellent; studying hard is a way out for her. We expect nothing in return.”

From then on, with the support of the teacher’s family, Diao Kexin finished middle school, then high school, and was admitted to Kunming University. Her godparents paid all of her university tuition.

The year Diao Kexin was in eighth grade she first met this godbrother. He wasn’t tall, he was thin and taciturn, with drooping eyebrows, very small eyes set farther apart than most people’s, a flat nose with upturned nostrils, thick lips and crooked teeth. At first, whenever he saw her, he simply ignored her and went into his room. But as Diao Kexin saw more of him, she would stare straight at him with her big eyes. He had known since he was little that he was ugly, and once a lively girl had gazed at him so long and so warmly, he too began to show care, in quiet, wordless ways, for this girl who so often ate dinner at his house. He wouldn’t put food on her plate, but he’d push the dish of braised meat in front of her; when her pen broke, he’d slip a new one into her schoolbag when she wasn’t looking. Only after she became his godsister did he exchange words with her for the first time. As a high schooler he boarded at school and only came home on Fridays; sometimes, when he got home, his godsister had already gone back to her own family, and on those weekend nights when he’d missed her, the boy felt a vague sense of loss.

“He was so ugly,” Diao Kexin told Old Zheng, “but I loved him very much, because I’d never been loved by anyone. He was ugly, but he was smart and sensitive and so good to me, so I gave myself to him heart and soul. In the three years he was at university, the two of us wrote nearly a thousand letters to each other.”

The summer Diao Kexin was admitted to university, she went with her godbrother to the nearby big city. By then he’d been at university in Beijing for three years and had seen a lot; he told Diao Kexin about the world outside, bigger cities, more thoughtful people. He took her to buy clothes and shoes, wanting to dress her up like a girl from the city before she went off to university. That day, Diao Kexin herself suggested staying the night at a hotel in the city. “I’m already eighteen,” she said to him. That night, Diao Kexin offered her youth to her godbrother of her own accord, and he, like someone opening a box of rare treasure, peeled her open layer by layer.

In Diao Kexin’s first year at university, her godbrother flew to her school twice to see her. They took a room near campus, and each time they met they didn’t leave the little inn for three days and three nights, until they were too hungry to stand it and the godbrother went out to bring back some food. From then on he was madly obsessed with her and missed her day and night, so much so that before going to study in England, so they could keep in touch, he made a special trip to Kunming to set up a computer in her dorm and bought her a mobile phone, so the two of them could reach each other any time, by QQ or by email. The era of paper letters was gone for good.

The godparents were naturally pleased with all this. Seeing the girl they had watched grow up become their future daughter-in-law, they treated her even better: besides paying all her tuition, even her living allowance became generous. Her clothes and her experiences grew richer by the day, the air of poverty gradually disappeared, and the elegant bearing of higher education flourished in Diao Kexin day by day.

In Diao Kexin’s final year of university, the boy was doing a PhD overseas, and Diao Kexin said, “I want to study in England too.” The boy and his family readily agreed. He applied on her behalf for a one-year master’s program at a university in the same city as his, at a tuition of three hundred thousand yuan, and Diao Kexin flew to London and lived with young Dr. Li.

“That was the most beautiful time of my life. The scenery there was so beautiful. Sometimes we went hiking together, sometimes we lingered along the Thames in London. I wished I could stay in that beautiful city with him and never come back.”

Dr. Li came back from England with his degree and landed a respectable teaching post in his own field at Xiangnan University. He put down roots in Changsha and soon married Diao Kexin, and the university allocated the newlywed young lecturer the use of a two-bedroom flat of a little over fifty square meters, so from then on Diao Kexin had a home of her own in this strange city.

“But he cheated on me. In the fourth year of our marriage he got together with an overseas visiting scholar. I saw them kissing and holding hands. My heart broke. I loved him so much, and he treated me like that. I asked for a divorce and he refused, and later he jumped off a building at the university. It caused a huge sensation at the time.”

“He didn’t die, but he agreed to the divorce. After he got out of hospital we went through with it, and I’ve never seen him again. Not once in seven years.”

Old Zheng asked, “When was this?”

Diao Kexin answered, “Dragon Boat Festival, 2017.”

Old Zheng asked again, “On the day of the Dragon Boat Festival, 2017?”

Diao Kexin began to cry as she spoke. “Yes, that very day. I felt that at that moment, in that instant, the soul in him that loved me leapt out of his body and flew to you.”

The instant Diao Kexin said this, a flash of lightning split the sky, and a few seconds later came the rumble of thunder. Old Zheng felt as if Heaven’s lightning had struck him. He froze. That very year, at the Dragon Boat Festival, he himself had inexplicably broken his right leg. Old Zheng asked, “Was he hurt anywhere?”

Diao Kexin said, “He broke his right leg.”

Old Zheng’s heart shook. In that instant he felt that this was the woman fate had arranged for him — the woman he could not help but love.

Then Diao Kexin’s loud sobbing came through his ear. “Brother Yong, knowing all this about me, will you still love me?”

Old Zheng said firmly, “I love you, I love you, Little Ke. Please come downstairs. I want to see you.”

Diao Kexin hung up, and no matter how many times Old Zheng called back, she never answered again.

By now it was past midnight, and the torrential rain pounded loudly on the roof of Old Zheng’s car, the sound becoming deafening…
