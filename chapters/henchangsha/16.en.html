Chapter Sixteen: Huodou

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Huodou” — the fire-eating beast of old. Wherever it passes, fire springs up from within, and it burns down even the house that shelters it. As the saying goes: whoever feeds on fire is in the end fed to the fire.

This month was also the crunch period for Qingneng’s deliveries. Qingneng’s business was gradually finding its feet, and Old Zheng had to make frequent trips to Dongguan and Foshan to oversee delivery. The rest of the time he came back to his own tiny office, where the little fish on the desk were fed with loving care every day by Diao Kexin and her sister-in-law. They were bursting with life, as full of hope as the company’s future.

During this period Diao Kexin spent every moment she wasn’t at work with Old Zheng, and the two were stuck together like glue. The fake-pregnancy scare had blown over just like that. Diao Kexin explained that it might have been the morning-after pill she’d taken after one of their times together, which had delayed things by two weeks; after that everything was back to normal, and there was hardly a day the two of them didn’t live like a loving married couple.

For Old Zheng, what fascinated him most about this woman was, without doubt, exactly that. He didn’t know why he could have such harmonious, wonderful sex with her; perhaps when a man and a woman are truly drawn to each other physically, that experience of deep union is irresistible and impossible to erase. In his life it was unique, one of a kind; he had never had it in his previous relationship or in his whole married life. And he believed that no other woman would ever give him this again. So he felt he had fallen completely, obsessed with this woman — and more importantly, this woman was now his. She had left Gao Fei completely, utterly. Not only that, she had introduced Old Zheng to her brother and sister-in-law, and told the aunt who lived with her, “This is my husband.”

From now on, her whole future was bound up with him, and with him alone. During this time Diao Kexin took him to meet many of Yuanwang’s big clients from the past, and told them point-blank: “This is my fiancé, Zheng Zhiyong. Yes, we’re planning to get married soon. We’ve started a business together now — please, boss, give my husband’s business your support from now on.” She even took him back to her hometown in Guizhou to meet her parents and told them, “This is my husband.” Her parents seemed overjoyed. The pain of their daughter having an illegitimate daughter was still a wound that hadn’t healed in their hearts, and now here was a dignified middle-aged man, the right age, divorced and single — a fine relationship that fit public morals and deserved their blessing. They too hoped their daughter could have a normal romance, a normal marriage, a normal life. Diao Kexin even took him to see her primary school and her middle school, and made love to him wildly in a hotel from which they could see her old school’s sports field. She acted as if she wanted him deep inside her life, and on that trip to her hometown Old Zheng finally felt he was on solid ground.

He did still have a few worries, though. His own parents hadn’t yet accepted his cliff-edge divorce. His mother always said to him sadly that she missed her grandson terribly — couldn’t they go and see him? But that was something even Old Zheng himself could no longer manage.

Zheng Zhiyong had another worry: lately he’d been paying for virtually everything whenever he was with her, and on top of that he’d bought her quite a few gifts in this time. All he’d had in his account was the latest month’s salary from Yuanwang, so he was basically living on credit cards, and he now owed tens of thousands on them. But fortunately the company’s first project was nearly delivered and the final payment was on its way; soon over a million would come in, and then he’d suddenly have several hundred thousand in cash, and his cash crisis would be over.

That month he came and went from her home as casually as from his own, sometimes staying the night and not going back to his little rented flat at all. So the nanny, Diao Kexin’s distant aunt, gradually came to accept this future son-in-law. One day, when Diao Kexin hadn’t yet come home from work, the little girl suddenly had a bad stomachache. The aunt called Old Zheng, and he was there within minutes and took the child to hospital — acute gastroenteritis. Luckily he’d gotten her there in time; she was admitted and put on a drip, and within a few days she had recovered.

Old Zheng felt this was the closest bond in the world: husband and wife, and comrades-in-arms besides. The only thing was that during this time Diao Kexin still often said to him, after they’d made love or at some especially sweet moment, in a slow, wistful voice, “Brother Yong, if — I’m only saying if — one day I go away, will you come and find me and bring me back?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Wherever you go, I’ll bring you back.”

Diao Kexin said, “Remember what you said. Wherever I go, you have to bring me back.”

Zheng Zhiyong took it as the bittersweet tang that comes with sweet times, and never gave it a second thought.

One morning Diao Kexin was going into Yuanwang’s Beijing office to show her face, and Zheng Zhiyong was meeting a client at his own office. They’d agreed to meet at the office at noon, have lunch together and then talk over how to go after their next project. But he waited and waited, and by after one in the afternoon Diao Kexin still hadn’t come. Zheng Zhiyong sent her WeChats — no reply. He called — her phone was off.

Unable to reach her, Old Zheng felt a flicker of unease. Every little while he messaged her, asked her aunt and her sister-in-law; nobody knew where she’d gone. Zheng Zhiyong had a feeling she’d gone to Changsha. But why wouldn’t she tell him?

Even into the evening he couldn’t reach her, and Zheng Zhiyong waited anxiously at the office. The office was full of traces this woman had left: her makeup bag, her compact mirror, a half-drunk bottle of water with her lipstick on it, a pair of her black wool stockings — left there a few days before, after a passionate exchange with Old Zheng.

And the school of little red fish swimming merrily in the tank on the desk.

At ten that night Zheng Zhiyong was still at the office. He’d work for a while, then call Diao Kexin, but the call never went through.

Then his phone rang, and the name flashing on the screen was “Gao Fei.”

Zheng Zhiyong felt a wave of unease. He answered. “Hello, Chairman.”

“Zheng Zhiyong, it’s me.” It was Diao Kexin’s voice on the line, trembling.

“I want to tell you: Gao Fei’s divorced. You and I — it’s over. Zheng Zhiyong, I’m sorry.”

“What do you mean, Diao Kexin? What do you mean?” Zheng Zhiyong demanded, nearly screaming himself hoarse.

But she had already hung up.

Zheng Zhiyong immediately called Diao Kexin’s number back; it was still switched off.

Then he called Gao Fei’s number back. “Hello?”

“Hello.” Still Diao Kexin’s voice.

“Brother Yong, I’m sorry. From now on, let’s have no contact of any kind. I mean any — none at all.”

“You love him, don’t you?” Zheng Zhiyong asked.

“Yes.” A firm, final answer from the other end. And with that she hung up.

Zheng Zhiyong could hardly stand. It was like a thunderbolt out of a clear sky.

He held his breath and steadied himself for a moment, then called her phone again: switched off. He sent a WeChat: he’d been deleted. Douban, NetEase Music — every app they had ever used to talk had blocked him, every single one.

He felt that if the sky had fallen, it could be no worse than this. The whole world went dark and lightless. He slumped to the office floor, a grown man, and in an instant his face was streaming with tears.

At last he understood that this woman had used him — used his very existence to gamble on whether it would rouse the chairman’s urge to compete. If she lost the bet, she’d still have a man devoted to her heart and soul; if she won, she’d get the chairman’s-wife position she had fought for so bitterly for eight years.

She had won.

A billion-yuan fortune — the thing she had dreamed of — she had gotten it.

Everything that had tied her down would be tossed behind her without a shred of attachment or guilt.

His hatred for her came surging up, making his blood boil, making him clench his fists; he smashed them hard against the floor until his fingers were bloody.

Only at that moment did he suddenly realize he had lost everything. Just a few months ago he’d had a wife who loved him, a child; he’d had parents who loved him; he’d had a home, a car, millions in savings; that warm home, small as it was, where everyone was kind and generous. His wife might nag him a little now and then, but at every crucial moment she had never once failed to back his decisions. And where was she now?

His own child — that beautiful little boy with big eyes, a high nose, skin as white as snow, long arms and long legs, who every morning before school would sweetly say, “Bye, Daddy!” — where was he now?

That company called Hanhai Chenxing had been a good company; this company called Yuanwang had been a good company too. And that smiling boss’s wife, to show how much she valued him, had told him: I’ll give you one percent of the company, one percent sales commission, and make you CEO. That steady monthly income, that million-yuan income within easy reach — where was it all now?

Now, apart from six figures of credit card debt, he had nothing.

In the dark night, apart from weeping his heart out, there was nothing at all he could do.
