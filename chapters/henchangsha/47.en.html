Yu

Chapter Forty-Seven: Yu

Translation first published: 2026-11-11 00:55 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-11 00:55 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Yu” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Northern Mountains”), there is a bird shaped like the kuafu ape but covered in boar bristles; its cry is like a call, and when it appears the whole world is flooded. A great bird without strength, it lives only by clinging to something else.

Once Wu Peng's identity came to light, he began driving himself to and from work. His car was a dark grey Tesla Model Y with black wheels, still showroom-new. Before that he had only now and then taken the company car to fetch clients; the rest of the time he rode a white Niu electric scooter. Now he drove the new grey Tesla every day, and before long everyone noticed that on the commute Shasha was coming and going with him.

Shasha lived in a residential district two kilometres from the office, so picking her up and taking her home was perfectly on Wu Peng's way. These days the two often went to see clients together: every morning he collected her, they made their rounds, came back to the office together, and in the evening she rode home with him. Sometimes they stayed at a client's for a whole day; on the way back they would have dinner and go over whom they were to see the next day and what to say, and then he would drive her home. The car played the music Wu Peng chose — and, by coincidence, it was exactly what Shasha liked: Lisa Ono and Keren Ann. Shasha leaned back in the passenger seat, heels off, feet tucked up on the seat, dozing sometimes when she was tired. Wu Peng said nothing; now and then he glanced over and turned the air-conditioning vent away from her.

Her card said Sales Director, and Wu Peng was nominally the general manager, but when the two were together there was none of the sense of rank that hung about Old Zheng and Shasha in public; it was entirely the feeling of partners. Several times, at dinners with clients, Wu Peng took the drinks meant for her — treatment Shasha had never had in all her time with Zheng Zhiyong. Once they waited more than an hour at a client's because the other side changed the time at the last minute; Shasha paced the corridor, and Wu Peng went downstairs, bought two coffees and handed her the one with no sugar and no milk. “Have some,” he said. “Don't wait on an empty stomach.” She took it, looked at him and asked, “How did you know I take it without sugar or milk?” Wu Peng said, “I've known for a long time.”

Later, after the trip to the Xi'an branch of the Fifth Academy of Aerospace, they came out of the building into a sky already dark, with a hard wind blowing. Shasha's heel caught in a gap between the paving stones and she lurched forward; Wu Peng caught her arm and kept her from falling. She straightened, said “I'm fine”, and walked on. At the car park he did not open the door at once, but went to the boot, rummaged, and brought out a pair of slippers folded neatly, and set them at her feet. Shasha looked at the shoes for two seconds, then looked up at him.

“He actually keeps a pair of slippers in the car.” At that moment something in Shasha was glad. That was Wu Peng: a kindness that moistened things without a sound. Everyone in the office got on well with him, and Shasha knew that this gentle man was especially attentive to her. She bent to change her shoes, the heels in her hand. Once in the car she slipped the slippers off again, rested both feet on them, and turned to look at his profile. It was the profile of a twenty-six-year-old boy: a lean, sharp jaw, a prominent Adam's apple — so unlike the middle-aged, oily clients she was used to, and full of the feeling of youth.

Something in Shasha began to loosen on that evening. She had been with Old Zheng for more than a year, and he had never driven her to her own home, never played the music she liked, never once asked whether her feet hurt.

Zhang Hailu, too, was gradually coming to understand the newly appointed Wu Peng. On the surface nothing had changed in the way he got on with Old Zheng or with Wu Peng. But after Wu Peng took office, he felt that this deputy general manager's style was altogether different from Zheng Zhiyong's. When the two of them went to the academy to coordinate a project, he still did the technical talking at the meeting; Wu Peng never cut in, quietly backed every one of his points and gave him the credit for all the company's technical work. But on business occasions where Zhang Hailu was not needed, Wu Peng took all that entertaining off his hands, so Hailu's work in substance was much lighter than it had been when he played second fiddle to Zheng Zhiyong. Wu Peng had gone from assistant to deputy, and yet the way he addressed Zhang Hailu, the courtesy when they met, the daily consideration and care, had all increased rather than lessened. And Zhang Hailu, for his part, was thoroughly tractable: he cooperated where cooperation was due, and reported where a report was due.

But Zhang Hailu's worries were growing heavier, too. The more Wu Peng stood on his own feet, the more plainly one could see that Zheng Zhiyong's heart was elsewhere. The Beijing projects were winding down or running smoothly now, so there was no longer any need to keep going up to Beijing with Old Zheng to tend the business, and the two had talked much less of late. Hailu feared that Zheng Zhiyong's position was no longer secure, yet could not find a chance to talk the worry through with him properly. The company's business in this period centred on Xi'an, and Zheng Zhiyong had not appeared at the office for a good while; nobody knew where he had gone. The equity had been left hanging from the very day they joined Xinghuan — it was promised as soon as the project landed, and the Sixth Academy project had been won, with ten million in revenue booked, yet the payout kept being pushed back, and now it looked very likely it would never come at all. Two years of year-end bonuses had come to precious little in hand. His wife and child were in Xiangtan; he rented alone in Xi'an, and every month the rent and the train tickets back and forth were a sum in themselves. The few brothers who had followed him here had all taken pay cuts to come, and they did not stint on their complaints; lately, with Old Zheng in trouble and still nowhere to be seen, some had come to Hailu privately to say they were afraid the pie they had been promised would be hard to deliver, and with the loneliness of long absence from home growing day by day, a few were beginning to think of going back to Xiangcheng.

Zhang Hailu had met with the same thing himself. One evening his wife called to say the child had a fever. In the past he could only have left it at a few words of instruction to his wife and to his parents at home, head and tail both unattended; this time he asked Zheng Zhiyong and Wu Peng for leave the next day, bought a train ticket and prepared to go home that same day. After all, since coming to Xinghuan he had worked overtime with the technical team on bids and on deliveries, and had taken almost no annual leave; he might as well go and see the child and take a few days. He had not seen Zheng Zhiyong for several days either. Leaving Xi'an, he felt he had every right to it.

On the second day after Hailu went home, Zhao Bin, whom he had not heard from in a long while, actually telephoned. Hailu had long since learned from Shasha the rumours about Diao Kexin, and knew moreover that she had taken Yuanwang over completely as its new actual controller. On this call he asked Zhao Bin in detail about Yuanwang and Diao Kexin, then the two old colleagues talked properly, catching each other up on how things stood. He also heard that Diao Kexin had got ten million out of Wang Yan, and with the money had hired back some of the technical colleagues who had left and made a fresh start; not only had she recently won another project in Beijing, she was preparing to bid for one in Xi'an. Zhang Hailu felt a deep astonishment. This woman was indeed no ordinary person.

That evening, with Zhao Bin brokering it, Diao Kexin learned of Zhang Hailu's circumstances and decided to go to Xiangtan to see him in person. Hailu felt at first that the meeting was not quite proper, afraid the company would hear of it and think ill of him; but he was deeply curious about how Diao Kexin had walked away unscathed from the detention centre the year before, and how she had got a sum of money out of her rival Wang Yan to bring the company back to life. So after a moment's hesitation he decided to see her.

“Yuanwang is going through liquidation and restructuring, but it will certainly get back on its feet,” Diao Kexin said. “Gao Fei is inside now. His shares were auctioned and went entirely to the local urban investment company, but the company's valuation today is absurdly low — that money isn't even enough to pay the bank. The earlier state investment was provided for in the liquidation settlement, so the company's debts are capped; once that is dealt with, it's finished. The good thing now is that Zhao Bin and the others are still delivering several long-standing deployed projects, which still bring in more than twenty million in gross profit a year; after all the staff costs, there is a surplus.”

“Oh — and the Yuanwang park has been sold too. The first auction failed. Later Zhu Yiwen put down forty million and took the park that was worth two hundred million. The sign on the Xiang River has long since been changed to Gaoda Group. Now Yuanwang is down to the old factory and Zhao Bin's seven or eight people. The park is gone; the good part is that the expense is gone too. Keeping that park used to mean keeping a good many extra people, and the annual costs were frightening.”

Zhang Hailu said, “I heard Sister Yan gave you ten million to revive the company. Is that true?”

Diao Kexin smiled. “How to put it. That money of Sister Yan's was the share-transfer money from her divorce from Gao Fei. The two had an agreement, notarised, but Gao Fei never actually paid it, so it became her claim against him. She had long since sued the company and Gao Fei together, and had more than twenty million of the company's cash frozen. After the company talked it over with its creditors, Wang Yan agreed to halve it and take only ten million — and she gave me two years as well, to repay within twenty-four months. So in effect she gave the company rather more than ten million. Without Wang Yan's concession, this company could never have been saved.”

“So that's how it was,” said Zhang Hailu. “Then you and Wang Yan have joined hands now, running Yuanwang together?”

“No,” said Diao Kexin. “Wang Yan's partner now is Zhu Yiwen. I hear Sister Yan's registration at Gaoda has already been changed, and she holds five per cent. On our projects in Beijing and Xi'an the competitor is Gaoda in both cases; many of Yuanwang's old hands have gone over to Gaoda, and took a good many of the earlier project leads with them. Gaoda has gone straight from a partner we worked with to a rival bidder.”

He had not imagined there could be so many changes. For a moment Zhang Hailu was dumb with surprise.

Diao Kexin looked at him and said, “Brother Hailu, we've known each other for many years. I know your technical strength; I know you haven't earned much at Xinghuan and you're never at home. I've come this time to ask you to help Yuanwang. Come back to Yuanwang. I'm very confident of winning the Beijing project this time, but Zhao Bin and I are not confident we can deliver it. If you and a few of the brothers come back to us, Zhao Bin and I will both have the confidence.”

Zhang Hailu smiled, a little bitterly. “I'm at Xinghuan now, working for Zheng Zhiyong. If I came back to work for you, the relationship really would be awkward.”

“Brother Hailu, I know Zheng Zhiyong. He isn't suited to the CEO's chair. His real strength is technical delivery itself; handling people, running projects, counting money — those are all weak points of his. And he has no luck with money. You must know that yourself: in these two years with him, quite apart from leaving your family and your work behind, did you actually earn anything?” Diao Kexin said. “Brother Hailu, come to me. The company is a forty-minute drive from your own home. I'll pay you fifty thousand a month, and when the project is won, ten per cent of the project revenue goes to the team to share. Whether it goes well or badly, your income will be a good deal higher than before. The risk is all on me; you carry none — and you can go home.”

Zhang Hailu said nothing more. Outside the window a boat went past, and the river darkened. After a long while he said, “Let me think about it.”
