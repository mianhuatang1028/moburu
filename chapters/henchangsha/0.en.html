Prologue: Kunpeng

Translation first published: 2026-11-09 22:57 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) Last updated: 2026-11-10 09:40 © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Kunpeng” — In the Zhuangzi (“Free and Easy Wandering”), there is a fish in the Northern Darkness that turns into a bird; it strikes the water for three thousand li and rides the whirlwind ninety thousand li into the sky. The same leap — some go up, some go down. As the saying goes: in a single leap, the one who rises becomes the Peng; the one who falls is never named.

Li Fulang finally stood at the railing on the roof of Building A of the School of Aeronautics and Astronautics, seven floors up, facing the back of the teaching block.

The whole building had been set against the front slope of a small hill. This was Xiangnan University, a nationally ranked “985” university in Changsha. Below him lay a sea of green forest; the passion of summer had dyed the thickets on this side of the hill an even deeper, more distant green. He wanted, like a warrior and like a lover, to look down one last time on the colors of this mortal world. Thirty-two years — the first half of his life flashed past his eyes like light skimming over water,

and then, without a trace of reluctance, he leapt…

He was born into a teacher’s family at the only key middle school in the county town of Rongjiang, Guizhou. His father was the principal of that school and also taught physics; his mother was from the Northeast, had come to southeastern Guizhou in her youth to support the frontier regions, later met her clever husband, put down roots there as well, and ran a lumber mill. Though he had inherited neither his mother’s emotional intelligence nor her refined, generous features, from his father he had inherited an extraordinarily brilliant mind and a goodness without equal. From childhood he showed an astonishing gift for mathematics and physics. Without question he was someone at once exceedingly brilliant and exceedingly naive. From high school on he had set his heart on crewed spaceflight and space technology, and his parents were delighted and supported him all the way. He got into Beiqing University, then went on to the space technology institute at Imperial College London for his master’s and PhD. But he was also extremely inarticulate. He was afraid of speaking in public, disliked socializing, and disliked baring his inner world to anyone.

None of this hurt his academic achievements in the least, and, curiously, his English soon outstripped his Chinese; he was adept at publishing his findings about the physical world in international journals, in English. Three years ago, after his PhD, he had come back to China to join this university as a young lecturer, and four months ago he had been promoted to associate professor in Xiangnan University’s School of Aeronautics and Astronautics.

He had a wife as lovely as a flower, a beautiful wife he had known since high school; she had been his father’s student. Beneath his plain, even slightly ugly exterior, this girl had been able to love his inner world and become his incomparable beloved, the only partner in the world to whom he could open his heart. Two months ago his wife had suddenly asked him for a divorce. The reason: she believed he was cheating. When she saw him having a meal with a female visiting scholar from the university, she burst into the restaurant, weeping loudly and accusing him, and drew a crowd of onlookers. Then she went to his school office and made a huge scene there too, demanding that he give her justice. She said: you’ve cheated on me with someone else, I’ve been yours since I was a girl, and now you’ve betrayed me, I want a divorce, you owe me for my fourteen years, I never want to see you again in this life or any other.

Do people of plain appearance not deserve a beautiful wife and children of their own? He missed his wife — that body warm as jade, those high breasts, that half-inviting, half-refusing look, that soft, bashful voice. She had given herself to him since she was eighteen; in bed she had always been a bride as gentle as water. In their four years of marriage they had not had a child of their own. He and his parents had been looking forward to the arrival of the next generation, but he had not yet had the chance to become a father. He could not voice his heartbreak, and there was no one to voice it to. At this moment he felt that the stretch of mountain forest before him might as well be his final resting place. He did not want a divorce, and at the same time he was desperate to prove to his wife that he had not cheated, that he was faithful. He wanted to say to her: I will love you forever; even at the moment of my death, I still love you.

His resolve to leap shot out of him like a launched missile…
