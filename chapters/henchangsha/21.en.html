Lingyu

Chapter Twenty-One: Lingyu

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Lingyu” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Northern Lands Within the Seas”), the lingyu has a human face, hands and feet, and a fish’s body, and lives in the sea. Half on the shore, half in the water, it can stand on either side. As the saying goes: a human face on a fish’s body — at home in both places.

Gao Fei restarted the signing of the capital-increase agreement, and Sister Yan’er wouldn’t agree. She and Gao Fei were deadlocked.

A few months earlier the letter of intent for the investment had been lying on the desks in her and Gao Fei’s offices, and back then the two of them had already reached an agreement once: no fundraising. Because she knew just how inflated, how all-show-and-no-substance that 1.5-billion valuation was for the company at that point. The money would bring cash, and along with it enormous risk. The company’s revenue barely reached 100 million; because they’d put a lot more into R&D in recent years, to pay off over the next few years, profit was about 20 million — but of that, only about forty percent of the business could be expected to hold up and keep growing over the coming years. The products they were developing still carried huge uncertainty, and this year’s surge in business owed something to personal connections and lucky breaks. Whether breaks like that could continue in the future, Sister Yan’er felt, was completely unpredictable. Especially in their line, the defense industry, where research institutes and the military units they paired with went through a reshuffle of personnel every few years — that was the iron law of the whole trade. Eight years: in that time they’d face a great many changes and uncertainties, and any one of them would be more than he could bear. If, when the time came, there really was no way to IPO, they’d have to pay 12 percent a year in buyback interest — and once the business slid, where would they find the money to pay it? When that day came, this agreement would bring them a disaster from which there’d be no coming back.

But this time Gao Fei seemed very insistent.

“The fact is, the way our company is now, it’s comfortable and flexible. We don’t take investment, we’ve got twenty or thirty million in profit a year, a hundred or so employees. Sure, it’s a mom-and-pop shop, but the core team is stable and it’s not hard to manage. Once we take investors’ money, everything changes. Then, if the business doesn’t grow or it shrinks, there’s no coming back from it.”

No matter what Sister Yan’er said, Gao Fei wanted to get this round of funding done. Right now he was soaring in the world of love, had turned into an eagle in the sky, and he had his own reasons. If the company’s valuation went up, cutting his share of the property away from Sister Yan’er’s would get a lot easier, and he’d still be able to keep most of the company’s equity for himself. And ever since that girl had learned the company’s valuation had reached the number 1.5 billion, it was as if a billion-yuan dream had been planted in her little head. On the days and nights they so often spent tumbling together, the young girl spurred Gao Fei on with her praise and worship: Yuanwang has to look further, and you, Gao Fei, have to fly higher. You can’t be content with a family business; you have to become the boss of a listed company. You’ve got not just the nerve but the ability to do it. And I’ll be the woman standing behind you, using my brilliant gift for sales to take on clients for you, hit your numbers, and make your dream of founding a listed company come true.

Half understanding, hazy, muddled: the valuation-adjustment bet in the funding agreement was an enormous latent risk, and that enormous, uncertain risk seemed to have been erased — whether deliberately or without anyone meaning to.

But there was a gentle, lovely girl looking up at him all day long, waiting for him. By now she had taken care of the divorce agreement for him; she was entirely his woman. Right now she was carrying his — Gao Fei’s — second son, and, strangely, now that she was into the second trimester, the woman’s appetite hadn’t dimmed one bit compared with before. Gao Fei had bought Diao Kexin a lavish, sprawling flat in Beijing and settled her into a new office and a new position. Right now Gao Fei wanted to use a bigger world to balance the two women in his life and their children, so as to find some peace in his own heart.

As for what would happen eight years after taking the 200 million, he couldn’t worry about all that anymore. He signed the investment agreement. The future — the future could wait.

Sister Yan’er had always carried a huge sense of unease about the fundraising. Because the money from the capital increase wasn’t actually needed for running the company — the R&D costs and the rest of their original plans were already covered — it went mainly into expanding the company’s ranks, and expanding the sales force in a more aggressive way, to chase faster growth. In the second year after the money came in, the company had grown from a hundred people to more than five hundred. Very luckily, that year their business rose with the tide too, and profit was even richer than the year before. The cash flow on the books was ample, the earnings outlook was clear, and the raised money was lying in the bank earning interest. Sister Yan’er talked it over with Gao Fei, worked their government relationships, and, using the company’s sky-high influence, got land for an industrial park at a cost so low it hardly counted. Then, with bank loans and leverage, they built a dazzling Yuanwang Technology Industrial Park on the bank of the Xiang River. The whole park cost less than 150 million to build, but once it was finished, its property value would be over 300 million.

Sister Yan’er understood that once the park was built, the buildings and land could be mortgaged. If, eight years on, the bet really did go against them, they would still have very valuable fixed assets, and those assets held enormous potential to appreciate. So the year after they got the 200-million capital increase, they started building the park, with Sister Yan’er’s sister-in-law, her brother’s wife, mainly in charge of the work.

But Gao Fei had his troubles. Diao Kexin’s child was born that year in the depths of winter, and he went back on his word. He couldn’t get divorced. The company’s funding and the growth of its business were all deeply tied up with Sister Yan’er. If he divorced now, the technical team Zhang Hailu ran would fall apart, the production and delivery her brother handled would go wrong, the investor relations and government relations Sister Yan’er handled herself would leave everyone’s jaw on the floor, and the lovely investment story of the professor couple would be smashed to pieces. So Gao Fei never had the slightest way of bringing up the idea of divorce. If anything, he and his wife got on even better. The company the two of them had built with their own hands had entered a period of healthy, rapid growth on every front — capital, business, industry — and all this gave them both a tremendous sense of achievement and joy in their work. Professor Gao himself knew in his heart that he still felt guilty toward his first wife, and when they were together there was a lot more tenderness than before. He never found a chance to bring up the business with Diao Kexin, and so it dragged on day after day, buried deep in his heart — until, when the child was a year and a bit old, Diao Kexin sent Gao Fei a video of the child, and Sister Yan’er saw it, plain as day. In the video, the little girl was calling Gao Fei, “Daddy, Daddy.”

Sister Yan’er felt her world collapse in an instant. She had known Gao Fei since university. Gao Fei, tall and handsome, the dashing PhD student; a campus romance; jobs at the same university after graduation; marriage soon after; working hard for titles and promotions; giving up the golden rice bowl to come out and run the company so it could grow. Their child was still in primary school, right at the age when he needed his parents’ attention, and because both parents were running the company he’d been sent to boarding school and only got to be with them on weekends. But at least the company was thriving. A few years earlier Gao Fei’s parents had passed away one after the other, and Sister Yan’er had arranged the old people’s funerals, dressed in mourning white, and helped him see both his parents off. A home like this, a spouse like this — they had long since stopped being lovers; they were more like friends, family, flesh of each other’s flesh. Divorce was definitely out of the question. But the thing had already happened. What Sister Yan’er thought about most was the business, the property and the child. After the pain had settled she reflected on it hard, and after several days of struggling with herself, decided to keep things as they were and never divorce. But Sister Yan’er set up an offshore trust and began, year by year, moving part of the wealth they’d built up into it. The sole beneficiary was her son, still in primary school.

Seven years went by. The Yuanwang park was now completely finished, standing on the bank of the Xiang River. Yuanwang Tower in the park was sixteen stories high and stood there in grand style. Gao Fei’s eagle had spread its wings and was soaring over the Xiang. But the lady of this park was still the company’s most central manager and had never once lost her grip on the company.

But when a great tower is going to fall, it often takes only an instant. And there are sharp-eyed people who sense the danger well in advance. One of them was Zhang Hailu. Two years earlier, Zhang Hailu had learned that a leader at the company’s most important client had gotten into trouble, and right after that, a whole swath of people connected to that leader were rounded up, nearly all of them.

Zhang Hailu was one of the company’s founding veterans, yet the company had allotted him only 0.5 percent of the equity. The figure wasn’t high, but Zhang Hailu really didn’t understand much about options, so he hadn’t paid it much mind. Then, two years ago, he happened to learn that his stake was smaller even than Diao Kexin’s, and for a while he felt he’d been made a fool of. So the two things together — Zhang Hailu, the man who held the company’s most core technology, chose to resign without a backward glance and went to be CTO at a tech company founded by another of his Xiangnan University classmates. Now he was sitting face to face with the COO who had come to Yuanwang after him.

He said to Zheng Zhiyong, “The truth is, the signs of Yuanwang’s crisis were already there when I left. The biggest problem was that once the top leader at the old client was replaced, if you wanted to keep going in that business you needed better technical iteration and a more systematic company setup to win bids — and more important, the ability to productize, instead of making money one project at a time. After I left, the company basically had no technical strength to speak of. The productization and commercialization they’d started got only halfway and then stopped. Two years ago that company turned into a resources company with nothing left but sales. So I chose to leave, and looking at it now, I was completely right.”

Zheng Zhiyong understood deep down that what Zhang Hailu said was true. Zhao Bin, who came later as Zhang Hailu’s successor at the company, really didn’t have a strong team, and that was why Zheng Zhiyong’s arrival had again and again solved huge problems for the company, and why he’d won the bosses’ appreciation and been given so much responsibility. Originally, with him on board — especially if he could become the company’s CEO — he could have organized a group of outside developers to help Yuanwang quickly make up its technical shortfall and then iterate its way to a product line it could productize. The company had a decent foundation; it would still have had a very good chance. But once Diao Kexin turned Zheng Zhiyong into her own personal CTO and did her best to steer all the company’s client resources to him, Yuanwang’s sales network ran into huge problems too.

The company’s sales director was drumming up business for her own fiancé, competing directly with her own company. A lot of clients said yes to helping on the surface but privately felt something had gone wrong at the company, and came to distrust both companies. And among them, someone told Gao Fei that Diao Kexin had gotten the company’s COO to start a new company with her, and was coming to the clients to poach business opportunities.

And that was something Gao Fei could absolutely not accept.

So Gao Fei told Sister Yan’er about it, and Sister Yan’er told Gao Fei that Diao Kexin had become Zheng Zhiyong’s girlfriend as much as half a year earlier. So Gao Fei and Sister Yan’er had to talk it over and take the whole present situation into account. Diao Kexin teaming up with Zheng Zhiyong as their competitor was flatly unacceptable too; it would be one of the biggest potential rivals and risks the company faced, now and in the future.

At that point husband and wife had just one shared goal: save the company, protect the assets. To begin with, from the outside the company still looked glossy and splendid, but in fact it was in a very serious state. Because the top leader at their biggest client had been taken in for party disciplinary investigation two years earlier, the company’s accounts receivable now totaled 200 million yuan, and that money couldn’t be collected. So even though the company was profitable on paper, there was no money to use. Because the company had built the industrial park, it had more than 100 million yuan in bank loans secured on the park, with hefty interest to pay every month. The company now had 530 employees in total, and every month there were enormous sales expenses and staff costs to pay out. That was why this year ordinary salespeople and business staff were earning less than in years past, and why people like Cong Shasha, who felt their pay dropping, chose to resign. But there was one thing that was deadliest of all: the 200-million capital increase they’d taken back then had now reached the end of its seven-year investment period, and there was no longer any hope of the company going public. So under the agreement, the 200 million in investment had turned into a buyback debt of close to 400 million.

The upshot of their talks was this. Sister Yan’er would take all the cash assets in the couple’s names, including the dividends and returns they’d built up over the years — about 100 million in cash and several properties — and leave Gao Fei one property to live in. Once all the financial matters were dealt with, the two of them would get divorced. After that, Gao Fei would buy all the equity in Sister Yan’er’s name, in the form of a loan left unpaid. By the agreement, Sister Yan’er had in fact withdrawn as a Yuanwang shareholder, but they wouldn’t register the change with the authorities, and on the surface she would keep her position at the company.

Once all of the above was done, Gao Fei called Diao Kexin and told her to drop everything and fly straight to Changsha to see him. They met in a suite at a luxury five-star hotel. Gao Fei told her he had finished divorcing Sister Yan’er, that apart from the portion in the investors’ hands, all of the company’s equity, one hundred percent, was now in his name, and that he knew Diao Kexin’s boyfriend these past few months was Zheng Zhiyong, who had already left. He said, “It’s all my fault that you chose him. Now that I’m divorced, I can finally marry you and give you and our child a home.”

After all these years, Diao Kexin suddenly had the outcome she’d wanted, and she felt dazed. Gao Fei came over, held her, kissed her, took off her clothes and made love to her on the enormous, luxurious bed. It felt to both of them just as it had for the past seven years — still tender, still sweet, impossible to stop.

When at last they stopped this time, Gao Fei said to Diao Kexin, “I know you’re running a company with Zheng Zhiyong. Stop doing it with him. Come back and run Yuanwang with me. From now on, you’re the boss’s wife at Yuanwang.”

He handed Diao Kexin his phone and said to her, “Call him and tell him you’re marrying me.”
