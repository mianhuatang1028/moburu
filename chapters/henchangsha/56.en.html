Chapter Fifty-Six: Huan

Translation first published: 2026-11-19 23:23 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-19 23:23 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Huan” — in the Classic of Mountains and Seas, the Western Mountains, on Mount Yiwang there is a beast shaped like a wildcat, with one eye and three tails, named Huan; its cry is like the seizing of a hundred voices, and it can ward off evil. Truly, one eye and three tails, a hundred voices contending — if a person looks at another with only one eye, what she sees may be no more than the projection of her own past; and when a hundred voices come pressing in, the voice that truly belongs to the person before her is all the more easily drowned out.

When word came back from the man Wu Peng had sent to keep watch in Xiangcheng, Zheng Zhiyong waited no longer; the next day he bought a ticket and flew straight to Xiangcheng.

He gave no one advance notice, nor did he follow the usual route of a business call; instead he drove straight to the building — once the Yuanwang tower, its top floor now bearing the characters for Gaoda — and parked opposite it. The winter wind poured through the gaps in the car windows, letting out a thin, sharp whistle. Old Zheng had rented an unremarkable white BYD Qin at the airport, and now sat in the driver's seat, smoking one cigarette after another, his eyes fixed hard on the revolving door and the side entrance.

A little after six in the afternoon, Zhang Hailu duly came out; and hard on his heels Diao Kexin emerged from the other side, a bag in her hand, her composure such that she might have been taking a stroll in her own back garden.

Zheng Zhiyong pushed open the car door and strode over.

The three met head-on in the winter wind of Xiangcheng. No greetings, no courtesies. The moment Zhang Hailu set eyes on Zheng Zhiyong his gaze noticeably faltered; his lips moved, and in the end he braced himself and came forward. “Mr. Zheng...”

“Hailu, Gaoda is our partner, you know. It's year-end; I've come to pay a call.” Zheng Zhiyong's voice was not loud, but it carried a cold edge long held in check.

Zhang Hailu hung his head and said nothing.

Zheng Zhiyong turned his head, and his gaze scraped toward Diao Kexin like a knife.

Diao Kexin stood where she was, the corner of her lips lifting in a faint, almost pitying smile. “So you've come to see Mr. Zhu, Mr. Zheng. Shall I ask him for you whether he knows you're here already?” She paused, and added lightly: “Though, Mr. Zheng, Xinghuan surely isn't working with Mr. Zhu any longer — oh, and by the way, there's no new business over at Xinghuan either, is there? You haven't come to see Mr. Zhu to look for a job, have you?”

The sarcasm landed like a dull, heavy blow to the chest, on Zheng Zhiyong.

He stared at the Diao Kexin before him. She really did take out her phone and begin composing a WeChat message, her expression so composed and cold that it struck him as strange. For a long time past, the Diao Kexin he had seen was always a woman who would stop at nothing to climb, to tear at the chips; later he had seen her too in her pitiable state, on the brink of prison, with not a soul to turn to. But the Diao Kexin standing now on the street in Xiangcheng, her face steady to the point of cruelty, was no longer the woman eager to attach herself to anyone. She had found her footing; resources, channels, the whole hand of cards were in her grasp. She had become the player and the power itself.

“Xiaoke, let's have a word alone.” Zheng Zhiyong forced down the turmoil in his chest and softened his tone somewhat. “Let's speak plainly. Whatever you're playing at, let's compete honourably, in the open. Don't stoop to these underhand tricks — pulling the rug out from behind, poaching people, draining the foundation out from under. It does no one any good. Fair competition, all right?”

Diao Kexin did not so much as glance at him, and refused outright. “Whatever do you mean, Mr. Zheng? Hailu is from Xiangcheng. He resigned from your company and has come to his old employer to renew old acquaintance and look for business opportunities — what's wrong with that?”

Seeing the air frozen to its limit, Zhang Hailu stood by, caught between advancing and retreating. He looked at Zheng Zhiyong, then at Diao Kexin, and said quietly, taking the hint: “Mr. Zheng, Director Diao is right — I've only come to renew old ties and look for opportunities. I have other business, so I'll... I'll be going.” With that, he actually turned and walked briskly away.

A few minutes later a black Maybach slid to the kerb from within the Gaoda compound. The door swung open and Zhu Yiwen stepped out, laid a hand naturally at the small of Diao Kexin's back, then opened the car door for her and said something in a low voice, helping her bend and get in. Zhu Yiwen then entered by the other rear door. The car, indicator blinking, swept imperiously past Old Zheng, then merged smoothly into the traffic and was gone.

Watching the tail-lights vanish around the corner, Zheng Zhiyong stood rooted to the spot, a bitter, rusty taste rising in his throat. He knew this understanding, this bearing, only too well — Diao Kexin was still, as ever, binding herself to a patron by the most — her — ready and most secret of means; in truth she had once more become Zhu Yiwen's mistress.

“No more hatred” was what Zheng Zhiyong had once said to Diao Kexin in her time of decline, back when he was riding high on a wave of success. But at this moment the woman, with the most — the same — stroke, shattered Zheng Zhiyong at a single blow — once again, and this time from a posture yet more loftily above him.

How could he not hate her?

The hatred now was not like fire, but rather like a damp cold seeping out from between the bones. Old Zheng stood on the winter street of Xiangcheng, the wind pouring down through the gaps of the Gaoda building and soaking him to the skin. He thought: a man's life, it turns out, can be stabbed twice by the same person, with the same knife, in the same place. The first time he had accepted it, feeling he owed her, that it was a calamity fated for him; he had even found excuses for her — said she'd had a hard time, said she'd clawed her way out of a small place, said that bit of ambition of hers was only a wish to live like a human being. But the second time he had not even the strength to find excuses. The worst thing in this world is not hating someone until your teeth itch; it is that, even as you hate her, your mind holds with perfect clarity the memory of that face — her astride your lap, tears brimming, saying “this is the moment I love you most.” Hatred and love stirred together, like a bowl of sour soup gone bad — disgusting to the smell, and yet you remember precisely the steam rising from it when it was first set on the table.

So those three words, “no more hatred,” are the most expensive sentence in the world. He had said them lightly at the time because she was down, because he stood high, because he thought he could at last look down on her — to look down on a woman who had once trampled him underfoot was a feeling more gratifying than anything. But only today did he savour the truth: that “no more hatred” of his was no forgiveness at all — it was a handout from on high, the mercy of a victor. And such things as handouts and mercy require the other party to accept them. She did not accept; she flung them straight back in his face, and with them a certain Zhu Yiwen — and that old fox Zhu Yiwen had taken every scrap of profit he had earned from the project, and now, besides, was in league with Diao Kexin.

He stood there, and suddenly the Xiangcheng winter felt damned cold — cold enough to tighten his jaw, cold enough to sting his eyes, cold enough to make him want to crouch down and whimper like a kicked dog. But he did not crouch. He only ground the cigarette out on the car door, pressing till his fingers burned, then pulled the door open, got in and started the engine. When the engine caught, he felt something in his own chest catch and sound along with it — a roaring, muffled sound, like an ancient machine that knows it is about to fall apart and must still grind on, teeth clenched.

He thought: let the hatred be, then. Hatred is more solid than love. Love is empty, hatred is real. Love makes a man soft, hatred makes him hard. He had been soft too often in this life — soft for Zhang Jingyu, soft for Diao Kexin, soft for everyone he thought he could open his heart to. And what had it come to? Zhang Jingyu had left with their son, Diao Kexin had left in a Maybach, even Zhang Hailu had walked off without a backward glance. Here was Zheng Zhiyong, near forty years old, having earned himself nothing but universal desertion; next month's salary in his pocket, and not even a place to live that wasn't handed to him as charity.

Suddenly he thought of Xiaoya — that artless, guileless, happy little girl, the girl who seemed to have fallen for him, to look up to him. At this moment, as a man, he needed more than anything a woman's innocent, guileless adoration and passion, with no interest attached to it whatever.

He merged into the traffic, drove on for a while without purpose, then pulled over, took out his phone and called Xiaoya. When the call connected, he heard his own voice — dry, as though it came from someone else's mouth. “Xiaoya, take the high-speed train to Xiangcheng tomorrow.”

Xiaoya arrived the next day, and it was already afternoon by the time she did. They ate a simple meal, then went back together to Old Zheng's hotel. Xiaoya put her case away, took off her coat, then turned and said: “Brother, I've noticed a pattern — every time you call me, it's because something's gone wrong at work. Am I right?”

Old Zheng went to her, drew her to the bedside. Xiaoya sat down beside him, asked nothing further, and laid her hand over his. Her hand was very small, and very warm.

“Let's lie down on the bed,” Xiaoya suggested. The two got onto the bed and lay down, and Xiaoya nestled in Old Zheng's arms like a kitten.

“Xiaoya, I want to ask you something.”

“I only see you now and then, and I've never told you to be my girlfriend. In your heart, do you take me for a fuck buddy, or for what? Why have you never once said you wanted me to be your boyfriend?”

Xiaoya replied: “From the day we were together, I felt we were boyfriend and girlfriend, so I didn't think it needed saying.”

“Xiaoya, do you belong to me alone?” Old Zheng asked suddenly.

Xiaoya said nothing; as if unsure, her body stiffened a little.

Old Zheng looked down at her. He could not make out her face. “Brother,” she said, her voice much lower than before. “Of course I belong to you alone...”

“But,” she paused, “I'm not divorced. We're separated, but the papers haven't been done.”

Old Zheng looked at her. He did not move, nor speak. The bridge lamps over the Xiang River were lit outside the window, and the light came in and fell upon the ceiling.

“He gives me money every month — child support, and money for my living. We don't live together, but... in law we're still husband and wife.”

Old Zheng sat up. He took the cigarette packet from the bedside table, drew one out and lit it. The flame of the lighter flared in the darkened room, then went out.

“Why did you deceive me at the beginning,” he said. His voice was flat — too flat to sound like a question.

Xiaoya sat up too. She hung her head. “At first it was through livestream gifts that we met, and I thought a single persona would be better. Later I really fell in love with you, and the more I loved you, the more I feared that if you knew, you'd stop seeing me. Brother, it really has been over between him and me for ages — it's only a piece of paper. I've been looking for time to do it, but...”

“But what?”

“He won't agree. He says if I do the papers, he'll stop giving me money.” Xiaoya fell silent. Her tears fell, but she made no sound of weeping. She sat there, wrapped in the quilt, like a very small animal soaked by rain.

“So every time you asked me for work, you really meant work, and you never liked me at all, did you?” Old Zheng's voice was a low, strangled snarl. Xiaoya's body trembled. “No, no — I like you, I fell in love with you long ago.”

Old Zheng stared hard at the woman before him, then suddenly seized her by the shoulders, with a force almost beyond his control. Xiaoya gave a little cry; her whole body was wrenched off balance, and she fell once more into Old Zheng's burning embrace.

“Brother...” She tilted her head up, and before the words had well fallen, Old Zheng's kiss came down upon her like a storm.

There was none of the usual tenderness or tentativeness in this kiss; it carried a near-predatory roughness. One hand locked hard around the back of her head, knuckles sinking into her damp hair, forcing her to tilt up and receive his burning lips and tongue; the other travelled down the slender line of her waist and roughly tore open her blouse.

The soft cloth fell away in an instant, laying bare a broad expanse of skin that gleamed finely in the dim yellow light. Xiaoya shivered slightly at the sudden invasion, almost suffocated by the strong tobacco smell on him and the heat of his body. But when she felt the faint tremor in his fingertips, felt in this man who always kept up appearances before the world a desire now nearly reckless, the fear in her turned suddenly into unflinching compliance.

She did not struggle; instead she closed her eyes obediently, and her slender arms went of their own accord around Old Zheng's broad neck, returning the kiss with greater ardour.

Old Zheng's breathing turned abruptly heavy and hot, like a trapped beast that had broken its tether. His lips and tongue left the corners of her mouth and travelled down along her jaw, biting and sucking, pressing hard over her heaving collarbones and breast, leaving trails of red marks, some deep some faint, on the snow-white skin. His palm wandered down the smooth hollow of her waist and lower, kneading and exploring without mercy, and with precision forced open the most sensitive defences of her body.

Xiaoya could not help drawing a sharp breath; all ten fingers sank deep into Old Zheng's damp shoulder and back, her nails even scratching several red lines into the skin.

The air-conditioner breathed warm air, and the air in the room grew thick as if about to catch fire. The two rolled and fell into the deep softness of the great bed; the white bedclothes bunched into a knot in their violent entanglement. Old Zheng bent over her, took Xiaoya's long thigh in his grip and forced it hard toward her chest, then lowered himself, his eyes fixed heavily on the woman beneath him — and in that interplay of light and shadow, her faintly knit brows, her moist eye-corners, that look at once stubborn and utterly surrendered, all at some instant overlapped completely with the shadow deep in his memory that he had hated all his life.

The thought brought a surge of near-savage possessiveness to Old Zheng's eyes, and a vengeful pleasure. He held nothing back now; bracing Xiaoya's slender waist, he drove into her heavily and without the least quarter, all the way to the depths.

“Ah—” Xiaoya arched her swan-like neck, a broken, high-pitched moan spilling from her throat; her whole body arched sharply as if struck by a current, all ten fingers gripping the sheet beneath her.

Old Zheng gave a low growl, the veins at his temples starting slightly, sweat running down from his sideburns and falling on Xiaoya's heaving breast. It was as though he meant to vent in this near-uncontrolled collision all the humiliation, betrayal and resentment he had swallowed the day before; each movement more violent than the last, each deeper than the last, every thrust carrying a dull, distinct sound of flesh meeting flesh, echoing in the silent room till the air grew hot with shame.

At first Xiaoya bit her lower lip, trying to hold back, but soon she was utterly routed under Old Zheng's onset, violent as a gale. Her breathing quite lost its rhythm; broken moans and soft gasps escaped one by one through her slightly parted lips, rising and falling with Old Zheng's pace, now high, now a low sob, like a small boat bobbing in surf, able to steady itself only by clinging for dear life to his broad shoulders.

“Brother... slower, slower...” Her voice was thick with tears, the corners of her eyes flushed a deep red, her wet lashes trembling; her whole body had gone soft as a pool of spring water.

But Old Zheng could not stop. He turned her over, locked his hold on her waist from behind, and sank into her again recklessly, his burning chest pressed tight to her sweat-damp back, his low hoarse breathing sprayed against the shell of her ear, sending waves of violent shivering through Xiaoya.

Nothing remained in the room but quickened breathing, the rustle of bedclothes rubbed roughly together, and the burning warmth of their two bodies mingled. After an unknown while, Xiaoya's body drew taut to its utmost, uttered a trembling sound near to suffocation, and collapsed wholly upon the bed; and immediately after, Old Zheng too gave a muffled low growl, and with the last few heavy surges let go of every fixation and every ounce of his strength.

The storm at last subsided.

Her chest still rose and fell violently, her whole body as though newly drawn from water. The room fell silent again, with only the sound of their unsteady heartbeats, and some inexpressible emptiness creeping quietly through the cooling air. Old Zheng finished his cigarette and ground it out in the ashtray. He stood up, went to the window and looked out at the Xiang River. In the dark the water showed no movement — only a deep, quiet black. He used his phone to transfer twenty thousand yuan to Xiaoya.

He thought of Zhang Jingyu. Of the evening she discovered the recording, sitting opposite him, her tears falling one by one into her cup, then producing an agreement and telling him to sign. He had thought her too cruel at the time. Now, standing here, watching a girl who said she “liked him” sitting on the bed, he suddenly understood one thing: these later people, these later “likes” and “feelings,” all had a price in the face of their own survival. Xiaoya's price was a month's living expenses; Diao Kexin's was a billion yuan; Shasha's was a possible career future.

He was no different. For the sake of Diao Kexin's “like,” he had once placed Zhang Jingyu and their child on the other pan of the scales. And yet he had once possessed a love without a price — the girl he had loved in his youth, Zhang Jingyu, who married him when he had nothing, the wife who set up a baby's cot in their cramped room, the woman who, through countless reluctances, still supported each of his ventures and struggles — a gentle wife, an intellectual woman, now a professor and researcher at a scientific institute.

Old Zheng did not turn around. “Take the first train back to Chengdu tomorrow morning, and let's not see each other again,” he said.

With that he got up and dressed. Then the door opened, and closed again. The room held only Xiaoya, and the night colours of the Xiang River outside the window.

A week later, the internal audit at Xinghuan into the accounts and the non-compete matter had already pried open the most critical crack.

Wu Peng sat in his office, several compiled breakdowns of customs declarations and settlements spread out before him. Following up the thread of the earlier Dongguan fan lights business, he had not simply looked at the surface figures; he had pulled the customs declarations and actual settlement records for the relevant export business and cross-checked them against one another.

The result was soon plain to see: orders for the fan lights fell sharply in the following year, which on the surface looked like shrinking demand in overseas markets or a cyclical swing in the industry; but once the export volumes, the actual declared shipment volumes, and the settlement volumes and commission splits confirmed on Xinghuan's books were laid side by side, the flaw was glaring — there was a clear numerical gap between the export volumes of the Dongguan company's products found on the customs authority's official website and the book settlements, and the criteria used for the commission and revenue share circulating around this business simply did not square with normal commercial logic.

Wu Peng took the data he had worked out and called Wu You to tell him about it.

The two shut themselves in and went over the numbers. The abnormal discrepancy in the commission meant that the money had not remained in Xinghuan's public profit pool, but had been intercepted and siphoned off somewhere along the transfer chain.

“Uncle, did Zhang Hailu pocket this money while it was in his hands, or did Mr. Zheng work some trick on his end?” Wu Peng's brow was tightly knit.

Wu You set down his teacup, his gaze calm yet so sharp it seemed to pierce every hidden channel behind the ledgers: “Zhang Hailu may have handled some procurement and project follow-up, but he had neither the authority nor the nerve to touch the core order settlements and commission allocation. The only one who could link up the contracts, shipments, settlements and revenue-share channels, and still funnel the money outward, was Zheng Zhiyong.” The conclusion held no suspense whatsoever; it pointed to Zheng Zhiyong.

The next day, Wu Peng did not hesitate over the non-compete matter; he had a formal cease-and-desist letter drafted and sent at once to Zhang Hailu, explicitly stating that his setting up a technical services company to provide services to the Yuanwang/Gaoda system had triggered the non-compete clause, and that Xinghuan would pursue liability for breach of contract in accordance with the law.

Not long after the cease-and-desist letter was delivered, Zhang Hailu took the non-compete agreement to Diao Kexin and the company's lawyer to have it checked, only then realising with a start how airtight the clauses were — it was no worthless scrap of paper designed to scare someone. What was more, the fact that he was now coming and going from the Gaoda building had already been spotted by Old Zheng; the moment Xinghuan sued him or claimed damages, the ramshackle outfit he had just thrown together in Xiangcheng would be instantly thrown on the defensive.

Zhang Hailu was thrown into a panic. He took the cease-and-desist letter and asked Diao Kexin what to do, and just at that moment Wu Peng happened to call. Diao Kexin signalled to him to put it on speakerphone. “Mr. Wu, on this non-compete business — you've really misunderstood. I haven't joined Yuanwang or Gaoda; I'm only here renewing old acquaintance with former colleagues. And wouldn't you know it, Mr. Zheng just happened to run into us. The thing described in the cease-and-desist letter has no basis in fact at all — surely we can't go around slapping labels on people, can we?”

Wu Peng said, “Mr. Hailu, we've already gathered evidence. The name of the director at your present company — that's Yuanwang's current head of finance, isn't it?” Diao Kexin and Zhang Hailu slapped their foreheads, both wearing looks of shock and chagrin.

Wu Peng did not soften his tone; instead he shifted tack and pressed straight on with the matter of the fan lights orders. “Hailu, besides the non-compete, there's another thing of yours that may be more serious. Those orders for the fan lights back then — why did the volume fall so steeply the following year? The commission and the settlement volumes don't match, and you were the one who handled it. How do you explain that?”

On the other end of the line Zhang Hailu fell silent for two seconds, then promptly pressed mute and said to Diao Kexin, “It wasn't me — it must have been Mr. Zheng! What am I supposed to say, Director Diao?”

“Tell him it was Old Zheng. You can offer to provide evidence — drive a wedge between them!”

Zhang Hailu hesitated for a few seconds, then lifted the mute. “Mr. Wu, don't you drag me into this! I noticed something wasn't right at the time too — the volumes and the books simply didn't match, and I even asked Mr. Zheng about it. Mr. Zheng told me not to look into the actual volumes, just to go along with what he said. How that money moved and whose pocket it ended up in, I truly have no idea — you'll have to ask Mr. Zheng.”

Zhang Hailu spoke without a single slip — while washing his own hands of all responsibility, he pushed Zheng Zhiyong onto the very crest of the storm without a moment's hesitation.

When the call ended, Wu Peng sat at his desk, and another phone in front of him had been in recording mode the whole time. The interesting thing was that, when he played the recording back, the conversation between Diao Kexin and Zhang Hailu had been captured in full. Zhang Hailu had been far too nervous: what he took for the mute was actually turning the speakerphone off, and then he pressed the speakerphone back on — so the conversation between the two of them was recorded in its entirety.
