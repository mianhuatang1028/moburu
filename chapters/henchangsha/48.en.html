Chapter Forty-Eight: Boyi

Translation first published: 2026-11-11 12:24 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-11 12:24 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Boyi” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Southern Mountains”), on Mount Ji there is a beast shaped like a sheep, with nine tails and four ears and its eyes set in its back; its name is Boyi, and whoever wears it knows no fear. Nine tails, four ears, eyes on its back: only one who can see what lies behind walks steadily on the road ahead.

While Zhang Hailu was away on leave in Xiangtan, Zheng Zhiyong was not at the company either; no one had seen him and no one knew where he had gone. He had said one word to Wu Peng about a business trip, and then had not shown his face for several days. In fact he had taken an early high-speed train to Dongguan. Xiaoya arrived a day after him: he told her to change trains at Guangzhou and come on to Dongguan Station, and arranged for a driver from Wang Renhou's company to collect her and take her to the hotel where he was staying.

Wang Renhou was, truly, a man born under a lucky star: the bladeless fan business had taken off beyond anything. At the start he had talked of a million units a year; in the end they sold close to three million. Then, with summer temperatures climbing across Europe these past years, a European customer took a fancy to a product originally meant for Africa and India and placed an order for several hundred thousand units a year on top. And Wang Renhou had been honest with Zheng Zhiyong: having promised a commission on every unit, he really did send him all the customs declaration data; for every motor Zheng Zhiyong moved out through Qi Jin, he paid the commission, and on top of that Qi Jin had been setting aside seven per cent of the net profit for him — a few hundred thousand more. Both commissions, by the agreement made six months earlier, had nothing at all to do with Xinghuan; they were Old Zheng's own. From the first delivery on, the money went through a shell company set up by a friend and onto a card inside the country — a bank card in the name of Zheng Zhiyong's elderly grandmother. Only Zheng Zhiyong knew the PIN, and he held the physical card too, handy enough for cash withdrawals and everyday spending. In six months the number on that card had grown fast; before he knew it, it had passed two million. That money became Zheng Zhiyong's backbone. He had always imagined the day he would take it and slap it down in front of Zhang Jingyu: even if he could never go back to that old home of his, it would be the day he held his head up.

Wang Renhou brewed tea in his office at the factory to welcome Zheng Zhiyong. Business was good this year — very good: orders had gone well past three million units. Now there were European customers too, four hundred thousand units in the first year, and talk of doubling next year. The new orders were being billed in US dollars through a Hong Kong company, with the money landing at HSBC in Hong Kong and no longer being brought back to the mainland, so he suggested to Old Zheng that he might think about opening a bank account in Hong Kong; he had heard it was quite straightforward these days for people coming over from the mainland, deposit rates there were higher than on the mainland, and keeping money outside would be convenient later on. Zheng Zhiyong thought this an excellent idea and said so several times over. He would go to Hong Kong and open the account; from then on this part of the commission need not pass through the mainland at all, but could go straight into the Hong Kong account. Then he asked Wang Renhou whether the rest could be moved there too.

“Of course,” said Wang Renhou. “Whether the money lands on the mainland or in Hong Kong, it's all fine, all convenient.” The commission of six mao a unit he had first agreed with Old Zheng was based on an estimate of a million units in sales. With sales grown as they had, Old Zheng was earning more from him than Wang Renhou had ever expected — though Wang Renhou himself was certainly doing better still. That small technical edge was no great feat for Old Zheng or Qi Jin, but Wang Renhou's costs were now several yuan a unit lower than at the start; on three or four million units shipped, the real profit had long since passed ten million.

“Though, Mr. Zheng, there is something I'd like to talk over with you. When we fixed that price, the exchange rate was 7.1. Now it's down to 6.8, and by the look of it, it will keep falling. At the rate back then, my margin was comfortable enough; at today's rate, our company's profit has dropped quite a bit. Still, next year's orders are three or four times the million I first mentioned to you, so your commission on each unit... well, the total income has gone up after all. So about the commission per unit — shall the two of us discuss whether it might come down a little?”

Zheng Zhiyong agreed readily to two mao off each unit. He liked this Guangdong merchant's way of putting everything out in the open. If Wang Renhou never mentioned cutting the commission, and simply left one customer's deliveries out of the count, or shaved a little off the shipment figures, Zheng Zhiyong would probably never have found out. But this plain, honest arithmetic, with the money set out where both could see it, felt solid to him. The two of them had only ever had a verbal agreement, so there was no contract to amend; they settled it there and then.

After his talk with Wang Renhou, Zheng Zhiyong took Xiaoya to Hong Kong the very next day. For Xiaoya it was the first time she had been to Guangdong, and the first time in Hong Kong. She stood on the K11 observation terrace above Victoria Harbour, looking across at the harbour, at the skyscrapers ranked along the waterfront, at the lights coming on one by one in the dusk, at the buildings of Central crowding into the twilight — and suddenly felt that the world was far larger than she had imagined. Zheng Zhiyong took her to a Hong Kong-style tea restaurant and ordered spare ribs in black bean sauce, three-cup chicken, chicken feet, shrimp dumplings and garlic greens; then he took her shopping in Tsim Sha Tsui and wanted to buy her a handbag. She chose a Coach on sale, a style from the previous season, only three or four thousand yuan — but in Xiaoya's hands it was the first bag worth thousands that she had ever owned. Holding it, the feeling was not the weightless one she used to have when tips came in during a livestream; it was as though something real had settled, with weight, into her palm. The way she looked at Zheng Zhiyong grew gentler too. This man was more than ten years older than she was, with the gravity and the steadiness that age ought to bring. For Xiaoya, being brought to Hong Kong, walking behind him, being looked after so thoughtfully on a trip — this was very nearly the dependence and the sense of belonging she had been waiting for, and none of the men her own age whom she had known before could give it to her.

When he opened the account at HSBC in Central, Xiaoya went with him, and Zheng Zhiyong told her to open one too. She did. As they were opening it, the bank clerk explained that a balance of three thousand Hong Kong dollars would waive the monthly fee; without a word, Zheng Zhiyong transferred ten thousand Hong Kong dollars into her card. “Not a word of explanation — the man just sent the money,” Xiaoya thought. And she remembered how they had first met: he had tipped his way through a whole evening until he was the top patron on her stream. Zheng Zhiyong was not stingy; with his own woman he was generous indeed. A certain admiration rose in her — with, perhaps, a little love mixed into it; she could not have said herself. She understood, too, that the distance between this man and the world she had come from was no ordinary distance. When he went out on business, the other company sent cars to fetch him and take him back — and for a hanger-on like her, there was a car as well. At the bank in Hong Kong he moved easily between English and Chinese, unhurried even though it was his first time doing this. Everywhere they went there were good restaurants and grand hotels; this time the hotel in Hong Kong was the Ritz-Carlton in the International Commerce Centre, a luxurious sea-view room with a king bed, and through the full-height panoramic window on the seventieth floor Xiaoya saw an angle on the world she had never seen before — the angle of looking at the world from a very great height. And in fact the hotel went higher still, all the way to the 118th floor, the kind of height that, seen from below, seems to run straight into the sky.

The two stayed another day in Hong Kong. By the time Zheng Zhiyong got back to Xi'an, the project had entered its tightest stretch, and Zhang Hailu had long since returned to Xi'an as well.

The tendering authority's building was in the high-tech zone, and Wu Peng and Shasha went there often enough. The engineers at the company lost track of day and night; the technical team carried box after box of bid documents up from downstairs, Zhang Hailu shut himself in the meeting room with a few men to revise drawings and check parameters, and once Zheng Zhiyong was back in Xi'an he joined them through the nights, until the tables were heaped, night after night, with takeaway cartons and cigarette ends.

It was just then that Dr. Lan — one of Zhang Hailu's people, a man of few words and hard work — solved a technical problem that had held them up through the whole validation stage. He changed the algorithm, making the simulation system's data more reliable under multi-environment modelling. The change was small, almost invisible, but it would tell in that line of the evaluation score. Zhang Hailu was delighted and clapped him on the shoulder; Zheng Zhiyong quietly stepped over for a look, said nothing, and nodded. Wu Peng was there that day too: he bought supper for everyone and stayed until late before leaving.

A few days later Wu Peng came to Zhang Hailu and said that, at the project owner's request, every employee had to sign a project confidentiality agreement and a non-compete agreement, to go in with the company's qualification documents for review — standard practice in the trade. Zhang Hailu took the thick stack of printed papers Wu Peng held out, and in a gap between writing the bid, signed them along with everyone else.

A few more days, and it was the day the bids were opened.

On the day, Zheng Zhiyong, Wu Peng, Shasha and Zhang Hailu all went to the site; they were the first session, and afterwards came out. As Zheng Zhiyong walked down the corridor towards the exit, he was astonished to see Diao Kexin coming towards him. Yet no one but him showed the least surprise — it seemed everyone already knew that Yuanwang was in this tender too.

Yes. This time Xinghuan, Yuanwang and Gaoda had all put in bids; it would not be unfair to call it a narrow road for old enemies.

Diao Kexin wore a black cashmere dress below the knee, and over it a dark grey plush coat whose texture looked like cashmere too, its hem two or three centimetres longer than the dress beneath. In her hand was a black Celine tote with no logo; Zheng Zhiyong knew that bag — they had bought it together when they were still together. Her hair had grown a little, parted naturally slightly off centre and falling over her shoulders. She looked very thin, perhaps the thinnest she had been in all the years he had known her. Behind her came Zhao Bin and two others he did not know. She seemed to see him too, but she neither stopped nor nodded; only, as they passed, she turned her face the slightest bit, and went in.

“Mr. Zheng,” Shasha called softly. “I saw her,” Old Zheng answered, and told the others to go on ahead. Wu Peng drove Shasha, Hailu and the rest back to the company, while Old Zheng sat in the car parked downstairs, took out his phone and sent her a message: “Didn't expect to find you in Xi'an. Shall we meet tonight?” Five minutes after he sent it, a line came back: “I have an engagement tonight.”

He put the phone down and looked up through the window just as Diao Kexin was walking out of the gate. A moment later a black Volvo people-carrier drew up; Zhu Yiwen got out and shook her hand. Diao Kexin went straight to the car. It turned, drove out through the gate, and was gone.

Zheng Zhiyong rolled down the window, flicked the cigarette end out, and followed the car.

He watched the two of them reach a teahouse and go in together. Zheng Zhiyong waited at the door for a while, then drove away.
