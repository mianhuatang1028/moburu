Yingzhao

Chapter Seventeen: Yingzhao

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Yingzhao” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Western Mountains, Book Three”), the god Yingzhao presides over Mount Huaijiang: a horse’s body and a human face, tiger stripes and bird’s wings. He patrols the four seas and tends the Celestial Emperor’s level garden. As the saying goes: he who keeps the garden must first keep to his bounds.

For two whole days Old Zheng couldn’t bring himself to leave that office. His beard grew out, his hair was a mess like a chicken’s nest, his eyes sank deep into their sockets; he barely ate or drank, and in two days he lost nearly ten jin. Unlike before, no one else came to the office at all.

He went back to his little rented room, lay on the empty bed, and endlessly scrolled through his and Diao Kexin’s WeChat history and photos, sending message after message to Diao Kexin’s work WeChat account.

This was the last channel that hadn’t blocked him, but she read everything and never responded.

After about a week he decided to go out and look at the sun of this world again. He went downstairs and first called Diao Kexin’s sister-in-law. After a long time she picked up. “Brother Yong, I know about my sister. I’m really sorry.”

Old Zheng said, “How is she now?”

Her sister-in-law said, “She’s doing pretty well. He’s divorced, and he’s going to marry her.”

Old Zheng asked, “Do you know the details?”

Her sister-in-law said, “Oh, Brother Yong…” She hesitated. “If I weren’t family on her side, I’d be furious. Honestly, you were so good to her! But I’ll just say this to you as advice — there’s no turning this around.”

Old Zheng: “When did he get divorced?”

Her sister-in-law said, “About a month ago. My sister knew right away, a month ago, but none of us did — she didn’t tell us. She only told us recently.”

The first few days of that month had been Old Zheng’s last days at Yuanwang, handing over and completing his resignation; for the rest of the month he’d been constantly on the road, busy making sure production and delivery happened for the two of them’s company.

And all through that time things between them had been as sweet as ever — sweeter, even. They had even gone to meet Diao Kexin’s parents during that time, like an official confirmation.

Old Zheng dimly began to understand something, and kept asking, “What about the company? Has the first contract been delivered?”

Her sister-in-law: “Brother, I don’t know anything about the company. That was all my sister.”

Old Zheng: “Weren’t you the one looking after the company seals and the USB key and all that?”

Her sister-in-law: “Brother Yong, my sister took all of it back long ago. I really don’t know anything about any of it. You’ll have to ask my sister. Please, don’t ask me.” And with that she hung up.

Old Zheng had no way left to reach Diao Kexin. He kept asking himself over and over: if Gao Fei had already divorced a month ago, why had Diao Kexin kept up this act with him for a whole month?

So he called the supplier in Dongguan. “Director Li, a quick question — has the order been delivered?”

“Oh, it shipped last week. The contract gave us two months, and we delivered a week early.”

Old Zheng: “Has the final payment been made?”

Old Li said, “Paid three days before we shipped.” He paused, then added, “Your missus called to arrange it.”

Old Zheng: “Ms. Diao, you mean?”

Old Li said, “That’s right. No problem with the payment — and no problem with the quality, I hope?”

Old Zheng understood. A 1.5 million order; by his agreement with Diao Kexin, he should have been due a share of profit of over two hundred thousand. But now he had no idea who to ask for that money.

Old Zheng now faced a pressing problem: he was completely out of money. His credit cards, Huabei, every place he could overdraw was maxed out. He’d even taken out some high-interest online consumer loans. Most of it had gone on the two months he’d spent living with Diao Kexin: clothes, shoes and handbags for her, her daughter’s hospital bills, everyday meals and entertainment, travel for the company, and so on. His debts had now piled up to over a hundred thousand.

Worse still, he had no job, and no company of his own either.

It was deep winter now. Walking down the bleak winter street, Old Zheng called Lu You. “Mr. Lu — no, Brother Lu — I’ve set up a company. I’d like to invite you over.”

Lu You said, “Oh, now that’s a big change. Why aren’t you at Hanhai anymore? I heard you’re already looking for underwriters — IPO’s not far off, is it?” Lu You was still stuck at the part where Old Zheng had gone to Hanhai Chenxing to do sales.

Old Zheng said, “Ah, it’s a long story. I left Hanhai a while back. Why don’t you come by my company and we’ll talk in person?”

Lu You agreed, but in no particular hurry, set the meeting for a week later.

But Old Zheng had no time left to wait, so he called Qi Jin to ask whether he could sell him his remaining seven percent of Qingzhi Guanglun for seventy thousand yuan.

Qi Jin readily agreed, but he’d need about a month and would pay at the end of December; he also promised to pay Old Zheng, around July next year, the dividend he was owed for this year as a seven percent shareholder. Under Qi Jin’s management Qingzhi Guanglun had done a solid eight million-plus in revenue this year; its clients had stabilized and its gross margin was over sixty percent, and Qi Jin planned to distribute two million of profit to all the shareholders, so Old Zheng’s former seven percent meant a hundred and forty thousand in pre-tax dividends for him. He expected revenue to grow twenty to thirty percent next year. The only drawback of a company like this was that its clients were all state-owned enterprises or state units — slow to pay, with long cycles — so growth was fairly slow. But whatever its future held, from now on the company would have nothing more to do with Old Zheng.

On the day he’d arranged to meet Lu You, Old Zheng went to the office for the first time in nearly two weeks. It was cold and deserted: cigarette butts all over the floor, empty water bottles scattered everywhere, the trash can knocked over, the windows shut tight. The air was thick with a strange smell of decay and dissipation.

On the desk, the small rectangular fish tank was still there. The bodies of the little red goldfish floated scattered on the surface; the water plants below had grown green, slimy stuff, and the water was starting to stink.

Diao Kexin’s square compact mirror still sat jarringly in the middle of the boss’s desk.

Old Zheng opened the windows, picked up the empty bottles, swept up the cigarette butts, tossed the compact mirror and the long wool stockings from the drawer into a trash bag, then carried several big bags of rubbish out of the office. Just as he got back, Lu You arrived.

He walked into the office; a sign reading “Qingneng Technology” hung on the wall by the door. Seeing Old Zheng, he said in surprise, “Engineer Zheng, you don’t waste any time! When did you set up your own company?”

Then he took a proper look and saw that the Old Zheng in front of him was a completely different man from the one he’d met in late autumn more than a year before: a full beard, half-long untrimmed hair, and through the tangle you could see how terribly hollow his cheeks were, how deep his eyes had sunk. He looked more than ten years older. Lu You was startled.

Old Zheng invited Lu You to sit at the tea table, and as he brewed the tea he began telling him about his life this past while — from Hanhai to Yuanwang, how he had fallen in love with a girl, then the divorce, then the girl breaking up with him for who knew what reason and refusing to see him again, and then his present situation and his troubles.

He smoked one cigarette after another, and Lu You poured one cup of tea after another. For Old Zheng, the most turbulent, heart-stopping ten months of his life had come and gone just like that.

In telling it, Old Zheng said “the girl” instead of Diao Kexin’s name, and left out the crucial fact that Diao Kexin was the chairman’s mistress and had borne him an illegitimate daughter; he left out that her ex-husband had jumped off a building during their divorce; and he left out the real reason his girlfriend had broken it off so abruptly. Perhaps deep down he still wanted to protect his goddess’s privacy a little. But as he talked about the whole affair, he didn’t hide the names Yuanwang and Gao Fei. Lu You said, “I’d never have thought that in such a short time your life could change so drastically. It’s really something. So what are your plans now? And why did your girlfriend have to break up with you like that?”

Old Zheng: “Maybe I just disappointed her too much.”

Lu You: “Can you go back home?”

Old Zheng: “Probably not.”

Lu You: “I think you should try. Think about it — yes, you’ve lost your job, but the biggest thing you’ve lost right now is your wife and not getting to see your kid. If you go back now and show her properly that you’ve turned over a new leaf for good, and win her forgiveness, so you can move back home first — then you haven’t actually lost anything, have you?”

Old Zheng: “These past months, whenever I’ve contacted them, apart from taking the money, mother and son haven’t said a single word to me.” He took out his phone and opened his wife’s WeChat chat. Scrolling down, there were countless green bubbles — all the caring messages he’d sent — and not a single reply.

Lu You: “It’s twenty years of feeling, after all. If that doesn’t work, wait downstairs at her place, wait outside the school; when you see her, talk properly, take it slowly, step by step, and try to win her round. Whatever you do, don’t let on what you just told me — that you still can’t let go of your girlfriend. Just swear up and down, totally devoted, that you let your dick do the thinking for a moment, and the only ones you’ve ever really loved, the ones you’ll always love, are her and your home. Wouldn’t that work?”

Old Zheng: “I’m completely broke now. What would I use to persuade her to come back to me? Right now my company and the place I’m renting are both less than five hundred meters from my old home. Deep down, how could it not be because I can’t bear to let them go, mother and son? I feel like if I’m a little closer to them, it’s like I’m still watching over them. But she’s given up on me completely. She wants nothing to do with me. Ah…”

“Ah…” Lu You sighed along with him. He took a sip of tea and asked, “You said just now you’d set up a company and done a few orders in the meantime. How are you and the girl splitting the money?”

Old Zheng: “We agreed on seventy for her, thirty for me.”

Lu You said, “If she was the one landing the orders, that split’s fair enough, as long as you’re happy with it. So are you a registered shareholder under your own name? Are the company seals, the USB key, the bank account in your hands?”

Old Zheng took a deep drag. From start to finish he hadn’t so much as touched those things, hadn’t even asked about them. He answered truthfully.

Lu You sighed quietly and asked, “Did the two of you ever sign a nominee shareholding agreement?”

Old Zheng: “Back then we were so close we were like one person. Of course we didn’t sign anything!”

Lu You: “So have the contracts been delivered? Has the money come in?”

Old Zheng: “One contract should be delivered by now, and the money should have come in.”

Lu You: “Have they been in touch to give you your share?”

Old Zheng: “Nobody’s been in touch with me at all. And when I try them, nobody picks up.”

Lu You: “Then I’d say it’s not looking good. I’ll be blunt, don’t take offense — it sounds to me like this girl used you to make this money and then kicked you to the curb.”

Old Zheng: “Maybe the company found out about the side business and she did it to protect herself.”

Lu You nodded. “That’s possible too. Oh, and Yuanwang and Gao Fei — why do those names sound so familiar? I feel like I’ve heard them somewhere.”

Old Zheng: “Yuanwang was started by a Xiangnan University professor, more than ten years ago. They started out in SAR. They’re fairly well known in the industry.”

Lu You: “What a coincidence — I’ve got a cousin at Xiangnan University in a similar field. Who knows, maybe they know each other?”

Old Zheng: “Small world. Which school is your cousin in?”

Lu You was from Shenyang. His mother had been a university English teacher, one of two sisters. His aunt, the younger sister, had married into Guizhou — married a physics teacher there — and run a lumber company; she had managed to grow crops on salt-alkali land, so to speak, making her family one of the richest in that small place, in a relatively poor and backward city in the interior. His aunt had a son who’d had mild autism since childhood: bad at socializing, never one to express himself, but extremely intelligent, with remarkable attainments in mathematics and physics. He got into Beiqing University, then went to Imperial College in England for many years of further study, and these past few years his academic standing had grown steadily more secure; he’d been made a professor in Xiangnan University’s School of Aeronautics and Astronautics.

Lu You: “Oh, that I’m not sure about. I only saw him at family gatherings at New Year when we were kids. Probably aerospace. But this cousin of mine has had autism since he was little, and his marriage didn’t go well a few years back either. We’ve never really talked. With his personality, he’d never care about anything outside his research. He doesn’t leave campus all year round, doesn’t even like going home for New Year — he’d know even less about people starting businesses outside.”

“My mom sometimes tells me about him…” Lu You added. In fact Lu You knew perfectly well about this cousin jumping off a building at Xiangnan University; it had been a tremendous shock to the whole family. According to his mother, this cousin cared about nothing but physics and space science, never handled money after he married, and handed his whole salary over to his wife to manage. In their second year of marriage his wife had demanded that his family help them buy a home in Changsha. Later Lu You heard that his aunt had even sold her lumber mill to support the young couple, buying them the best river-view flat in town to set up house in. His aunt had been very easygoing about it, saying she was getting old and couldn’t do the work anymore anyway, so selling the mill meant she could retire, and later, when the young couple had a child, she’d move in and help raise her grandchild — and so she backed them without a second thought. Then at the divorce, when it came to dividing the property, his cousin discovered there was no property left to divide: all the cash was gone, the big flat had been sold at some point, and the money from the sale had supposedly been put by his wife into P2P lending, which had all collapsed — not a cent left. When his aunt found out, she was so upset she had a mild stroke, and only gradually got her speech back over several years. That cousin had been extremely introverted to begin with, had grown up with his wife from childhood, and had been utterly devoted to her all along. The divorce left him with neither wife nor money, and had dragged his mother’s business and health down with it too; in a moment when he couldn’t see any way through, he jumped off a building. Luckily he survived.

Every time Lu You’s mother brought it up she sighed endlessly — met the wrong person, met the wrong person — and she’d also say that, luckily, when the boy was small his aunt had asked her to choose his name, and a feng shui master had said the child’s name should contain water, because one day water would save his life. So she, the English teacher, gave the little one the formal name “Li Fulang” — fulang, riding the waves — which sounds like “live long,” for a long and healthy life. Later the family said that the pine grove Li Fulang had jumped into had a name too: Pine Billows Grove. He’d landed in the trees, so he didn’t die, only limped on his right leg ever after — which really did bear out the saying that water would save his life. After the divorce, young Professor Li never had the slightest contact with his ex-wife again; reportedly he lived even more like a recluse after that, alone at the university, rarely leaving campus, wholly devoted to his research.

After all, family shame isn’t to be aired in public, so Lu You deliberately skipped over the subject of his cousin and went on asking Old Zheng, “Which school is this Yuanwang professor, Gao Fei, from? When I get the chance I’ll make some quiet inquiries.”

Old Zheng said, “Should be the School of Aeronautics and Astronautics. But I can’t go back to Yuanwang, and I don’t want to anymore either.” He paused, then said, “There’s something I need to ask you right now. I need to borrow some money.”

Lu You said, “How much?”

Old Zheng said, “A hundred thousand.”

Lu You said, “Then let me ask you two questions. One: from here, are you planning to look for a job or start a business? Two: if I agree to lend it to you, when will you pay it back?”

Old Zheng said, “My last two jobs only lasted a few months each — counting the one before that, three companies in a row I’ve only stayed a few months. Finding a job won’t be easy. I still have some connections right now, so if there’s a chance I still want to start a business — do a few projects first to make some project money, and while I’m doing them, if I come across the right company, join them and work together. Right now there’s still a seven-hundred-thousand order from my last company being delivered. The money for that project goes through an affiliated company, and I’m their only point of contact; the goods are being delivered by my supplier, so in theory I have some control over that money. I’ve also arranged to go to Xi’an next week to meet their boss in person and talk about working together from here on.”

“If you lend me the money, I’ll pay it back within twelve months, without fail.”

Lu You said, “Engineer Zheng, I know very well what you can do technically. If starting your own business doesn’t go as smoothly as you imagine, finding a job and working steadily isn’t a bad thing either. If I see the right opening, I’ll keep an eye out for you. And yes, I can lend you the money — but you’ll need to write me an IOU. That all right with you?”

Old Zheng nodded.
