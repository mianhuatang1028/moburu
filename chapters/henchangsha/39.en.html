Tianma

Chapter Thirty-Nine: Tianma

“Tianma” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Northern Mountains”), on Mount Macheng there is a beast shaped like a white dog with a black head; when it sees people it takes flight. It is called the tianma, the Heavenly Horse.

The bid results came out in late April.

The Seventh Academy notified Old Zheng that they’d won the R&D tender for the night-vision project. As the joint development partner, Xinghuan would work with the academy on the materials for the R&D stage. The notice said nothing about money. Old Zheng sent Zhang Hailu to ask, and what he came back with was this: the R&D fee, originally said to be eight million, had in the end been cut to four million.

When Zhang Hailu reported the figure to Zheng Zhiyong, his face didn’t look good. He said, “From eight million to four — that’s one hell of a cut.” He was very unhappy about this. After all, last year they’d slogged their way through a twenty-million contract, and then, with one stroke of the pen, most of the company’s profit had been handed to Gaoda. He’d learned from Wu Peng that the company’s project as a whole was running at a loss, which was why at the end of last year only a few key technical people got year-end bonuses, a token few thousand yuan. Zhang Hailu’s wife and child were in Xiangtan; he’d left home to stay in Xi’an following Zheng Zhiyong, and now it was coming up on a year. Yet the equity promised when the brothers set out hadn’t materialized. He’d taken a pay cut to come, worked hard for a year, and hadn’t made any money — his take-home was actually a third less than last year, when he’d been working at his classmate’s little factory. The bitterness in his heart hadn’t built up in a day or two.

Old Zheng said, “When will the four million be paid?”

Zhang Hailu said, “It’s already been paid.”

Old Zheng said, “Then that’s fine. At the R&D stage we won’t lose money, but there’s not much profit either. The real money is in the commissioning stage — orders for tens of thousands of units. That’s the long-term business.”

Zhang Hailu said, “Whether we can even make the thing is still an open question. Commissioning’s not even on the horizon.”

Old Zheng said, “We grind it out. Finish the R&D stage first, get the thing made. By the rules, whoever does the R&D and makes it work becomes the primary supplier when it’s commissioned, and the volume won’t be small.”

Zhang Hailu said, “Mr. Zheng, I need to talk to you. Things have been really rough lately.”

The two of them lit cigarettes. The little year-end bonus Zhang Hailu got was as good as nothing. But back when the project was tight, the few brothers he’d been able to bring on at low pay were all men Zhang Hailu had led before, and they were all in much the same boat. Several were from Xiangcheng or Xiangtan, renting here and unable to go home, their income down instead of up. Zhang Hailu was miserable enough himself, and the brothers under him couldn’t help grumbling either — grumbling over meals, grumbling over cigarettes. Someone would post a meme in the tech group chat, and underneath someone else would add one word: “Broke.” Back then Zhang Hailu had told them, full of confidence, “This time it’s our own startup, and rewards go by merit” — because Old Zheng had promised to set aside at least ten percent as a bonus for the guys. Now the Sixth Academy’s final payment had been settled in full, and the Seventh Academy’s R&D fee, though it had been cut, was still four million, yet the year-end bonus was only a few thousand yuan each. Zhang Hailu didn’t know about any hidden costs, still less that Zhu Yiwen’s side had taken eight million. By his own rough reckoning, the company had made at least several million, yet for the technical people, nothing had materialized to this day. And now they’d settled in Xi’an, stuck in a position where they could neither go forward nor back.

So Hailu complained to Old Zheng about several of the key technical people on the team who’d taken pay cuts from the day they came. Under him there was a Little Chen in the algorithm group, who’d jumped over last year from a big company in Xiangcheng and taken a forty percent pay cut. He’d come because Hailu was his old boss, believing he was technically good and kept his word. Now, nearly a year in, he’d worked so much overtime he’d given himself a stomach problem, and his year-end bonus was 4,800 yuan. Little Chen hadn’t said anything in the group chat, but Hailu had seen him eating instant noodles at his desk at lunch that day, and seeing that these brothers who’d followed him hadn’t made any real money left him feeling pretty bad.

Old Zheng said, “I know all this.”

Zhang Hailu said, “Everybody knows things aren’t easy for the company. But the people under me have to eat. They took pay cuts to come, the year-end bonus was next to nothing, and a few of the key people are already close to breaking. I’ve been holding them back, telling them that the project is at its tightest right now, that it’ll be commissioned soon and there’ll be big money to share. But you can’t keep painting pie in the sky forever. I can’t keep holding them back.”

Old Zheng said, “How much are you thinking of asking for?”

Zhang Hailu said, “I don’t want anything. What I want is how much the team can get. This R&D money — can we carve out a piece and set up a milestone bonus? Not paid as a year-end bonus, but paid by project milestones — finish a milestone, pay a sum. It won’t be much money, but it’ll let the guys see something coming in.”

Old Zheng was silent for a while. He said, “The R&D tender is four million. After costs, there’s not much we can touch. This bonus you want — I’ll find a way.”

Zhang Hailu said, “It’s mainly for the stability of the people under me. If they scatter, even if we win the commissioning tender, there’ll be no one to do the work.”

Old Zheng stubbed out his cigarette and said, “Let me think about it.”

Zhang Hailu said, “Mr. Zheng, let me say something honest. These brothers came for the two of us, not for the company. To them the company’s just a nameplate — it’s the few of us. If you make them feel there’s no future in following us, they’ll leave. And once they’ve left and you hire again, the people you get won’t necessarily be as good as this lot.”

Several of the ablest of them were men who, back at Yuanwang, had served under both Hailu and himself. Zheng Zhiyong answered, “I know.”

Zhang Hailu stood up and said, “I’ll head back, then.”

When he reached the door, Old Zheng called him back: “Hailu.”

Zhang Hailu turned his head.

Old Zheng said, “Your part — I’ve got it in mind. When the commissioning tender comes through, I’ll work yours out separately.”

Zhang Hailu looked at him and said, “Mr. Zheng, I’m not telling you all this for my own sake.”

Old Zheng said, “I know.”

Zhang Hailu left. Old Zheng sat in his office and smoked a cigarette. It was only now that he was the boss himself that he understood how many petty matters there were in running a company. Only when he went to Wu You for the year-end review had he learned there were so many costs he’d never thought about. Now, doing the accounts himself, he was much more careful than before, and he’d never again, with one stroke of the pen, let a partner walk off with ninety percent of a project’s profit.

Old Zheng had finance put through a technical-services contract, with the payee an outside company Dr. Xiao had named. The contract said “infrared algorithm technical consulting,” for an amount of two hundred thousand, paid over in a single installment. Finance made the payment without asking questions, but the monthly report still flagged the expense in red.

The day the money went out, Old Zheng sent Dr. Xiao a WeChat: “Check your account.”

Dr. Xiao replied with an OK-hand emoji.

A little later she sent another: “When are you coming to Beijing? I’ll treat you to dinner.”

Old Zheng said, “I’m going next week. You pick the place and I’ll tell you the time.”

The next week Old Zheng flew to Beijing. Dr. Xiao had chosen a restaurant over in Fengtai, a little Huaiyang place, inconspicuous but clean. When Old Zheng arrived it was already evening. Dr. Xiao was already there, sitting at a table toward the back with two cups of tea in front of her. Today she wasn’t wearing that fleece jacket; she’d changed into a navy knit cardigan, and her hair had grown out a bit these past few months. She looked more feminine than before; she seemed to have no makeup on, but her face was fair with a pink glow, much softer-looking than she usually was at work.

Old Zheng sat down and said, “What made you think of treating me to dinner today?”

Dr. Xiao said, “We’ve divvied up the loot — don’t we have to celebrate? Besides, I can’t owe you a favor.”

Old Zheng said, “You don’t owe me. It’s what we agreed.”

Dr. Xiao said, “What we agreed.”

The food came and they ate. Dr. Xiao brought out a bottle of yellow wine. She asked Old Zheng, “From a hundred-year-old brand. Have some?”

Old Zheng laughed. He’d always thought this woman PhD was blunt and prickly in the way she talked, with no warmth in her; he’d never imagined she’d have so many contrasts in private — liked to sing, could hold her drink and didn’t let it get in the way of work. So he said, “You drink yellow wine? It goes to your head easily, this stuff.”

Dr. Xiao said, “Afraid of getting drunk? I didn’t even drive today. I came prepared.”

Old Zheng thought his own capacity for drink was pretty good, so the two of them went back and forth. After a bit of drinking, Dr. Xiao asked Old Zheng, “Do you believe in fate?”

Old Zheng answered, “Not really. You?”

Dr. Xiao said, “I’m a bit embarrassed to say it, but I do, quite a lot. The year I finished my doctorate, my mother had someone tell my fortune. They said I’d have two bonds in my life: the first an early marriage and early divorce, and at thirty-five I’d meet the second, my true match.”

Old Zheng said, “And how old is Dr. Xiao this year?”

Dr. Xiao said, “Thirty-five.”

Old Zheng said, “Then the match you’ve met must be one surnamed Zheng.” The moment it was out of his mouth he felt the joke had gone a bit far, maybe a bit improper.

Dr. Xiao said, “Mr. Zheng, behave yourself.”

The mood between them just then was very relaxed, and both of them were in good spirits. They drank a while longer. Dr. Xiao really wasn’t much of a drinker — she was the type with plenty of nerve and not much capacity — and now her face was red and she was talking a lot. She talked about things at the academy, about things from before. She said her ex-husband was a PhD too; the two of them had been married three years, couldn’t make it work, and divorced. She said she didn’t regret it, only that being on her own was sometimes pretty boring.

Old Zheng listened without cutting in much. When he saw she’d had about enough, he said, “You’ve had about enough. Stop drinking. I’ll take you home.”

Dr. Xiao said, “I’m fine.”

Old Zheng said, “Your face is all red.”

Dr. Xiao said, “So it’s red. Nothing to be ashamed of.”

As she said this she stood up, and halfway up her legs gave way and she sat back down. Old Zheng went over to help her up; her whole weight came against him. She couldn’t stand, her head drooped, her eyes were half closed.

Old Zheng paid the bill, half supported, half carried her out the door and hailed a car. Dr. Xiao gave the address, her voice slurred; she had to say it twice before it came out clearly. The car drove for twenty-odd minutes and reached the gate of a housing compound over in Fengtai. Old Zheng hauled her out of the car. She was unsteady on her feet, lurching every couple of steps, her whole body leaning on Old Zheng.

The compound wasn’t new, but it was clean, the buildings well spaced, the landscaping nicely done, the trees along the paths just coming into bud. Her building was at the very back of the compound. Old Zheng held her up for a stretch, went in through the building entrance and pressed for the elevator.

The elevator went up to the twelfth floor. Dr. Xiao fished out her keys, but her hand was shaking and she couldn’t find the keyhole. Old Zheng took them, opened the door and helped her in.

The lights inside were off. Old Zheng felt for the switch on the wall and pressed it.

The living room wasn’t big, but it was neatly kept. To the left inside the door was a row of floor-to-ceiling shoe cabinets in a light wood finish, with an open shelf in the middle holding a small potted plant and a tray; in the tray were keys, an access card and a tube of hand cream. To the right was the kitchen, half open, the counter clean, with only a kettle and an upturned cup on it. Further in was the living room: a gray fabric sofa, a beige knit throw draped over its back, folded very neatly. In front of the sofa was a low wooden table with a book lying open on it face down, and beside it a white porcelain cup with a ring of tea stain at the bottom. Against the wall was a whole wall of bookshelves, floor to ceiling, mostly technical books, but also a few novels and two volumes of poetry, the spines lined up neatly, with a gap on just one shelf, as if a book had just been taken out. On the windowsill were several potted plants — mint, pothos, and a whole long row of succulents, lots of pots, lots of varieties. The curtains were light gray, half drawn; outside, the Fengtai night was lit up, the streetlamps below shining one after another.

Beyond the living room were two rooms. One had its door open; you could see it was a study — a desk, an ergonomic chair, a laptop and a stack of printouts on the desk. The other door stood ajar: the bedroom.

Dr. Xiao, held up by Old Zheng, kept sagging toward the floor. Old Zheng helped her toward the bedroom, pushed the door open, felt for the switch on the wall and turned on the light.

The bedroom wasn’t big. A one-and-a-half-meter bed with pale yellow bedding, patterned with fine, dense, layered little flowers; the comforter spread over it, one pillow standing upright and one lying flat. On the nightstand were a lamp, a photo frame and a book with a bookmark in the middle. At the foot of the bed stood a white clothes rack with a set of loungewear and a big wool scarf hanging on it. Against the wall was a wardrobe, its doors shut. On the other side was a dressing table, not big, crowded with skincare products of every height: that white porcelain jar of face cream Old Zheng had seen before — La Mer, a brand Diao Kexin had used too — a lipstick, a wooden comb, a little mirror, not messy, but clearly things used every day. On the windowsill there was another long row of succulents, which looked much the same as those in the living room.

Old Zheng helped her sit on the edge of the bed. Dr. Xiao couldn’t stay sitting; she kept tipping to one side. Old Zheng put out a hand to steady her shoulder and let her lean against his arm. Her head drooped, her eyes were closed, and she kept sinking down.

Old Zheng laid her flat on the bed and reached out to slip the knit cardigan off her shoulders. Underneath she wore a fitted gray top with a soft velvety sheen; it hugged the lines of a woman’s body closely, leaving nothing hidden. Old Zheng was too embarrassed to look any longer, so he picked up the cardigan and went to hang it on the rack at the foot of the bed. But he still couldn’t help looking back. Xiao Jing was lying there now, breathing calmly — her long, snow-white neck, a little bit of collarbone showing. Old Zheng pulled the comforter over and covered her.

Dr. Xiao rolled over to face him and buried her face in the pillow.

Old Zheng stood by the bed and looked at her for a while. Her lashes were lowered, the long lashes flickering ever so slightly in the lamplight. The room was very quiet, with only her light, even breathing. The lamplight fell on the half of the back of her head that showed above the covers, her short hair spread loose, unbelievably smooth.

He went to the door and looked back once. Then he turned off the bedroom light, leaving only the one in the living room, went to the front door, turned off the living-room light too, closed the door softly behind him and left.

This time Shasha was going to Beijing a day after Old Zheng. That evening she was working late in the office, studying the night-vision technical materials so she wouldn’t get anything wrong when she saw the leaders in Beijing the next day. Wu Peng came over and said, “Sister Shasha, still here this late?” The two of them were actually only a few years apart in age, both fairly lively, and on top of that both Wu Peng and Shasha were tall, so they usually got on very well and talked a lot in private.

“I’ll be leaving in a bit. Mr. Zheng went to Beijing today. How come you’re still here?”

“Oh, I didn’t even know Mr. Zheng had gone to Beijing. I’ve just been dealing with some stuff till now. Sister Shasha, I’ve ordered a bit of barbecue — there’s enough for two. Want to eat together?”

“Sure,” Shasha replied.

Since the end of last year, Old Zheng had been deliberately keeping his distance from Wu Peng. Not openly — slowly, without showing it, he’d been moving certain things out of his hands. Before, booking tickets for trips, setting up meetings, passing on messages had all been Wu Peng’s job. Now Old Zheng went around Wu Peng on a lot of things, especially meeting people, picking people up, where he was going on his trips. When Wu Peng asked, Old Zheng would say, “You get on with your stuff. I sorted it myself, it was nothing.” Wu Peng didn’t let anything show on his face, but inside he felt things had changed. Especially since Old Zheng used to say so much in front of him and now said so little; whoever he was picking up, Wu Peng didn’t know. He was a bit closer to Shasha, and both of them had an interest in swapping information.

The food arrived, and the two of them went into Wu You’s office on the second floor, which was empty year-round, and ate at the coffee table, sitting on either side on the sofas.

Wu Peng said, “Sister Shasha, how come you didn’t go to Beijing today?”

Cong Shasha said, “Old Zheng left all of a sudden and didn’t tell me beforehand. I found out when he got on the plane. He wants me to come in a day or two.”

Wu Peng said, “I don’t know why, but lately I feel like there are lots of things he doesn’t tell me anymore. Not like a year ago when he first came, when he was like Xianglin’s Wife in that Lu Xun story, telling you everything over and over again. Now when he has me pick up or drop off guests, I don’t even know who they are.”

Cong Shasha glanced at him and said, “Really? What guests have you picked up or dropped off lately? Anyone special?”

So Wu Peng told her that there really had been one very special one. A while back he’d taken a very young girl to the train station — from Chengdu, it seemed. Mr. Zheng had gone to Beijing the day before, so he’d arranged for Wu Peng to take her. The girl was pretty curious and kept asking about Mr. Zheng personally and about the company the whole way, so it didn’t seem the two of them knew each other all that well. Stranger still, the girl wasn’t very educated and wasn’t in the industry either — she seemed like someone with no connection to any of it whatsoever.

“So what was special about her?”

Wu Peng said mysteriously, “I’ve seen photos of Mr. Zheng’s ex-girlfriend, Diao Kexin. This girl looks like Diao Kexin when she was younger.”

Cong Shasha exclaimed, “Seriously? Do you have her WeChat? Let me see.”

Wu Peng shook his head. “I don’t. But I’ve got her phone number — Mr. Zheng sent it to me on WeChat.”

Cong Shasha said excitedly, “Quick, quick, search it, see if it’s a WeChat account.”

Wu Peng took out his phone and searched the number, and sure enough it was a WeChat account, under the name Zeng Xiaoya. He tapped to enlarge the profile picture, and the two of them put their heads together over the photo. Cong Shasha was astonished: “Holy crap, that’s way too alike. Let me show you a picture of Diao Kexin!”

Cong Shasha took out her own WeChat and brought up Diao Kexin’s Moments. The last post was still a New Year’s selfie with holiday greetings. She enlarged the picture, and they held the two phones side by side. Wu Peng and Shasha both exclaimed that such an amazing thing could happen in this world, laughing like mad and marveling at the same time. The gossip tasted better than the food.

Wu Peng said, “Sister Shasha, what do you think — should I add her?”

Cong Shasha said, “Don’t. Don’t beat the grass and startle the snake!”

The two of them ate their barbecue and chattered about all sorts of other odds and ends. Wu Peng genuinely liked this older sister four years his senior, and had no idea that Cong Shasha and Old Zheng had been together for more than half a year. Shasha, meanwhile, had learned from Zheng Zhiyong a while back that Wu Peng was Wu You’s own nephew, so she used the connection to find out things about the company at the shareholder level from Wu Peng now and then. Each of them had their own ideas, but you could still say they were sincere with each other.

This Zeng Xiaoya, tonight — when had she burst into Zheng Zhiyong’s world? Who’d have thought Old Zheng would keep a secret so well? Shasha had told him long ago that she had no other demands of him, only one: whatever it was, he had to tell her everything and hold nothing back. But now it seemed this man had broken his word. The excitement she’d felt with Wu Peng a moment ago drained away completely, and what was left deep in Shasha’s heart was jealousy and suspicion. Why would this girl called Zeng Xiaoya come to Xi’an to see Old Zheng? Could the two of them already have slept together?
