Chapter Fifty-One: Huashe

Translation first published: 2026-11-13 00:31 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-13 00:31 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Huashe” — in the Classic of Mountains and Seas, the Central Mountains, there is a water beast: a human face on a jackal’s body, bird’s wings, and it moves like a snake; its cry is like a shout of rebuke, and where it appears, the whole district is flooded. As the saying goes: a human face and a jackal’s body, gliding through the dark of night.

Fifteen days passed in a blink, and the day of the bid presentation arrived. On the Xinghuan side Old Zheng led the delegation himself, bringing Zhang Hailu and Wu Peng to the venue. The whole presentation went off without a ripple. On stage Zhang Hailu took the panel through the system architecture and the simulation data with tight logic and handled the experts’ questions with reasonable poise. Old Zheng sat in the front row below, steady in expression, his eyes drifting now and then across the faces of the experts on the review panel. When it was over, the Xinghuan team packed up their laptops and materials and walked out along the thick red carpet.

Just as they rounded the corner of the second-floor administrative corridor, a group came the other way. At its head was Diao Kexin. Today she wore a dark blue business suit of exacting cut and carried a fine leather briefcase; behind her came two technical men whose faces were unfamiliar.

The corridor was wide; the two groups passed each other with two or three metres between them.

No one stopped; no one offered a word of greeting. Diao Kexin looked straight ahead, chin slightly lifted, a faint smile at the corner of her mouth that might not have been there at all. Old Zheng’s face gave nothing away; he did not even raise an eyelid, only kept his right hand in his suit pocket, his fingertips turning over that string of old walnuts, already polished smooth. For that one second the air seemed to set, perfume and the winter chill of the corridor braided together — then it broke apart as quickly as it had formed.

Outside the gates of the Sixth Academy the winter sun over Xi’an was glare without warmth. Old Zheng went back to the office with Zhang Hailu; Wu Peng said he had something to see to and drove off ahead of them.

The grey Tesla turned smoothly into an upscale residential compound on the southern outskirts and stopped below the building where Shasha lived. She had not come to the venue today; it was only after asking Zheng Zhiyong that Wu Peng learned she was down with a fever.

On the way he had already bought simmered chicken soup and seafood congee, packed them in an insulated bag and set it on the passenger seat. Now he took out his phone and called her. It rang seven or eight times before it was picked up, and her voice came through thick with congestion, a little weak: “Hello — Wu Peng?”

“I’m downstairs.” He kept his voice low, with an urgency he could not quite hide. “Heard you’ve got a fever. I picked up some hot congee and chicken soup on the way — would you rather come down for it, or shall I bring it up?”

Two seconds of silence, then a small rustle of movement. “Don’t come up — the place is a mess, and I don’t want to give you this. I’ll throw on a coat and come down. Give me two minutes.”

He hung up, took the insulated bag from the passenger seat and got out. The winter afternoon breeze carried a bite; it felt cold on the face. He leaned against the door of the grey Tesla, eyes fixed on the glass door of the entrance.

Before long the door was pushed open. Shasha came out: a cream-coloured knit sweater underneath, a thick down jacket thrown on over it, her long hair loosely gathered at the back of her head, a few strands stuck to her pale cheeks. The woman who was always so crisp and composed at the office, always so neatly made up, had shed every guard now; the rims of her eyes were faintly red, her lips a little cracked, and her whole person gave off a fragility that hurt to look at.

Wu Peng stepped forward and handed her the bag without ceremony, his gaze resting a moment on the unnatural flush of her cheeks. His brows drew together slightly. “Has the fever come down? Have you been to the hospital?”

“It hit thirty-eight this morning. I’ve just taken something for it — much better now.” She took the bag, and her fingertips brushed the warm back of his hand. The warmth from the bag reached her, and something in her shifted. “Today of all days, with the presentation — why did you drive all the way out here? Can Old Zheng manage without you?”

“The defence went well. I came as soon as it was over.” He looked at her, and the seasoned, well-mannered manner he wore at the company for business and politics was gone; only plain concern remained.

The two of them stood in the shade of the trees below the building. The afternoon light sifted down through the thin branches; it was quiet enough to hear the wind moving in the leaves. Shasha had her head down, about to say a polite thank-you and send him off to rest, when Wu Peng suddenly took half a step forward and closed the distance between them.

“Shasha.” He said her name low, lower than usual, and remarkably steady. “I didn’t see you at the presentation today. I only found out you were sick because I asked Old Zheng. And driving over here, it suddenly came to me — if I don’t say certain things to you now, I may well regret it.”

She raised her head and looked at him, caught off balance.

He drew a deep breath; in his eyes was a seriousness, a candour, that had never been there before. “Shasha — will you let me take care of you?”

The cold wind lifted the stray hair from her forehead. “What are you talking about?” she said.

“Then pretend you didn’t hear it.”

A wicked little smile crossed her face. “Is what you want to say the same as what I’m thinking?” Then her nose pricked and she sniffed sharply. “I have to get back in.”

The tension in his eyes dissolved in an instant, replaced by a joy he could not hide. He did not step back; he reached out and rubbed the top of her head through the down jacket. “What I said is exactly what you’re thinking. Go on up, drink the congee while it’s hot, get a good sleep — I’ll message you tonight.”

He watched her turn with the bag and go in, watched until she disappeared around the turn of the stairwell, and only then did he open the car door and get in. He sat with his hands on the wheel, looking out at the quiet winter street, and could not keep the corners of his mouth from rising.

On the third day after the defence, a strange quiet settled over the technical department on the fourth floor of Xinghuan.

The first to knock on Old Zheng’s door were two of the team’s senior hardware and structural engineers — Xiao Chen and Engineer Li. Both were men who had come up hard with Zhang Hailu from out of town, the kind who kept their heads down and worked and almost never asked for anything beyond reason. That afternoon they stood awkwardly before his desk and handed over two printed resignation letters, the reasons written with the utmost courtesy: family matters at home, a plan to go back to the old hometown, a change of scene.

Old Zheng thought little of it at the time. In R&D, some movement of people after a project has been through a hard fight is normal enough. He said a few words to hold them, urged them again and again to think it over a while longer, and hurried off to find Zhang Hailu and get to the bottom of it.

Zhang Hailu said he had been talking them round for days without result. “Xiao Chen especially — you know as well as I do, his family has been leaning on him hard these six months. There are things you just can’t force.” Old Zheng asked whether it would affect the work ahead; Zhang Hailu thought that for carrying the projects already in delivery it should not matter much if the two of them left, though if the Xi’an bid came through, they would need to take on replacements.

But barely a week later, Dr. Lan walked into his office.

Dr. Lan was the soul of the whole core algorithm, and his resignation dropped through Old Zheng like a stone. Lan had never been a talker — an engineering man to the bone — and now he stood before the desk with his head down, saying only that he wanted a rest, that he might go south and look at other openings. However hard Old Zheng pressed — was it the pay, was it the division of work — Lan kept his jaw set and would not let out one more word.

An indefinable chill crept slowly up Old Zheng’s spine.

That night Zheng Zhiyong called Zhang Hailu straight to his office to discuss Lan’s resignation. Hailu came in, closed the door gently behind him with his back hand, and walked to the desk without a word. Old Zheng saw that his face was wrong, and the outburst already forming died there — his fingers froze in mid-air.

Zhang Hailu spoke. “I know what you want to say to me, Mr. Zheng. Lan has already told me. This time he’s set on it — there’s nothing I can do, I can’t talk him round at all. More than half the men I brought up from Xiangtan are gone now. I don’t feel I can keep going myself.” Old Zheng looked into those bloodshot eyes, and half his heart went cold on the spot.

The two of them were silent for several minutes, and then — unexpectedly — Zhang Hailu put in his own resignation, quietly. “Mr. Zheng, the truth is the pressure at home is heavy on me too. Two years with you, the income’s been too low, and I’ve taken too little care of my family. Lately it’s been one illness after another — the child sick, the old man sick — and my wife complains more and more. Now that the team’s people are leaving, even if I stayed I couldn’t do much for you. You’re better at the technical side than I am anyway, and the young ones the company hired all look to you. My going won’t hurt you much.” Zheng Zhiyong knew the situation in Hailu’s house well enough — the child’s fever a while back, the father’s stroke and hospital stay had all been talked over in the ordinary run of things, and Old Zheng knew every word of it was true.

Then Zhang Hailu went on about how these past years he had been grateful to Old Zheng for spotting him and raising him up, and how it was for the brotherhood of Mr. Zheng that he had stayed and held the line and worn himself thin. But now the old man at home had fallen ill, and the pressure on him was more than he could bear — if he did not go back, he could not answer to his family at all. “So what I came to tell you today, Mr. Zheng, is that I’m formally tendering my resignation.”

A dead silence fell over the office. Outside, the neon signs came on one after another, casting their light on Old Zheng’s face as it darkened.

“Hailu — the lot of you leaving at once, that’s pulling the firewood out from under the pot, for me and for this company. You can’t just hand me the whole mess without warning. We’ve been brothers building this thing for years. The last two years the money truly hasn’t satisfied anyone, I know that — but the year-end is right here, isn’t it? I’ve already squared it with the company: this lunar new year everyone goes home with a good bonus. And the numbers for next year are already worked out. Even without the Xi’an project it looks fine. The company is getting better and better.”

Zhang Hailu said, “Mr. Zheng, you know my situation. I give you my word: I won’t leave at once. I can stay on a while — a few months of transition if need be — and I’ll hand everything over so that no business of yours slips. I’ll be responsible to you.”

“Then don’t talk resignation for now. Take what I’ve promised you and put it to Lan, and let’s talk again in a few days — all right?”

Zhang Hailu neither agreed nor refused. He walked out of the office.

Old Zheng leaned slowly back into his chair. His head was roaring, and then he was plunged into a violent shock and a frenzy of conjecture.

How could it be? Could this be coincidence — so many of them walking out all at once? From Xiao Chen and Engineer Li at the bottom, to Dr. Lan at the core of the algorithm, to Zhang Hailu himself as the last piece on the board — could it really be that since the company was founded, since all of them had left homes and families to come here, they had never once been paid the number originally promised, until the team lost the heart to keep going? But only weeks ago Hailu had been bucking him up, saying that next year the figures already worked out, that even the floor-line return would be respectable; and he knew, too, that Wu You had promised the 6% team incentive equity would all be in place before the lunar new year.

Impossible.

Coincidence — too much coincidence. The entire core technical bench of Xinghuan, and at the very moment the defence had just ended, they wanted to leave as a body, in formation. This could not be coincidence. Most likely the whole lot had been poached, the whole team pulled up by the roots.

Who?

Old Zheng’s eyes flickered violently in the dim room. In one leap he thought of Diao Kexin … but Yuanwang itself was drowning in debt, ready to fall — what would she feed an entire team costing a hundred and fifty, two hundred thousand a month? And now that she had Zhu Yiwen’s technology, a better algorithm team than his, why would she want to poach a team that was not as good? And yet, turn it over how he might, it could only be Diao Kexin. But Zhang Hailu and the others had been working late at the company nearly every day lately; there had been no chance of any contact between them at all. When had they come to terms?

What Old Zheng could never have imagined was that, only days before, in a suite at the Shangri-La, the woman he had written off as finished had already broken clean through Zhang Hailu’s defences with a sophisticated underlying algorithm architecture and a promise of doubled salary. And Zhang Hailu — above all once he understood the tangled knot of love and hate between Old Zheng and Diao Kexin — out of a last shred of guilt toward Old Zheng, would never let slip half a syllable about Yuanwang or Diao Kexin.

The water on the tea table had long gone cold. Old Zheng sat alone in the shadows, and for the first time truly felt that piercing chill of having the firewood pulled out from under him.
