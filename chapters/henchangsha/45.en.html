Chapter Forty-Five: Heju

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 18:02 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-10 14:38 (UTC+8) Last updated: 2026-11-12 16:39 © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Heju” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Eastern Mountains”), on Mount Beihao there is a beast shaped like a wolf, with a red head and rat’s eyes, whose cry is like a pig’s, and it eats men. As the saying goes: a wolf’s shape at your side, a red head before you, rat’s eyes in the dark, a pig’s grunt at your ear — and it makes men eat men.

Shanghai, Pudong. Lujiazui.

Wu You had already learned that the night-vision order had come down to a mere twenty percent, and he had also heard that the other simulation project had stalled — the budget, they said, had been cut by more than half, and the project looked close to dead. Old Zheng had been working under him for over a year now. The business had, more or less, got itself into a few projects, but too many things had come up one move short, and the accounts never balanced: put plainly, after a year and a half the company had made no money, and was still losing it.

Then, as it happened, an old family friend of the Wu household passed Wu You word of a project in Xi’an: a tender for an intelligent sensing system and a data centre, put out by the Xi’an branch of the Fifth Academy of Aerospace and by the Xi’an Economic and Technological Development Zone. An information gap is a business opportunity. Wu You had also come to feel that letting Old Zheng run the place as he pleased while he himself kept his hands clean was no solution, so he called Wu Peng to discuss it: from then on Wu Peng would go to the Xi’an company and serve directly as deputy general manager, and take charge of sales and client relations for Xi’an as well. What was more, the acquaintance behind this Xi’an lead happened to be a family friend of the Wus — Wu Peng addressed him as uncle, and the man had watched him grow up — so the project could be handled as a matter of course, with a proper name to it.

Wu Peng was of course only too glad; it also showed that his uncle was satisfied with the year he had spent keeping his light under a bushel. Since the winter before, when Zheng Zhiyong had learned who Wu Peng was, Wu Peng might still have carried the title of assistant, but plainly many things were now being kept from him. Wu Peng guessed that something had changed, but he was even-tempered, knew how to handle people for one so young, and had a certain depth to him, so he never named the thing outright. Old Zheng went out of his way to screen information from him; he, by contrast, had struck up an easy friendship with Zhang Hailu and the young technical staff under him, and had grown close to Cong Shasha as well. Now that the formal appointment was out, Wu Peng went from being an informant planted in Old Zheng’s company to being a nail driven into it. From then on, Old Zheng could no longer decide everything on his own. This was a major decision of Wu You’s. When he had given you your chance back then, he had handed it over in good faith — but the chance was given, and you did not use it well, did you? Nearly two years had passed, most of his patience was spent; the hand that had moved the pieces lifted again, and began to consider a new disposition of the board.

Before the appointment email went out to the whole company, Wu You had of course spoken with Zheng Zhiyong on the phone. Old Zheng understood that the situation had changed. But this time Wu Peng was being put in charge of a new business opportunity in his own right; uneasy as Old Zheng was, on the surface there was nothing he could fault, and he had no choice but to accept it. The Beijing simulation project, after all, was now openly on its last legs: the technical staff hired while the team was being assembled, and the preliminary research done beforehand, would all go down the drain. And this new project happened to point in the same direction, with military-civilian integration applications to follow — a genuinely excellent opportunity.

Once the email went out, the undercurrents started. In a company of barely a dozen people there were, openly and quietly, several little circles, and everyone discussed in private how Wu Peng had gone from assistant to deputy general manager in a single leap, whether Zheng Zhiyong was about to leave, and, if Zheng Zhiyong were not secure, whether the head of the technical team would be replaced. Wu Peng got wind of these whispers from Shasha; having thought through how to answer them, he decided to stop pretending, told Cong Shasha the truth about his background, and worked out a response with her.

So it turned out that besides being his own uncle, Wu You was also the owner of the company; Wu Peng’s grandfather’s generation had been in the army, and his own father had at one point served as a major general. Sadly, while Wu Peng was abroad at university his father died young, and so his uncle took on the responsibility of looking after him. Though they were barely a dozen years apart, this young uncle had been father and brother to him both, and had always cultivated him in his own way; and Wu Peng, having lost his father in his teens, had grown shrewd beyond his years, and his temper had settled into something exceptionally steady.

Shasha had in fact long known that Wu Peng and Wu You were uncle and nephew; hearing the details from Wu You himself now, she was still deeply struck by how well the young man had kept it hidden. But being taken into his confidence naturally added to her good opinion of him. What was more, in Cong Shasha’s eyes this appointment, made at a moment of crisis, was a chance for the crown prince to win his spurs. Shasha was close to Old Zheng, and over that year and more of work she had seen clearly enough that beneath the boss’s title he was at bottom a professional manager; gradually she had come to understand that Xinghuan, this company, was in the end named Wu. So Shasha asked Wu Peng: “Do I go on keeping your background secret? Or should I play along with you, and the two of us work out a way to steady people’s nerves?”

Wu Peng said: “I was planning to come clean to Mr Zheng and Mr Zhang anyway. I don’t understand technology and I don’t understand the industry; I’ve come into the business side of this company, but I haven’t taken a title for it. My uncle simply wants me to take part in the actual business. The two of them are the ones he leans on, absolutely, and there is no question of anything else changing in the company.”

Cong Shasha said: “So you mean I should set up the meeting with the two of them?”

Wu Peng said: “Yes.”

A few days later the four of them held a private dinner at a farmhouse restaurant outside Xi’an — a Northeastern place, where the dish was goose stewed in an iron pot. Wu Peng brought along Guizhou Daqu. After three rounds the drink had loosened them, and Wu Peng told them that he was Wu You’s own nephew. He explained that at the outset he had had no technical background, that his uncle had wanted him tempered at the bottom for a few years, and that he had not wished to be given special treatment in the company, which was why he had not disclosed the relationship. He said nothing of being descended from a senior army officer, nor of the family’s history. He said only that he would concentrate on learning sales and client relations from Shasha, and that the company’s core technical business and operations still rested on the two elder brothers; he did not wish to take part in them, he only wanted to work with the two of them properly.

With that one dinner Wu Peng declared his loyalty to his two elder brothers. Zhang Hailu understood and believed him completely, and went back and told his team as a piece of gossip; the anxieties of the technical staff eased. Zheng Zhiyong’s anxieties, however, did not ease in the least. Only now did he notice that Cong Shasha had been very close to Wu Peng of late, and that she had spent far fewer nights in his fourth-floor flat. That day, before the office emptied, Zheng Zhiyong asked Shasha privately to stay the night.

“I can’t, Mr Zheng. I have to go home and pack tonight — that supplementary contract for the Sixth Academy’s simulation system. They say the budget’s been halved, but it isn’t entirely hopeless, and I fly to Beijing tomorrow. I want to put in a bit more effort.” Shasha was telling the truth. In the office she always called him Mr Zheng, never Brother Yong; only in private did she still call Old Zheng, warmly, Brother Yong.

“Then when are you back? Come to me the day you’re back — you haven’t been up to the fourth floor in ages,” Old Zheng said, a little deflated.

The next day Cong Shasha went to Beijing.

On that trip Cong Shasha heard from her superiors a piece of astonishing news: Diao Kexin, who had vanished without a word for the better part of a year, had in fact been taken in as well — arrested, she now learned, at the airport, and held for six months afterwards. The official term was “residential detention”: nominally cooperating with an investigation, chiefly to establish the facts of Gao Fei’s crimes. Because Diao Kexin had had thorough discussions with Gao Fei about this matter before she was taken in — and Gao Fei, one had to admit, was a man of feeling and honour; Diao Kexin had after all borne him two daughters — he had beforehand set up trusts for her in Hong Kong and in Britain, bought some bitcoin, and stored the cold wallet physically in a safe in Hong Kong; altogether he had left her assets of perhaps twenty or thirty million yuan. Then, during the whole review, Diao Kexin was accounted to have performed meritorious service by informing. Though she had been held the better part of a year, in the end she came away whole, without a scratch on her, and returned to Yuanwang in Changsha; now she shuttled between Beijing and Changsha and had become the new person actually in control.

So it had been: every person actually in control and every owner of Yuanwang had lost contact for the better part of a year, and the finance director had been taken away for questioning too. Under Sister Yan’s remote direction the company, in accordance with its statutory compensation obligations, had let most of its staff go, leaving only two finance and administrative people, and the seven or eight engineers Zhao Bin had kept, to maintain the plant’s capacity. The company still had the equipment orders left over from before; these people drew their salaries and made their deliveries, and production went on.

The main gate of the Yuanwang Building on the Xiang River had been sealed by the court. But not far from the building stood a small military production plant from Yuanwang’s earliest days; small it was, but under one CTO after another it had got the production line to eighty percent automation, so the people who remained moved their offices there. It was plainer than before, but the business carried on, and the volume that survived was more than enough to keep this one small team that was left.

After Diao Kexin came out, Zhao Bin and Wang Yan discussed with her how to handle the company. With bank loans, buybacks of state investment and several other holes of debt to face, someone had to stand at the front: either cooperate with bankruptcy liquidation, or cooperate with bankruptcy reorganisation.

The news shook Shasha until her features seemed about to come apart. She hurried to telephone Zheng Zhiyong and tell him everything she knew, exactly as she had it.

Zheng Zhiyong was stunned. He had thought Diao Kexin and the child had gone to Britain. Many times he had taken out WeChat to look at where their conversation had stopped, and each time he had not wanted to intrude, and so had not contacted her again.

He had not expected her to come back. Stunned, heartbroken, and in pain.

Half a year of detention, and he had noticed nothing at all. In that half year he had been through several women and had long since put Diao Kexin out of his mind — and now she had come back, in this way.

Zheng Zhiyong pressed her: “Shasha, is Diao Kexin in Beijing now?”

Shasha answered: “How would I know? I heard it all from Old Wu. They say she’s come back to do projects. As for where she is, I’ve no idea.”

Zheng Zhiyong said: “All right. Come back soon.”

Shasha hung up. There was one other thing she had not told Zheng Zhiyong: when she left the Seventh Academy today she had run into Dr Xiao in the compound, and Dr Xiao had asked to meet her — tomorrow evening the two women would have a meeting of their own, with Zheng Zhiyong not there.
