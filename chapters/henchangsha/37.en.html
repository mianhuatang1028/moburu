Bingfeng

Chapter Thirty-Seven: Bingfeng

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Bingfeng” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Western Lands Beyond the Seas”), the bingfeng lives east of Wuxian; it is shaped like a pig, with a head at both front and back, and it is black. Two heads, one in front and one behind, on the same body: one wants to go east, the other west; neither can overpower the other, and neither can leave the other. As the saying goes: two heads on one body, growing in opposite directions — you say a line and I answer it, neither gives way, neither gives in; evenly matched at the chessboard, a general meeting a worthy foe.

Cong Shasha had been working the night-vision project for two months, and she knew the key person was Xiao Jing — Dr. Xiao.

Xiao Jing was thirty-five, the overall technical lead on the night-vision project over at the Sixth and Seventh Academies. She had a doctorate, was divorced, single. In Cong Shasha’s own words: “She’s seriously strong technically, but she’s way too much her own person. I’ve been in two meetings with her and she didn’t smile once from start to finish. The way she talks could choke you — if you get anything wrong she points it out on the spot, doesn’t leave you an ounce of face. But everything she says is right. You can’t argue with her.”

Old Zheng said, “People like that are easy. On technical matters, you explain things clearly and she’ll accept you. If you can’t explain them clearly, you could buy her ten dinners and it wouldn’t help.”

Cong Shasha said, “Then you’re the only one who can go work on her. Who else is there?”

Shasha set up a meeting with Dr. Xiao for Old Zheng, at a teahouse near the Seventh Academy. Cong Shasha had sent him the project requirements ahead of time, and Old Zheng spent three days on them, working through the few technical sticking points in them.

Old Zheng set off from Xi’an in the morning and came straight to the teahouse from Daxing Airport. Dr. Xiao got there before him. When Old Zheng pushed the door open and went in, she was sitting at a table by the window with a cup of tea in front of her. She wore a dark fleece jacket, her hair cut very short, thin-framed glasses, no makeup. When she saw Old Zheng come in she didn’t stand up, only nodded and said, “Mr. Zheng. Sit.”

Old Zheng sat down and ordered a green tea. Dr. Xiao said, “I’ve read your proposal. The overall approach is fine, but there’s one place I need to ask you about first.”

Old Zheng said, “Go ahead.”

Dr. Xiao said, “What you’ve written as ‘infrared preprocessing plus AI enhancement’ — the AI enhancement part, what’s the actual architecture? I read the proposal you attached, and it’s very vague. I need to know whether you’re taking an off-the-shelf model and slapping it on, or whether you’ve really adapted it to infrared data.”

Old Zheng picked up his tea and drank. He knew what this question meant. On defense projects, a lot of companies writing proposals treated “AI” as a basket to throw everything into; in reality they’d just run an open-source YOLO and call it done, never mind how well it worked. Dr. Xiao was asking a real question, and she wanted an answer.

He said, “Dr. Xiao, let me give you the conclusion first. We don’t intend to use anything off the shelf. Infrared images aren’t like visible-light images. Visible light has texture, color, edges. The backbone YOLO trains on visible light — bring it over to infrared and the response patterns of the shallow features are completely wrong. Targets in infrared images are imaged by temperature difference; interference from background thermal radiation is much greater than in visible light, the signal-to-noise ratio is low, and small targets are easily lost during downsampling.”

Dr. Xiao put down the cup in her hand. She looked at Old Zheng. “Go on.”

Old Zheng continued, “Our approach is to add a lightweight feature-enhancement module before the detection head, to preprocess for the low contrast and thermal noise of infrared images. Specifically, instead of feeding the raw image straight into the network, we first do a multi-scale local contrast enhancement to ‘pull’ the target region out of the background thermal radiation. That step doesn’t need deep learning — traditional filtering methods can do it, with little computation, so deploying it at the edge won’t add latency. Then at the feature-extraction stage, we plan to use a dual-branch structure: one branch initialized with weights pretrained on visible light, the other starting from random initialization and learning infrared features specifically. The two branches are fused at the intermediate layers, but the fusion weights aren’t fixed — we let the network learn them itself.”

Dr. Xiao said, “What you’re solving is the domain gap between infrared and visible light. But for this ‘dual-branch fusion’ of yours, training needs paired visible-light data. Where are you getting paired data?”

Old Zheng said, “For the training stage we pretrain on public paired datasets — KAIST or DroneVehicle would both do. At deployment only the infrared branch runs; the visible-light branch is cut off once training’s done. That way training gets the benefit of the visible light’s auxiliary information, and inference relies on infrared alone.”

Dr. Xiao was silent for a few seconds. She took a pen and paper from the bag behind her, drew a circle on it and said, “This approach of yours has the same logic as that InfraNet paper. RGB assists in training, and only IR is kept for inference.”

Old Zheng said, “Right. I’ve read that paper. Their QualGate module uses auxiliary features to control how strongly the RGB intervenes in the IR. The idea’s right. But I think their gating mechanism is on the complicated side; deployed on an edge chip, it may not run. We plan to make a lighter version, using a simple attention weight that dynamically adjusts the fusion strength according to the infrared branch’s confidence. When the infrared branch is highly confident, it listens less to the visible light; when its confidence is low, it listens a bit more.”

Dr. Xiao said, “Have you considered noise-equivalent temperature difference? The detector you’ve chosen — what’s its NETD?”

Old Zheng said, “Our proposal specifies an uncooled vanadium oxide detector, with NETD kept within 30 millikelvin. If your academy has a designated model, we’ll go with yours.”

Dr. Xiao said, “Thirty mK is enough. But you need to watch out: a low NETD doesn’t necessarily mean good image quality. The optics’ F-number, the focal-plane array’s fill factor, and the denoising algorithm downstream all affect the final image. That ‘local contrast enhancement’ preprocessing of yours — if you don’t handle it well, it’ll amplify the noise too.”

Old Zheng said, “That’s why we’ve added a stage of adaptive denoising after preprocessing. We use a guided filter, with the enhanced image as the guide image, to filter the original infrared image. That way the edges are preserved and the noise is suppressed. It’s less computation than a bilateral filter, so it suits real time.”

Dr. Xiao looked at him for a few seconds. Then she put down her pen, leaned back in her chair and said, “Mr. Zheng, have you done infrared before?”

Old Zheng said, “Not night-vision devices. I’ve done data processing for satellite remote sensing. The underlying stuff is the same. Image enhancement, feature extraction, edge deployment — the logic is much the same.”

Dr. Xiao said, “Then you learn pretty fast.”

Old Zheng said, “I read up for a few days. Wherever I’m wrong, point it out.”

Dr. Xiao said, “So far I haven’t heard anything wrong. One question: after your dual branch finishes training and you cut off the visible-light branch, does the IR branch’s detection head need to be fine-tuned again?”

Old Zheng said, “It does. Once it’s cut off and the IR branch runs on its own, the distribution will shift. We plan to do a round of small-learning-rate fine-tuning on pure infrared data after the cut, to realign the detection head.”

Dr. Xiao said, “Is there enough data for fine-tuning?”

Old Zheng said, “There is. The scenarios in your project are relatively fixed — night vision, low light, clearly defined target types. We don’t need to generalize to every scenario, only to be stable in this one. If there isn’t enough data we can synthesize some, use thermal simulation to generate supplementary samples.”

Dr. Xiao didn’t press any further. She picked up her teacup, drank, put it down and said, “Mr. Zheng, I’ve been in two meetings with people from your company, and to be honest, I didn’t have high hopes before. I’ve seen plenty of people who write beautiful proposals and then have a pile of problems when it’s time to deliver. But what you’ve said today is what someone who does the work says.”

Old Zheng said, “I am someone who does the work. People who write proposals say the polite things. People who do the work talk straight.”

The corner of Dr. Xiao’s mouth moved — whether it counted as a smile, it was hard to say. She said, “All right. Revise the technical proposal for this project along the lines you described today and send it to me. If I look it over and it’s fine, everything after that will be easy to push forward.”

The real business was done. Old Zheng put his documents away, and the two of them sat a while longer. Dr. Xiao asked, “Mr. Zheng, apart from work, what do you usually do?”

Old Zheng said, “Smoke, drink tea, have a drink now and then. No real hobbies.”

Dr. Xiao said, “Then your life’s pretty dull.”

Old Zheng said, “And you?”

Dr. Xiao said, “I sing.”

Old Zheng was taken aback. Dr. Xiao saw the look on his face and said, “What, a PhD can’t sing?”

Old Zheng said, “No, it’s not that. I just didn’t expect it.”

Dr. Xiao said, “I go to KTV once a week, by myself. Sing for two hours, come out and get on with things.”

Old Zheng said, “Sing by yourself?”

Dr. Xiao said, “Sing by myself. No fighting anyone for the mic, no listening to people sing off-key, sing whatever I want. It’s great.”

Old Zheng looked at her. As she talked, her face still had that expressionless look, but when she got to singing, something different came into her eyes.

He said, “Then let’s go together sometime. I’d like to hear what a PhD sounds like singing.”

Dr. Xiao glanced at him and said, “Do you sing off-key?”

Old Zheng said, “Don’t know. I haven’t sung in ages.”

Dr. Xiao said, “Then let’s go tonight. There’s one right across the street.”

Old Zheng said, “Tonight?”

Dr. Xiao said, “You’re not going back to Xi’an tonight, are you?”

Old Zheng said, “No.”

Dr. Xiao stood up, zipped up her jacket and said, “Let’s go. You paid for the tea, I’ll pay for the singing. That’s fair.”

By the time Old Zheng got back to the hotel it was ten at night. The car reached the hotel, he got out, and standing at the entrance he sent Cong Shasha a WeChat: “I’m here.”

Cong Shasha replied: “I’ll come down and get you.”

Two minutes later she came out of the elevator with the room card in her hand, in hotel slippers, a coat thrown over her. She saw him, came over and handed him the room card. Shasha asked, “Go well?”

“Pretty well.”

“Did you get her?”

“Got her.”

“Not bad,” Shasha said with a laugh.

The two of them got into the elevator. Cong Shasha swiped the card and pressed the floor. As the elevator went up, she turned her head to look at him and said, “You’ve been drinking?”

Old Zheng said, “A couple of beers at the KTV.”

Cong Shasha said, “Dr. Xiao sings?”

Old Zheng said, “She sings pretty well.”

Cong Shasha smiled and asked nothing more. The elevator arrived, the two of them got out, and the corridor was very quiet. Cong Shasha walked ahead, swiped open the door, went in first and turned the lights on halfway.

Old Zheng followed her in, took off his jacket, tossed it on the little sofa and leaned back on the sofa himself. Cong Shasha came over, sat down in front of him and reached out to undo his shirt buttons one by one. Old Zheng didn’t move, just watched her. Halfway down she looked up and said, “Not tired, are you?”

Old Zheng said, “Not tired.”

Cong Shasha finished with the buttons, helped him out of the shirt, and pressed her lips to him.

What came after, neither of them spoke. Only the bedside lamp was on, its light falling on the covers. There was only heavy breathing, the rustle of the bedding, and the woman’s cries. After half an hour, the room went quiet.

Cong Shasha spoke first. She said, “Brother Yong, I heard something today.”

Old Zheng said, “What?”

Cong Shasha said, “Section Chief Wang at the Seventh Academy told me this afternoon. Zhang Deming’s been taken away — the man who used to be a top leader at the Third Academy. Supposedly it has to do with Yuanwang. And word is Gao Fei’s been placed under detention for investigation too.”

Old Zheng said nothing. He lay there looking at the ceiling.

The next morning Old Zheng woke very early.

Cong Shasha was still asleep, her back to him, her breathing even. He got up quietly, dressed, went to the window and opened the curtains a little. Beijing in the morning: the sky gray, the traffic below already thickening.

He stood at the window, lit a cigarette and took two drags. What was running through his head was still what Cong Shasha had said the night before — Zhang Deming taken away, Gao Fei detained. He thought for a while, then took out his phone and scrolled to Diao Kexin’s WeChat. The last time he’d talked with her was in that restaurant, when she was big with child and he’d said, “Just today, I don’t hate you.”

He typed a line: “Are you in Beijing? How have you been lately?”

When he’d sent it, he put the phone on the windowsill and went on smoking. No one replied. Maybe Diao Kexin had been taken in too — she’d handled all of these things. If they found problems on that side, Diao Kexin couldn’t escape being implicated either. Just as he was thinking this, halfway through his cigarette, the phone lit up. Diao Kexin: “Nursing the baby. You’re in Beijing?”

Old Zheng replied: “I am. Shall we meet?”

Diao Kexin replied: “This afternoon. I’m leaving tomorrow.”

Old Zheng looked at the words “leaving tomorrow” for a while and replied with an “Okay.”

At two in the afternoon the two of them met at a teahouse near Diao Kexin’s home. Old Zheng got there first, asked for a private room toward the back and ordered a pot of Pu’er. When Diao Kexin pushed the door open and came in, he looked up and was taken aback.

She’d slimmed down again — a whole lot slimmer than when he’d seen her in the Sixth Academy’s parking lot. The flesh was gone from her face, her chin was pointed. She wore a dark gray cashmere coat over a black turtleneck, and perhaps because she was nursing, her breasts were a size bigger. Her hair was cut short, neat, not permed, not dyed, her face bare. She carried a smallish handbag — not one of the LVs and Chanels of before, just an ordinary little leather bag of no particular brand.

She saw Old Zheng and smiled, a very faint smile, and said, “Brother Yong.”

Old Zheng said, “Sit. Is it true about Gao Fei? How far has it gone?”

Diao Kexin nodded. “Detention. Zhang Deming’s been arrested. Gao Fei and Zhang Deming have known each other more than ten years. They’ve done a lot of projects together.”

Old Zheng said, “Are you in trouble?”

Diao Kexin said, “I handled things. If anyone in there gives up my name, I won’t get away.”

Old Zheng said, “And you can still leave?”

Diao Kexin said, “For now I can still leave. I just got back from Hong Kong yesterday, after sorting out the money and the trust.”

Old Zheng said, “You’ve got money, though?”

Diao Kexin said, “I do.”

Old Zheng said, “Where are you going?”

Diao Kexin: “London. I’ve got a classmate there, a local — we were together back when I was studying. I’ll stay in a place of his that’s sitting empty for a while first.”

Old Zheng said, “Living in England can’t be cheap.”

Diao Kexin glanced at him and didn’t answer directly. She said, “Brother Yong, you don’t need to worry about that. I’ve got enough money. After the baby was born, Gao Fei set aside a sum for me overseas, and with what I’ve saved all these years, it’s enough that I won’t have to worry for the rest of my life.”

Old Zheng said, “I never asked — did you have a boy or a girl?”

Diao Kexin said, “Another girl.”

Old Zheng said, “Girls are good. I always wanted a daughter.” After a pause he said, “Before you go, is there anything I can help with?”

Diao Kexin looked at him for a while. She said, “Brother Yong, there’s nothing to help with. Before I left, I just wanted to see you once. Now I’ve seen you, I’ve got no regrets.”

Diao Kexin said, “Brother Yong, I hear you’re doing well these days.”

Old Zheng said, “Still a hired hand. Born to work for other people.”

Diao Kexin said, “One day you’ll do it for yourself. You will, for sure.”

Old Zheng picked up the teapot and topped up her tea. “When are you leaving?”

Diao Kexin said, “Tomorrow afternoon’s flight. Heathrow.”

Old Zheng said, “I’ll see you off.”

Diao Kexin said, “No need. My brother’s taking me to the airport.”

Old Zheng said, “Then take care of yourself.”

Diao Kexin said, “You too.”

Diao Kexin arrived at Capital Airport at a little after two the next afternoon.

Her brother drove, and dropped her at the Terminal 3 departures level. In the trunk were three big twenty-eight-inch suitcases. When they got out, her brother helped push the cases. She had a backpack on and pushed a stroller, in which a little pink baby lay snug and comfortable. Her older daughter walked beside her with a little school backpack on, asking the whole way, “Mummy, where are we going?”

Diao Kexin said, “To where Mummy used to go to school.”

Her brother helped her push the cases to the check-in counter and check them. The clerk behind the counter tapped at the keyboard for a while, looked up and said, “Passport, ma’am.”

Check-in done, Diao Kexin hugged her brother. Her brother left, and she joined the line to go through border control.

The officer at border control didn’t even look up as she took the three crimson passports Diao Kexin handed over. The officer flipped through them a couple of times, tapped at her keyboard for a while; her expression didn’t change, but her movements slowed. She said, “Ma’am, please wait a moment. There’s a bit of a problem with the system.”

Diao Kexin said, “What’s wrong?”

The officer said, “Please wait.”

Diao Kexin stood at the counter holding her baby, waiting.

Two men in uniform, about thirty, came over, took the passports, looked at them, looked at Diao Kexin, and said, “Ma’am, would you mind stepping over to the side with me?”

Diao Kexin said, “What’s this about?”

The supervisor said, “There’s something we need to check with you. It’ll only take a few minutes of your time.”

Diao Kexin put the baby in the stroller and, with her older daughter following behind, went with him to a small room set aside at the side. A female officer came out and took the older girl’s hand: “Come on, come with me and wait for Mummy over here.”

In the small room, the stroller still at Diao Kexin’s side, one of them said, “Ma’am, there’s an alert on your passport information in the system. We need to contact the relevant department to verify it. Please have a seat for now and wait a moment.” He went out of the room, and the other one sat on a stool a step away from Diao.

Diao Kexin sat down. There was no expression on her face. She knew the time had come. “What will happen to my children?”

The man beside her said, “We still need to verify some information. Please wait a moment.”

Diao Kexin said, “My brother’s outside. Can I call him?”

Just then the uniformed man who’d gone out came back in with two other men she’d never seen before. “Diao Kexin? You can’t leave the country. We’re taking you back to be investigated.”

She stood up. “Can I contact my brother?”

“No. You can’t use your phone anymore. We’ll contact him for you.”
