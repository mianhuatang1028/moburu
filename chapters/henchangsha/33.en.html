Ziyu

Chapter Thirty-Three: Ziyu

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Ziyu” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Eastern Mountains”), the Ci River is full of ziyu, shaped like crucian carp, each with one head and ten bodies. As the saying goes: one head and ten bodies — every matter has to be handled on its own.

Two months went by quickly.

Delivery on the Sixth Academy project went more smoothly than expected. Zhang Hailu kept a tight eye on production scheduling; on the supply-chain side, Zhu Yiwen fronted materials and the Dongguan suppliers cooperated too. After the first batch of samples was confirmed, mass production went ahead without any major hitch. The Sixth Academy passed it at acceptance, and the first development payment, six million, landed in Xinghuan’s account.

The day the money arrived, Zheng Zhiyong sat in his office looking at the figure in online banking for a long time. He called in finance and went through it item by item — suppliers’ payments, staff salaries, next month’s expenses — and when he’d gone through it all, there was still some slack left on the books to work with. He leaned back in his chair and let out a long breath.

Wang Renhou in Dongguan kept his word too. After the second batch of fan-light motors was delivered, the commission came through as originally agreed, sixty fen a unit, not a cent short — 1.8 million transferred over. Wang Renhou said, laughing, on the phone, “Mr. Zheng, the way you do things, I can rest easy. There are more orders coming, and I’ll come to you for them.”

On Wu You’s side, after the project was delivered, he gave Old Zheng a one-off bonus of two hundred thousand — “hard-earned money for winning the first order,” he called it. It wasn’t much, but the meaning came across: Wu You was using it to tell him, you’ve done well, and I’ve seen it.

With those three sums together, Old Zheng’s money pressure eased completely. He paid back Lu You’s hundred thousand, cleared his credit cards and the small loans he’d owed, and still had a bit of slack left in his account to work with.

That evening he was alone in the fourth-floor dormitory. He opened a bottle of red, sat at the table and looked out at the streetlamps in the park. Cong Shasha was in Beijing seeing clients and hadn’t come back. Sitting there on his own, he suddenly felt a little empty.

These two months, things between him and Cong Shasha had been on-and-off, close one moment and distant the next. No one at the company had noticed. By day the two of them talked and laughed in the office as they should, no different from an ordinary boss and subordinate. At night sometimes she came up to the fourth floor and sometimes she didn’t. When she came she didn’t say much — they ate, they drank, sometimes they made love and sometimes they didn’t, and when they did, they didn’t linger over it but each went to sleep. The next morning she’d get up first, dress, go downstairs and get on with whatever she had to do. There was a tacit understanding between them, like putting something into a box and closing the lid, and neither of them would touch it.

Sometimes he woke in the middle of the night, looked at Cong Shasha asleep beside him, and thought of Diao Kexin. Thought of how, when Diao Kexin slept beside him, she always curled up, buried her face in the hollow of his shoulder, rested her hand on his chest, her fingers pressing, again and again. Cong Shasha wasn’t like that. Cong Shasha slept very properly, lying flat, or turned on her side with her back to him, her hands at her sides. She wasn’t clingy like Diao Kexin, and she didn’t whisper soft things in his ear the way Diao Kexin did. She was more like a partner: partners in work by day, partners in bed by night, everything clear, nothing dragged out, nothing owed.

Sometimes Zheng Zhiyong thought this was fine. Sometimes he felt something was missing.

That night he drank half the bottle of red and got a bit tipsy, lay on the bed scrolling his phone. Scrolling through Douyin, he suddenly landed in a livestream.

The streamer was a woman, chatting with people on camera, all smiles. Zheng Zhiyong’s finger stopped on the screen and didn’t swipe on.

The woman looked almost exactly like Diao Kexin.

Not just looked like her — exactly like her. The shape of her face, her brows and eyes, the curve of the corners of her mouth when she smiled, the way she tilted her head slightly when she talked, even the dimple that came and went on her left cheek — all in the same place. Zheng Zhiyong stared at the screen for a long time. He was half sober now.

He brought the phone closer, then held it farther away, looking again and again. He knew it couldn’t be Diao Kexin. Diao Kexin wouldn’t livestream on Douyin; Diao Kexin was in Changsha now, Gao Fei’s wife, the boss’s wife at Yuanwang. But this woman looked so very like her. Like her enough that the stone he’d been pressing down in his heart for so long was suddenly shoved up from below.

He went into her profile and read her bio: Chengdu, freelance, likes singing and chatting. He went through her videos, one after another, until the middle of the night. In the videos she was singing in some, cooking in some, sitting in the sun on a balcony in others. Every frame was like Diao Kexin, and every frame wasn’t her.

He went back to the livestream; she was still on. He watched for a while, then sent a gift. At his first gift she read out his ID and said, “Thank you, big brother.” Listening to that voice, his heart gave a jolt. The voice was like hers too. Not exactly the same, but that soft lilt at the end of a phrase, with a bit of a southern accent — that was like hers.

He sent another gift. Once, twice, three times. That night he didn’t know how many gifts he sent. By the time he came to his senses, he had become the top gifter in that livestream.

Before ending the stream the streamer made a point of reading out his ID: “Thank you, my top gifter, big brother. Good night, big brother.”

He lay on the bed looking at the words “Livestream has ended” on the screen, and suddenly felt a bit ridiculous. He rolled over, put the phone face down on the pillow and closed his eyes. But that face stayed in his head.

The next day he got a private message. It was from the streamer: “Big brother, thank you for your support last night. Would it be all right to add you on WeChat?”

He looked at the message for a long time. Then he sent her his WeChat ID.

After they added each other on WeChat, the two of them chatted for several days. Her name was Xiaoya. She was from Chengdu, twenty-nine, and had been livestreaming for two years. She talked very directly, without beating around the bush, and asked him what he did. He said he was in engineering. She said, “An engineering boss — no wonder you’re so generous.” He smiled and didn’t explain.

After a few days of chatting she said, “Big brother, when are you coming to Chengdu to have some fun? I’ll treat you to hotpot.”

He looked at this line, his finger stopped on the screen. He knew what the line meant. He knew too what would happen if he went. He thought of Diao Kexin. Thought of the woman who’d walked arm in arm with him by the Xiang River, the woman who’d turned back on the Zhangbei grassland and said to him, “If one day you’re not by my side, I’ll miss you,” the woman who’d said “Yes” on the phone, flat and final.

He replied: “Next week. I’ll come to Chengdu next week.”

When he’d sent it, he put down the phone, leaned back in his chair and lit a cigarette.

Outside, the Xi’an sky had gone fully dark. He smoked, looking out the window, and that indescribable something in his heart came churning up again. He knew he shouldn’t go. But he wanted to.

Summer comes early in Chengdu.

When Zheng Zhiyong came out of Shuangliu Airport, the hot wind hit him in the face. It wasn’t like Xi’an’s dry heat; Chengdu’s heat was humid and clung to the skin. He wore a light gray linen shirt, the sleeves rolled to his forearms, dark casual trousers below and a pair of brown loafers. This outfit was something Cong Shasha had dragged him to SKP to buy two months earlier; he’d grumbled about the price at the time, and Cong Shasha had said, “You’re the boss now. When you see clients you’ve got to look the part.” He’d put on a fair bit of weight. Since the Sixth Academy project was delivered, there’d been more business dinners and more drinking, and his belly had started to bulge a little. He’d grown a beard, kept neatly trimmed, and there was a touch of gray at his temples. He looked a few years older than he was — rather like the kind of middle-aged man who has “done a few things and has something behind him.”

Xiaoya had sent him a location on WeChat: a hotpot restaurant near Chunxi Road. He took a taxi over, and when he got to the door he saw a woman standing under the streetlamp outside, looking down at her phone.

She wore a white short-sleeved knit top, a light blue denim skirt and a pair of little white sneakers, her hair tied loosely in a ponytail, a few loose strands falling by her ears. She looked up, saw him and smiled.

Zheng Zhiyong stood there, stunned for a moment.

Diao Kexin. At first glance, those three characters were still what jumped into his head. At second glance, still Diao Kexin — that there could be two people in this world so alike! And yet he knew for certain she wasn’t. She was younger than Diao Kexin, with a collagen fullness in her face that Diao Kexin didn’t have. She was a little taller than Diao Kexin, a little fairer, a little slimmer. When she smiled there was a dimple on her left cheek, in exactly the same spot as Diao Kexin’s, but the smile was relaxed, with no calculation in it. Diao Kexin’s smile was always held back, always leaving you to guess and wonder and take the bait yourself. This girl’s smile was wide open — the smile of someone happy to see a “big brother” who’d come a long way.

She came over, tipped her head back to look at him, and said, “Big brother, you look bigger than in videos.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “You look slimmer than in videos.”

Xiaoya laughed out loud and said, “Come on, I’ve booked a table. This is a hotpot place locals queue for — tourists never find it.”

The restaurant was on the second floor, loud with voices, the pots bubbling with red chili oil, the air thick with Sichuan pepper and beef tallow. Xiaoya picked a table by the window, pushed the menu over to him and said, “Big brother, you order. I don’t know if you eat spicy.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “I eat spicy. You order — you know Chengdu.”

Xiaoya didn’t make a fuss about it either, and whipped through ordering a whole tableful: tripe, duck intestines, aorta, brains, little “rat fish,” crispy pork, ice jelly. She ordered very briskly, talking to the waiter in a Chengdu accent, soft, the ends of her phrases lilting upward. Zheng Zhiyong watched her and thought of how Diao Kexin ordered. Diao Kexin always asked him first, “What would you like to eat?” and then “while she was at it” ordered a few things that were expensive and good. This girl was different. When she’d finished ordering, she looked up and said, “Big brother, do you want something to drink? I’ll have a few beers with you.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Sure.”

The beer came, and they clinked glasses. Xiaoya said, “Big brother, let me introduce myself first. I’m Xiaoya, Chengdu born and bred, twenty-nine already. I’ve got a junior college diploma — studied accounting. After I graduated I worked two years at a little company, then got married and had a baby, and stopped working. I got divorced last year. The kid’s with me — she’s two, a girl. Right now I live at my parents’, my mum looks after the baby in the daytime, and I livestream at night to make a bit of a living.”

When she said all this her tone was very flat, as if she were talking about someone else. There was no sense of grievance, nothing like complaining. She picked up a piece of tripe with her chopsticks, swished it in the pot, popped it in her mouth, chewed a couple of times and said, “What about you, big brother?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “My name’s Zheng Zhiyong. I’m from Xi’an, I’m in engineering. Divorced; my kid’s with his mother. Last year I left a company and set up a little company of my own, doing defense supply work.”

Xiaoya said, “Defense — no wonder there’s something about you… I can’t put my finger on it. Really steady.”

Zheng Zhiyong smiled. “Steady? I’m just knocking around.”

Xiaoya said, “From how you looked in the videos, you seemed like one of those really successful people. Honest, I’m not sucking up. Look at your clothes — they don’t look cheap, but you’re not showing off either. My dad used to have a little business, and I’ve seen those bosses — some of them all decked out in gold and silver, they’d hang their whole fortune on their bodies if they could. You’re not like that.”

Zheng Zhiyong glanced down at his linen shirt and remembered Cong Shasha saying, as she picked it out at SKP, “This color suits you — it’s not flashy.” He smiled and didn’t answer.

Xiaoya said, “Big brother, how long have you been divorced?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “A year and a bit.”

Xiaoya said, “It’s been nearly a year for me too.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Why did you get divorced?”

Xiaoya said, “My husband cheated on me while I was pregnant. At first I forgave him, but then he kept on doing it, so I got divorced, didn’t I.”

Her tone was as light as if she were telling someone else’s story, without any sense of pain. Zheng Zhiyong looked at her.

“I’ve made my peace with it. Life goes on, the kid needs raising. I didn’t have a job, so I started livestreaming. At first nobody watched, then slowly I got a few fans, and I can make a bit from gifts — enough to buy formula for the baby.”

Here she stopped, looked at Zheng Zhiyong and said, “Big brother, all that money you sent me last week — honestly, I was really surprised. In all the time I’ve been streaming, I’ve never been sent that much. I’m really happy I got to meet you. But why did you send me so much?”

Zheng Zhiyong was taken aback. He hadn’t expected her to bring this up. He said, “No reason. I just watched your stream and thought it was fun.”

Xiaoya said, “Big brother, don’t fib to me. I’m a pretty good judge of people. The rhythm you were sending gifts at that night wasn’t someone doing it for kicks. It was one at a time, like you were thinking about something. I just want to know — what were you thinking about?”

Zheng Zhiyong picked up his beer, took a drink and said, “If I told you you’re the spitting image of my ex-wife, would you believe me?”

Xiaoya said, “Seriously?”

Zheng Zhiyong handed her his phone and showed her Diao Kexin’s profile picture.

Oh my God, the spitting image! Xiaoya thought so herself — they were incredibly alike. “This is way too crazy,” Xiaoya exclaimed. “Can I meet this sister?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “The two of us haven’t said a word to each other in almost a year.”

That was true enough. Whether it was his ex-wife or Diao Kexin, Old Zheng barely had any contact with either now. As for his ex-wife, apart from the money he transferred her every month, even his son wouldn’t pay him any attention.

After that the two of them talked about lighter things. About Chengdu, about Xi’an, about which hotpot places were best, about funny things in the livestream. Xiaoya was fun to listen to. She told him about the strange big brothers in her livestream: some sent gifts the second they arrived, some wrote poems in the comments every day, some sent private messages asking to meet and got blocked. She told it all with great animation, and Zheng Zhiyong laughed several times as he listened.

After hotpot the two of them walked in the street. In Chengdu in summer, the evenings were a little cooler than the days. The leaves of the plane trees along the street were very big, and the streetlight leaked down through the gaps and fell on the ground in broken pieces. Xiaoya walked beside him with a very light step, sometimes pointing at a shop by the road and saying “The skewers here are good,” or “I’ve been eating the ice jelly here since I was little.” Zheng Zhiyong walked along with her, listening, and suddenly it all felt a bit unreal.

He thought of a few months back, alone in that little one-bedroom in Xi’an, smoking, feeling his life was over. Now he was walking down a street in Chengdu beside a twenty-six-year-old girl with a dimple when she smiled, telling him which ice jelly in Chengdu was best. He felt as if he were dreaming.

As they walked, Zheng Zhiyong said, “Shall we go to the Wuhou Shrine? I really love Zhuge Liang.”

Xiaoya said, “Sure, the Wuhou Shrine isn’t far — it’s real close, you know.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Let’s go, then.”

Xiaoya said, “I’ll take you in for a look around. There aren’t many people this late. It’s lovely.”

They got in just before the Wuhou Shrine closed. The sky was darkening a little, and the red walls and bamboo shadows outside looked very still at night. Xiaoya said, “When I was little my dad brought me here all the time.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “This is my first time.”

Xiaoya said, “What is it you like about Zhuge Liang?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Is there anyone who doesn’t like Zhuge Liang?” And he shook his head and laughed.

When Xiaoya saw he wasn’t saying anything more, she tilted her head to look at him, smiling, and didn’t say anything either.

Then she turned her face forward again, looked down and said, “My dad passed away last year. Heart attack — he went very quickly. After he went, I got divorced. My mum says I’ve got bad luck, that everything came at me in that one year. I tell her it’s not bad luck. It was just time.”

Zheng Zhiyong looked at her. As she said this her face stayed the same, perfectly calm, her voice with no great grief or anguish in it, as if again she were talking about something that had nothing to do with her. Zheng Zhiyong turned toward her and took her hand, and Xiaoya turned to face him too. The two of them stood there under the pavilion at the Wuhou Shrine, face to face, holding hands, looking at each other, saying nothing.

When it was dark, the two of them walked slowly from the Wuhou Shrine back to Chunxi Road, and after a while more Xiaoya said, “Big brother, I should go home. The baby’s getting a vaccination tomorrow morning, and my mum can’t manage on her own.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “I’ll take you.”

Xiaoya said, “No need, I’ll just get a cab.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “I’ll take you.”

Xiaoya glanced at him and didn’t argue anymore.

The car pulled up at the gate of her housing compound. It was an old compound, the paint flaking off the iron gate, with a twenty-four-hour convenience store by the entrance, its lights still on. Xiaoya got out, stood by the car door and bent down to look at him. “Big brother, thank you for today. I haven’t been this happy in a long time.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Me neither.”

Xiaoya said, “Will you still be in Chengdu tomorrow?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Yes. I leave the day after.”

Xiaoya said, “Then let me treat you to dinner tomorrow. You treated today; tomorrow it’s my turn.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Let’s not meet tomorrow. I’ve got other things to do.”

Xiaoya smiled. “All right then.” She turned and walked into the compound, took a few steps, looked back and waved to him. Then her figure disappeared into the shadow of the stairwell.

Zheng Zhiyong sat in the car looking at the compound gate for a long time. Then he told the driver to go back to the hotel.

Back at the hotel he showered, lay on the bed and picked up his phone. Xiaoya had sent a WeChat: “Big brother, are you back at the hotel? It was really nice to meet you today.”

He replied: “You too. Get some sleep.”

When he’d sent it he put the phone on the nightstand and looked at the ceiling.

Outside the window, Chengdu still had its lights on into the night. Lying on the hotel bed, he went over everything from that evening: the red oil of the hotpot, Xiaoya’s smile, the bamboo shadows at the Wuhou Shrine, that plain, even tone when she told him about all the sad things in her past.

He thought: this girl isn’t like Diao Kexin. And she isn’t like Cong Shasha either. There’s no calculation in her, and no ambition to climb. She’s just an ordinary person, divorced, with a child, hoping for a settled life. When she looks at him, her eyes look straight at him — no dodging, no going around.

But then he thought: and what about himself? What kind of person was he? A divorced middle-aged man who’d been in debt, been used by a woman, and had only just got back on his feet. What right did he have to get involved with a girl ten years younger than him, with a two-year-old child? And yet this girl looked almost exactly like Diao Kexin — not even a TV drama would make something like that up. Was heaven trying to give him some hint, some guidance, again?

He rolled over and buried his face in the pillow.

After a while he picked up his phone again, went to Xiaoya’s WeChat, and looked at that “It was really nice to meet you today” for a long time. Then he put the phone down and closed his eyes.

Outside, the Chengdu night was very still.
