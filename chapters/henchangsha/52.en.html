Xiao

Chapter Fifty-Two: Xiao

Translation first published: 2026-11-13 23:36 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-13 23:36 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Xiao” — in the Classic of Mountains and Seas, the Western Mountains, on Mount Yuci there is a beast shaped like an ape, with long arms and a gift for throwing: its name is Xiao. In the Northern Mountains, on Mount Liangqu, there is a bird shaped like a Kua Fu, with four wings, one eye and a dog’s tail, also called Xiao, whose cry is like a magpie’s. An ape’s face and long arms, a voice like a magpie — when hidden sparks scatter in the dark, beneath what looks like silent ruin, a great fire that will devour them all is already gathering.

After Zhang Hailu tendered his resignation, Zheng Zhiyong told Wu Peng to come to the company first thing the next morning to talk it over.

He himself had barely slept all night. He had forced himself to lie down and close his eyes, but a thousand thoughts crowded into his head and would not still for a moment; by five in the morning he could no longer bear to stay in bed. At five o’clock in deep winter the whole world was still sunk in heavy, pitch-black night, yet he had not a trace of sleep left in him. So he washed and dressed, went down from the fourth floor where he lived to the second floor, and switched on every light across the whole floor of offices, inside and out. The corridor was empty; a cleaning cart stood quietly in the passage by the lift.

Old Zheng walked up to the glass door of the technical department and stood looking through the glass for a while.

Several of the workstations were already empty.

Xiao Chen’s and Engineer Li’s desks had been cleared spotless — even the screwdrivers, adaptors and data cables they usually left lying in the drawers were gone. Dr. Lan’s place was just as it had always been: the monitor still on, several unprinted algorithm test reports left on the desk. The door to Zhang Hailu’s office stood ajar, and the light inside was off too.

Old Zheng pushed the door open, walked over to Dr. Lan’s workstation, picked up the test report and flipped through a couple of pages.

There were a few marks in the report, in the notation Dr. Lan always used. Old Zheng could read them, and knew what they meant. Over the past few years, many a technical problem had been solved by the few of them sitting here, working it through and revising it again and again until at last they found the way out.

He put the report back on the desk.

The office was very quiet just then — so quiet it made a man uneasy.

The two desks joined together at the very back by the window were Zhang Hailu’s. His workstation was exactly as it had been every other day: on the partition of the innermost desk, a photograph of Hailu’s family of three; two large monitors; beneath them a heap of technical material and paper documents, and a jumble of mechanical modules and samples of every kind. The desk was piled full. There was an ashtray with a little water in it, and a great many cigarette ends soaking there.

Old Zheng sat down quietly at Zhang Hailu’s desk, took out a cigarette, and before long had added one freshly made cigarette end to that ashtray.

“Wu Peng — where are you?” Old Zheng could not help sending him a WeChat message. Then he stood by the window again, watching the sky lighten bit by bit, watching the first vehicle drive into the park down below — a white BYD — which turned a corner and headed for another office building in the complex.

Wu Peng arrived early, and the two of them shut the door of Old Zheng’s office.

“They’ve gone. Every one of them.” Zheng stubbed out his cigarette. “I know who did it? Diao Kexin!” Wu Peng thought there was something off about it — after all, the bid for the military-civilian integration project had not come down yet, and it was still unclear which of the two sides would win — and said with a furrowed brow, “But why? The bid hasn’t come down. Yuanwang is all but bankrupt. Hiring a team this expensive now — that’s financial suicide?”

Old Zheng told Wu Peng the conclusion he had reached over a whole night’s thinking: “Diao Kexin doesn’t really care whether these few people can be used on her project once she wins the bid. She wants to drain me dry. She wants me left without a single person. Because she knows that without them, I can’t do it anymore.”

Wu Peng said nothing.

In the meeting between Diao Kexin and Zhang Hailu, she had made Hailu certain that this bid would go to her side; so, after looking over her technical proposal, the simple-hearted Zhang Hailu had believed it at once. But on one point Diao Kexin had not told the truth: she had no need of this technical team at all, and she knew it perfectly well. And Zhang Hailu, with an engineer’s simplicity, had not seen through it. She had never thought that poaching Zhang Hailu, Dr. Lan and the few others could replace the research team of over a hundred at Zhu Yiwen’s twenty-year-old company. In fact, whether or not these people joined Yuanwang, she would go on honouring her agreement with Zhu Yiwen; meeting after meeting, her relationship with him had begun to turn into one of mutual trust. She knew that if she was to keep going in this industry in the years ahead, she must hold fast to Gaoda’s thigh, and the surest way to do that was to keep deepening her tie with Zhu Yiwen himself — to have him making money alongside her, even to let it grow into a love affair. That was the most secret, most unguessable calculation buried in Diao Kexin’s heart.

After that whole sleepless night, Zheng Zhiyong had finally got this thing right! So taking Zhang Hailu’s entire team was the one move Diao Kexin was most set on winning in her return to take charge of Yuanwang — not because she needed them, but because she knew Zheng Zhiyong needed them! Besides, these people had originally been Yuanwang’s own; every one of them had settled and made a home in Xiangcheng, and luring them back to their old employer was, in the nature of things, only right and proper.

The Sixth Academy had always been Diao Kexin and Gao Fei’s home ground. Back when Zhang Hailu joined Xinghuan, he had taken Dr. Lan, Xiao Chen and several other technical mainstays from the company with him, and with Zhu Yiwen changing sides at the critical moment, the Sixth Academy’s “Simulation System and Ancillary Products” project had landed at Xinghuan. It was precisely because Yuanwang lost that bid that its finances worsened further, until step by step it came to liquidation — and all of it was the bitter fruit of Zheng Zhiyong’s determination, back then, to cut off his own arm rather than spare Yuanwang.

When Diao Kexin thought she was about to lose her freedom and leave for Britain, she had once considered laying down every old grievance, right and wrong alike, and having nothing more to do with Zheng Zhiyong. But now she had not only come through safe and sound, she had returned to Yuanwang as its de facto operator, accepted even by the state-owned shareholders; and on the strength of the network of connections she had built up over years at the Sixth Academy, she had won back the greater part of the Sixth Academy’s additional project orders that had been lost before — which was the real reason Old Zheng and his people later lost that share of the orders. After that, through Wang Yan’s introduction, Diao Kexin had called on Zhu Yiwen three times, and made him once again a hidden collaborator on her side.

When Diao Kexin returned to Yuanwang and first made contact with Zhu Yiwen, she was stunned to learn what he was taking from this project. The price Zheng Zhiyong had paid back then to work with Zhu Yiwen and squeeze Yuanwang out had planted a deep fear in her heart. To cut off his own arm rather than let his rival live — Diao Kexin understood that Zheng Zhiyong’s hatred of her could never be what he had said the last time they met: “I don’t hate you anymore.”

Diao Kexin could taste it — Zheng Zhiyong’s hatred of her, that hatred stretching on without end, pure, without a single flaw. Hatred is never the opposite of love; forgetting is. Hatred is love carried on in another form, bearing in it the bent and the intent to destroy everything.

This was how Diao Kexin had grown up: she feared poverty, so she destroyed poverty; she feared betrayal, so she betrayed first; she feared being hurt, so she finished off the other person with a greater hurt — just as she had dealt with Li Fulang all those years ago. Nothing mattered as much as her own survival. She must live, Yuanwang must live, and so Xinghuan and Zheng Zhiyong had to be driven out of this war. There was only this one road, if she wanted to keep herself safe.

Zheng Zhiyong suddenly understood what Diao Kexin was doing. He asked Wu Peng, “The non-compete agreements we signed with them before — do they hold?”

“Mr. Zheng, I’ll go and have our legal colleagues look over everyone’s agreements,” Wu Peng answered.

Zhang Hailu arrived at the company, and Zheng Zhiyong called him into the office.

“Hailu, tell me the truth. All of you leaving together — that can’t just happen. Someone’s been poaching you, hasn’t they, taking the whole team.”

Embarrassment showed on Zhang Hailu’s face. “You know my situation at home as well as I do. My father’s in hospital with a stroke, the child’s been ill, my wife complains at me all day long. And besides — these two years with you, you know as well as I do how little I’ve earned in total. My family has put it plainly: either bring money home, or come home yourself and look after them. I’ve done neither. Keep on like this and my wife will divorce me. The others are much the same, and the company’s situation is no clearer. Truly, we can’t hold on any longer.”

What middle-aged man does not have family duties and money pressures to carry? These were solid, real reasons, not excuses made up on the spot. And precisely because of that, Old Zheng did not know how to blame him; all he could do was rue how little and how late he had made good on the promises he had given the team over the past few years.

In business there is a saying: gather the money and the people scatter; scatter the money and the people gather. The meaning is that the more you share out, the stronger the bond — but at Xinghuan these past few years everyone had been stretched tight. When Old Zheng had heard Zhang Hailu out, he had nothing to say in reply. He thought for a moment, then said:

“Hailu, see if you and Dr. Lan can stay on a while longer. If the bid opening goes our way this time and we land the contract, I’ll draw an advance from the company and pay this year’s bonus before the Lunar New Year — three hundred thousand yuan for you; Dr. Lan, a hundred and fifty thousand for him. The rest you divide among the others according to the budget.”

Zhang Hailu’s face was hesitant. He thought for a moment and said, “Mr. Zheng, I promised you a proper handover, and I won’t let anything slip. The bid opening is only half a month away. That much I give you my word on.”

After Hailu left the office, Old Zheng opened his computer and went through Xinghuan’s project schedule for the next six months again.

The plan, once packed so full, suddenly looked a little absurd.

If the Xi’an project were won, the technical team would have to be quickly staffed and moved down to Shenzhen, with mould engineers arranged for locally as well; if it were lost, the delivery projects already in hand would still be enough to keep the company going. They had run the numbers before: even with no new business at all, the company would show a few million yuan in profit next year. It was worth doing the sums properly, to see whether a sum of money could be set aside — it was time the team had a raise. He and Zhang Hailu had talked it over several times: once the team incentive equity was in place, salaries could go up too, and with the year-end bonus paid out, morale ought to rise higher than it ever had.

On top of that, Wu You had already promised that the 6% team incentive equity would be put in place before the Lunar New Year, that the GP of that Shanghai partnership would be changed over to Zheng Zhiyong, and that Zhang Hailu could receive technical dry shares in Xinghuan there, free of charge.

But now they had no idea what Diao Kexin was offering, and the pull of going home to look after wife and child was stronger than any number of arguments; even if he wanted to keep them, it was no longer something a little more equity or a little bigger bonus could fix.

With the core of the technical team gone, whether those left could carry the work was one question; and even if they could, how was all that technical experience to be recovered — the kind that had never been written down and had lived only inside a few men’s heads?

Old Zheng closed the laptop and leaned back in his chair.

It came to him suddenly how thoroughly, these past few years, he had grown used to treating Zhang Hailu as a reliable answer. Hand the goal to Zhang Hailu, and the technical side need cost him no more thought. The people Zhang Hailu brought, Zhang Hailu managed; Zhang Hailu guaranteed delivery. Old Zheng looked after the clients, the business and the resources; each did his own part, and so Xinghuan had come all this way.

But now that answer was gone.

He picked up the old walnuts from the desk and turned them slowly twice in his hand.

In the office next door, Wu Peng had just come off the phone with the legal department about the details of the non-compete agreements, and afterwards glanced down at his phone.

Shasha had just sent a message: feeling much better today, finally don’t have to lie down anymore.

He smiled and replied, “Eat something at noon. I’ll bring food over tonight and see you.”

After a while, Shasha sent back a sticker.

Wu Peng stood at Shasha’s door with a large bag heavy with Cantonese dim sum. He had driven her to the foot of this building more times than he could count, but this was the first time he had come to the door. The flat was in an old compound with no lift; he climbed all the way to the fifth floor, and by now a thin sheen of sweat had broken out on his forehead.

The door opened. Shasha wore a loose, pale pink flannel nightdress, her hair bundled carelessly into a bun, a few loose strands falling against her cheeks, her face bare of make-up — but her colour was truly much better than the past two days.

“Come in,” she said, turning sideways and shuffling inside in a pair of fluffy slippers. “It’s a bit of a mess — don’t mind it.”

Wu Peng stepped in, and a warm smell came to meet him, laundry liquid mixed with a faint diffuser scent. It was a one-bedroom flat.

To be honest, it really was a little messy — but not the dirty kind of mess; it was the crowdedness peculiar to a girl living alone, full of the marks of a life being lived. On the white shoe cabinet by the entrance stood a tight cluster of blind-box figurines, and beside them hung an enormous strawberry-bear keyring. On the floor, delivery boxes still unopened were stacked in the corner, and next to them stood a small pink folding drying rack with her change of clothes hanging on it.

Into the living room — or the bedroom, for it was one large open room. A double bed spread with a pink-and-white checked duvet took up most of the space; on it were piled two huge plush cushions, a round, roly-poly Kirby, and a Cinnamoroll squashed somewhat out of shape. The rug beside the bed was fluffy pink too, soft underfoot.

Wu Peng set the heavy takeaway boxes on the little coffee table. It was a low round table covered with a floral cloth, and served, most likely, as her dressing table and desk both — for it was crowded in cheerful disorder with skincare bottles (he recognised a few as big-name brands, the rest all cute Japanese and Korean packaging), an open lipstick, a laptop plastered with all sorts of sweet stickers, and a small power bank blinking as it charged.

“Sit wherever you like — I’ll get these out,” Shasha said, crouching by the coffee table to unpack the food.

Wu Peng looked around, and his eyes came to rest beside the TV cabinet. There stood a clear white acrylic shelf, filled entirely with the dolls she had clawed from the machines, ranked in three full tiers. Beside it a feather floor lamp was lit, giving off a warm yellow glow.

The most striking thing in the room was the pale pink fabric sofa against the wall, heaped with clothes of every sort — nightdresses, knitted cardigans, jeans, and the straps of a few lace underthings poking out of the pile. She had plainly taken it for a wardrobe, tossing her everyday changes across it as she went. Next to the sofa stood a half-open white wardrobe, so crammed with hanging clothes that the doors would not quite shut.

In the little open kitchen, a row of cute fridge magnets was stuck to the microwave, with a pink kettle and a set of cartoon-patterned dishes beside it. In the sink, yesterday’s two cups were still soaking.

Looking at all of it, Wu Peng felt a strange sensation rise in him. In this small, slightly untidy space full of pink, plush toys and the clutter of ordinary days, the sales director who moved so effortlessly and calculated so shrewdly at the banquet table had vanished from Shasha. The woman sitting on the rug now, opening food boxes, was like any ordinary university girl.

“What are you staring at? Think my place is a pigsty?” Shasha looked up, shot him a laughing sideways glance, and lifted a steamer of shrimp dumplings out as she spoke.

“No,” Wu Peng said, shaking his head with a smile. He took off his suit jacket and laid it over the pile of clothes, bent his long legs and sat cross-legged on the rug beside the coffee table. “It’s just that this place … it’s very you.”

“What’s that supposed to mean?” Shasha handed him a pair of chopsticks.

“It looks all soft, not an ounce of fight in it — and yet it hides every treasure away in its own nest.” Wu Peng pulled the chopsticks apart, looking at the enormous Cinnamoroll. “This whole flat is covered in your traces.”

Shasha laughed, her eyes bending into two crescents. “The way you say it, you’d think I was some little animal guarding its food.”

“Aren’t you?” Wu Peng picked up a shrimp dumpling, but did not eat it yet; he looked at her. “Out there you fight to drag the prey home, and the moment you’re back you curl up in this pink burrow and go to sleep hugging Kirby.”

Shasha froze for a moment, something flickering in her eyes. She did not contradict him; she only lowered her head and poked at the custard bun in its box, and said softly, “Everyone wants a place that feels safe.”

The room went quiet, with only the faint glow of the feather floor lamp falling on the steaming dim sum on the coffee table, and on the little puppy doll beside it, watching them in silence.

By the next day, the air in the technical department was still that of high altitude and low pressure. Zhang Hailu came to Wu Peng to discuss delivery arrangements for the last project, and toward the end he would always stop, as if there were things he could not conveniently say. Wu Peng for his part was weighing how to broach the non-compete agreements; he had talked it over with Wu You these past few days too. Since Hailu and Dr. Lan would at least still be here until the holiday before the Lunar New Year, and until the bid opening, then once the bonuses were paid — firmness and kindness together — might there still be room to turn their leaving around?

Not long after Hailu went out, Old Zheng came in again. He wasted no time on pleasantries and told Wu Peng to sit down at once.

“Have a look at this first.”

Wu Peng took the papers.

They listed every project Xinghuan currently had in execution, sorted by delivery date, technical lead and remaining workload. Several of the key projects had already been marked beside in red pen.

“If the Xi’an project comes through, the old technical bench certainly won’t be enough,” Old Zheng said. “I’ve also drawn up a plan on the worst assumption — that there’s no way to win them back from leaving.”

Wu Peng turned through it for a while and said, “Then we can’t set the schedule on the old division of labour alone.”

Old Zheng looked up at him. “Right. The old division of labour rested on Mr. Zhang and Dr. Lan and the others still being here. Now the people aren’t certain; if the project comes in and we’re not prepared, we’ll be caught flat-footed in an instant. On the simulation algorithms, the technical proposal is all in our own hands, so we have a foundation — but the plan has to be broken apart,”

“You mean we rebuild the division of labour?” Wu Peng asked.

“Yes. The worst of it now isn’t that we’re short a few people — Hailu’s work was too central; the whole hub of the information flow sat with him. These next few days I’ll go through the projects first, separate the structure from the people and sort it all out again, and get every bit of Zhang Hailu’s working information handed over to me. First I’ll identify the parts of each project that truly cannot be interrupted, then which work must be done by core technical people and which can be taken on by outside resources or others inside. The best thing would be to find two algorithm people to slot in, and make a backup of Dr. Lan’s and Xiao Chen’s work. The people — I’m going to go and talk to them now.”

Wu Peng: “Do you have candidates, Mr. Zheng?”

Old Zheng: “Tomorrow I’m going back to my alma mater, Northwestern Polytechnical University; on Monday I’ll be off to Beijing on a trip. I have a few candidates in mind.”

Wu Peng: “You go and talk to the candidates, Mr. Zheng. I’ll stay here and keep working on Hailu — maybe I can find out from the side whether they were poached. I’ll follow up on getting the incentive equity in place too.”

Old Zheng nodded.

“And one more thing — you’ll need to talk to the Beijing client in advance too. Engineer Li was the one handling delivery; if we swap people abruptly, the client will know something’s gone wrong inside.” Wu Peng reminded him.

At quitting time that evening, Wu Peng sat in his Tesla and phoned Wu You to report how matters had developed that day. He spoke of Old Zheng’s reaction in the office the day before. Old Zheng had plainly already come to suspect Diao Kexin, but could find no solid proof. The family pressures Zhang Hailu spoke of were real enough — yet real as they were, those reasons might not explain the fact that everyone had left at once.

Wu You asked, “And do you think it’s Diao Kexin?”

Wu Peng paused. “Like I just said — the reasons they gave for leaving do exist: family, being away from home, dissatisfaction with pay. Diao Kexin has no real use for these few people. Uncle, I’d like to hear your view.”

“What the enemy wants is where I am weakest.” Wu You said it slowly: “Mr. Zheng’s judgement is most likely right. For Yuanwang to poach people at this moment is pulling the firewood out from under the pot. They aren’t fighting Xinghuan for one city or one patch of ground; they’ve truly made Xinghuan their rival. And that tells us Old Zheng’s work these past nearly two years has begun to carry weight in the industry. That’s a good thing.”

In his heart Wu Peng admired the reach of Wu You’s judgement.

“So what do we do now?”

“Keep Zhang Hailu until the bid opening. Get the equity incentive agreement ready, stamp it and give it to him; once it’s signed it belongs to the few of them. If he won’t sign, that’s all right too — the gesture is that we offered it first.”

Wu You paused.

“If the Xi’an project ends up coming to us, Hailu and the others may well stay. They’re probably wavering even now.”

Wu Peng: “Are you saying you already know the result of the bid?”

Wu You: “I don’t know the result yet. But we’re still at the table.”

Wu Peng: “I’ve just reported Mr. Zheng’s Plan B to you — what do you make of it, Uncle?”

Wu You: “For Old Zheng to take over all of Zhang Hailu’s work is good for us — the projects in hand won’t slip. What we need is to find a very good algorithm team. Tell Mr. Zheng for me that I can introduce candidates too, and let him go and talk to them and judge for himself.” Then he said with a sudden laugh, “The best algorithm teams are all in finance — I’m having them introduce a few people to me.”

“I understand, Uncle,” Wu Peng said.

The call over, Wu Peng sat in the car without starting it at once. The phone screen lit up — a message from Shasha.

“Are you done with work?”

Wu Peng replied, “Just got out. How are you feeling today?”

“Much better. I’m going to fix myself something to eat.”

“Don’t bother — I’ve already ordered takeaway. Wait for me.”

Shasha replied at once: “Okay.”

Wu Peng looked at that one word — Okay — and the corners of his mouth lifted before he knew it.

He set the phone aside and started the car.

The car pulled out of the car park and merged into the traffic after the evening rush. Streetlights fell away one after another, and the city beyond the windows was still busy, as though nothing at all had happened.
