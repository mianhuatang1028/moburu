Chapter Fifty-Three: Lili

“Lili” — in the Classic of Mountains and Seas, the Southern Mountains, on Mount Ju there is a beast shaped like a pig, with spurs on its feet, its cry like a dog's barking: its name is Lili, and wherever it appears the district is much given to earthworks. Truly, when the beast is seen, earth and timber works rise; before the old ground is levelled, new foundations go up. What is called rebuilding has never been a matter of setting fallen things upright again, but of finding anew, upon the ruins, a foundation capable of bearing weight.

Two weeks later, in the afternoon, word came suddenly of the review result for the Xi'an project. Everyone had expected the winning bid to be announced outright this time, or at least for there to be a fairly clear indication of which way things leaned; but the final notice took both sides by surprise: the review committee judged that each side's bid had its own strengths, and that each also had content requiring further verification and supplementation, so for the present it would make no final decision, requiring the two to submit supplementary materials separately before organising another round of review.

This meant the project was not over yet.

For Yuanwang, this was naturally not bad news. Since Diao Kexin had taken charge of Yuanwang again, she had built up the Sixth Academy connection quite solidly, and the old client relationships and technical resources had gradually recovered. What she needed was not a dazzling bid presentation, but a project that could prove once more that Yuanwang possessed full delivery capability.

For Xinghuan, however, the result was somewhat delicate. Zheng Zhiyong and Wu Peng had kept their hearts suspended for a long while; now they hung higher than ever.

Had the winner been announced on the spot, Old Zheng could at least have taken the contract and reorganised his team; even short-handed, he would still have had funding and the project timeline to lean on. Now both sides had to submit supplementary material, which meant that Xinghuan had not only failed to obtain a final result, but must instead complete another, more painstaking round of proof at a time when its technical strength had already been visibly weakened.

One item the review comments singled out for Xinghuan to supplement was the generalisability of the algorithm's implementation across different hardware platforms. The phrase looks uncomplicated enough, but for anyone who has truly done embedded-algorithm engineering, what lies behind it is far more complex than a model's recognition accuracy. The technical material Xinghuan had submitted in the past mainly demonstrated the algorithm's effectiveness on a given dataset and in a given test environment. The model was trained on a workstation and then verified in a simulation environment, with detection accuracy, miss rate, false-alarm rate and recognition performance across various scenarios all backed by corresponding data. The trouble was that a workstation is not the final product, and a simulation environment is not the real operating environment. In the end the algorithm must be deployed onto different embedded computing platforms, working together with infrared sensors, image-acquisition modules, data-transmission interfaces and the underlying control system.

In the laboratory, a computation that a high-performance GPU performs with ease may, on the actual device, turn into an entirely different problem.

First is the difference in computing resources. Different platforms differ in CPU architecture, GPU or NPU computing power, memory capacity and bandwidth, and the instruction sets and underlying operators they support also vary. A neural network that runs well on a server at FP32 floating-point precision will usually need model compression and inference optimisation when migrated to an edge device. One of the most common approaches is to convert part of the floating-point operations into lower-precision integer operations such as INT8, so as to reduce memory footprint and computational cost. But quantization is not simply a matter of shrinking numbers: the distribution of weights and activations affects the quantization scale, and certain network layers especially sensitive to numerical change may suffer a drop in accuracy after conversion, even making small, faint targets that were once recognisable become unstable.

Second is the difference in the software runtime environment. The inference toolchains provided by different hardware vendors are not identical; even if the same model adopts a unified intermediate format, that does not mean every operator will be natively supported by the target device. Some operators can be handed directly to the NPU for execution, while others may fall back to the CPU; once frequent data transfers and switches between computing units occur, the expected speed-up is greatly reduced. More troublesome still, different platforms may handle operator precision, tensor layout, dynamic input sizes and memory management in different ways, so the fact that a model runs normally on one device is no proof that it will maintain the same performance on another.

There is also an easily overlooked issue: differences in the data of real-world scenarios. Infrared sensors from different batches may differ in response curve, noise level, distribution of bad pixels and imaging dynamic range; changes in the lens, focal length, detector resolution and image-preprocessing pipeline also affect the data the algorithm ultimately receives. That an algorithm performs well on one batch of devices is no guarantee that, switched to another batch, it will maintain the same recognition performance. Especially in scenes involving low light at night, distant small targets, complex backgrounds and rapid motion, the model's confidence distribution may shift, and thresholds set in the past may no longer apply.

Thus what the review experts truly wanted to see was not another, prettier algorithm test report from Xinghuan, but proof that this algorithm possessed the capability for cross-platform deployment and engineering adaptation.

The supplementary material needed to break the whole process down and explain it step by step: beginning with the baseline version of the trained model, specifying its architecture, input size, preprocessing method and key parameters; then describing the concrete process of model conversion, quantization calibration, operator adaptation and compiled deployment; and finally carrying out actual tests on different hardware platforms, recording such metrics as recognition accuracy, miss rate, false-alarm rate, per-frame inference latency, memory footprint and power consumption. For any operator that hardware could not natively support, they must also state what substitute implementation was adopted, and whether it would cause additional data transfers and latency.

If conditions allowed, they were also to put the algorithm on real equipment for hardware-in-the-loop testing, letting sensor input, underlying computation and output control form a complete closed loop, rather than merely playing a few pre-recorded videos on a computer.

This was no longer, in fact, a purely algorithmic problem, but one of co-design among algorithm, software and hardware.

Having read the review comments, Old Zheng's first thought was of Dr. Lan. Of all this, Dr. Lan had once known the most. He knew not only how the models were trained, but also which operators had been specially treated to accommodate the hardware, and which parameters had sacrificed a measure of accuracy to meet real-time requirements. Zhang Hailu was responsible for organising and delivering the whole technical system, while Dr. Lan was the one who could truly turn an algorithm from something on paper into a running result on a device.

But now Dr. Lan was gone. Zhang Hailu had stayed on at the company and promised to continue overseeing the handover and project transition, but he himself knew perfectly well that a clear gap had opened up on the algorithms side. In the past, with someone like Dr. Lan on the team, Zhang Hailu had only needed to break down the overall tasks, set the schedule and coordinate resources, and the project would move forward smoothly. Now that the core algorithm specialist had left, the engineers who remained could keep the existing projects going, but would find it hard to undertake on their own the cross-platform verification this supplementary material demanded. This was not a matter that could be solved by hiring a few more ordinary programmers.

Between adults, a departure sometimes needs no explicit reason.

For the first time Old Zheng truly realised that the technical system he had relied on over the past few years was, to a considerable extent, built upon the personal experience of a handful of people. That experience had never been fully captured in documents, nor had it formed a research-and-development process capable of running on its own. While Zhang Hailu and Dr. Lan were there, many things could be done with a single glance or a brief word of instruction; now that they were gone, the technical edifice that had looked so complete suddenly showed many blanks that could not be filled.

What Old Zheng had to do now was rebuild the technical system as quickly as possible. He first made a trip back to Northwestern Polytechnical University. The school still had several of his former teachers and senior fellow alumni; though they might not be able to join the project directly, they knew at least a little about people working on vision algorithms and embedded deployment. Old Zheng stayed near the campus for two days and met with several groups of people in turn. Some were PhDs fresh from the lab; some were engineers who had spent a few years at research institutes before moving into industry; and others were young people working on image processing and low-level optimisation. Each had his own strengths. Some wrote excellent papers and knew the theory of target detection and feature extraction intimately, but had limited real experience of product delivery; some had overseen model deployment in industry and were familiar with ONNX, TensorRT and the inference toolchains of various platforms, yet lacked sufficiently deep understanding of algorithm design for complex scenarios; and others had done FPGA and embedded development, able to solve low-level computing and interface problems, though not necessarily able to shoulder an entire algorithm system on their own.

For Old Zheng, these academic types were probably all not quite right. Perhaps what he needed most now was not a single person who could replace Dr. Lan, but a small team that could cover the different technical links. Algorithm modelling, inference deployment, hardware adaptation, data processing and test verification — all of these must each have someone responsible, and at the same time there must be someone able to integrate them. The tacit understanding that had formed among Zhang Hailu, Dr. Lan and the others in the past could not be replicated in a short time, but at least every key link must have a clearly designated person in charge.

After returning from Xi'an, he began making the rounds in Beijing.

Beijing was somewhat different from Xi'an. There were more algorithm talents here, especially teams working on visual recognition, autonomous driving, industrial inspection and edge computing, all with certain industry connections among them. Drawing on relationships accumulated over the years, Old Zheng met with several groups in succession — some from university labs, some who had left large tech companies to start their own ventures, and two or three engineers who had once worked on military projects.

He found that truly suitable people were few. Some were very capable, but their salary demands had already approached the level of the large tech companies, and Xinghuan's current finances could hardly sustain that over the long term; some were willing to join a small company but wanted greater say in technical decisions and a share of project revenues; and others were not comfortable with the cycles and secrecy requirements of military projects, feeling that commercial products offered more freedom and made it easier to see results.

All Old Zheng could do was talk and screen at the same time. While in Beijing, he phoned Wu Peng — who at that moment was still holding the fort in the Xi'an office — to bring him up to date on the past few days' progress. When the call came, Wu Peng put it on speakerphone, and Cong Shasha listened quietly beside him, without a word.

Old Zheng was feeling the pressure. After hanging up on Wu Peng, he smoked several cigarettes hard, then called Xiaoya and asked her to come to Beijing for a few days too.

Xiaoya was very considerate and arrived the next day. During the busy daylight hours, while Old Zheng was occupied, she wandered around the neighbourhood on her own, waiting until he returned to the hotel that evening. Afterwards the two ate near the hotel, and Xiaoya and Old Zheng walked back to the hotel, the two of them going back to the hotel together; Xiaoya sat on the sofa in the room, scrolling through photos on her phone.

When Old Zheng came out of the shower, the two lay down together on the bed, and Xiaoya, quiet and obedient, fell asleep on his shoulder.

Old Zheng: “Why so good? I called you and you came at once, and then waited for me quietly all day — that deserves praise.”

Xiaoya: “Because I know you're under a lot of pressure at work right now.”

Old Zheng: “Yes.”

Xiaoya: “Is work not going well?”

Old Zheng: “The team suddenly collapsed.”

Xiaoya was silent for a while, as if repeatedly arranging her words in her mind, not knowing how to comfort him. So she laid her hand on Old Zheng's chest and stayed still, quiet. If he did not want to say anything more, she would not ask.

Old Zheng lowered his head and kissed her, and she kissed him back. All along she had known that Old Zheng treated her well — found her work, took her to see the wider world, and was willing to help her when she ran into difficulty. Sometime, she could not have said when, she had discovered that she had fallen in love with him.

On the other side, Wu Peng and Cong Shasha had also arrived in Beijing. The day before, after they had finished speaking with Old Zheng on the phone, Shasha said, “Why don't the two of us go to Beijing? For one thing, we can make another bid for a share of the supplementary contract for the simulation system in the Sixth Academy project. As for the relevant leaders, I'll arrange the meetings, and you can get to know them too.” On this contract, Xinghuan had already completed part of the work on the simulation system and its ancillary products, and the client was fairly familiar with how the system was being used; only later, when Yuanwang re-entered the Sixth Academy's business system, had Diao Kexin, relying on relationships built up over the years, won back a portion of the supplementary orders that might otherwise have continued to go to Xinghuan. Shasha's reason for making these appointments now was chiefly to have Wu Peng, as the young general manager, connect early with the key client resources in Beijing as well. Now both companies were waiting for the result of the supplementary review of the Xi'an project, and the business relationships at the Sixth Academy had grown more delicate too.

Wu Peng knew perfectly well that Old Zheng's old relationships alone were no longer enough to guarantee Xinghuan continued to win orders. Since Diao Kexin's return to Yuanwang, those former client relationships had been gradually recovering, and Yuanwang's business staff had begun to stir again. If Xinghuan wanted to hold on to even part of its business at the Sixth Academy, it had to show the client that it still possessed stable delivery capability. Cong Shasha was a great help in this. In a sense, Shasha's range of activity and network of contacts in Beijing were now utterly different from the little girl of a few years before; she had truly grown into a sales director of sharp judgement and worldly tact, and she wanted Wu Peng to become part of this network too, to maintain these people and matters alongside her.

But there was another thing Shasha had thought of: in the present talent shortage, there was one person who might be able to help.

That person was Xiao Jing.

Xiao Jing was a rare technical expert, equally accomplished in algorithms and hardware. Xinghuan's present difficulty was something Zhang Hailu could not solve and Zheng Zhiyong could not solve either — but give Xiao Jing the right staff, and it might well be done. Cong Shasha had sensed this sharply. The problem was Xiao Jing's former romantic relationship with Zheng Zhiyong: if Zheng Zhiyong were the one to approach her about cooperating or even joining, it would almost certainly backfire. Shasha felt it was time to let Wu Peng give it a try. She herself might perhaps go ahead and prepare the ground for her boyfriend.

The two had already agreed in Xi'an to come to Beijing to handle these two matters. Once in Beijing, the two of them worked unusually smoothly in talking with clients and about orders, and Cong Shasha also secured a meeting with Xiao Jing for the next day.

That evening, after the two had eaten and returned to the hotel, Wu Peng walked Shasha to her door, intending to say a few words about the next day's arrangements and then leave — but Shasha did not immediately swipe her card to enter.

She looked at Wu Peng and asked whether he was still going back to his own room.

Wu Peng said there was still a little something to take care of back in his room.

Shasha smiled and said that since the contract had been settled today, wasn't it worth celebrating?

Wu Peng asked how she wanted to celebrate.

She did not answer, but simply reached out and took his hand.

In that moment Wu Peng suddenly felt a little nervous. They had confirmed their relationship and had already had many physical encounters, but — liking is possession, while love is restraint — so the two of them, in all their time together, had still not punctured that last layer of window paper.

In that moment Shasha seemed to read his hesitation.

She leaned straight in and kissed him; Wu Peng reached out and held her, and went with her into the room.

After that night, the two of them truly crossed, for the first time, the last boundary between lovers.

The next morning, Wu Peng was roused by a thread of sunlight leaking through a gap in the curtains. He opened his eyes. The hotel room was hung with thick blackout curtains and the light was dim, save for a single golden band that fell exactly across the duvet. He turned his head slightly and saw Shasha beside him. She lay on her side facing him, most of her face buried in the soft pillow, her loose black hair spilled across her fair shoulders. The hotel duvet covered her only to the waist; with each calm breath its edge rose and fell a little, laying bare a broad sweep of smooth, clean-lined back.

The fever of the night before still hung in the air. Watching her sleeping face, Wu Peng felt a strange sense of fulfilment well up in him. He could not resist reaching out, letting his fingertips slip lightly down her spine, as though tracing the contours of some precious piece of porcelain.

Shasha was woken by his touch. She did not open her eyes, but instinctively snuggled closer into his arms like a cat, letting out a languid little hum through her nose, her arm settling naturally around his waist.

“What time is it?” Her voice was terribly hoarse, thick with the nasal drowsiness of fresh sleep.

“Still early,” Wu Peng murmured, his hand sliding from her back to her waist and, with a little pressure, drawing her whole body into his arms.

Their bare skin met, and their body heat mingled in an instant. Wu Peng lowered his head to find her lips. Shasha tilted her chin up slightly to meet his kiss. At first the kiss was gentle, with the clinging languor peculiar to early morning. But very soon the familiar, shivering chemistry of the night before returned. Wu Peng's kisses grew ardent, his tongue parting the barrier of her teeth, his hand travelling slowly up the line of her waist to close over the soft fullness of her breast.

Shasha's breath quickened at once. Her hands slipped from Wu Peng's waist, came around his neck, her fingers twining into his rather short hair and tightening a little.

Wu Peng rolled over, pinning her beneath him. The thread of morning light through the window fell exactly across Shasha's collarbone, and with her panting the little shadow shifted along with it. Wu Peng's gaze followed the light downward, over that thin layer of skin rendered half-translucent by the sun, and his Adam's apple rolled once.

“Again?” Shasha half opened her eyes, the corners still heavy with the lingering warmth of desire, looking at him with something like a challenge, a faint smile playing at the corners of her lips.

Wu Peng said nothing; he answered her with action instead. He bent and kissed her neck and collarbone, then took her legs and wrapped them around his waist. Shasha drew in a sharp breath, the arms around his neck tightening abruptly, her whole body arching upward beyond her control like a fully drawn bow, then slowly relaxing with his rhythm, only to draw taut again as he sank into her.

The hotel room was very quiet in the early morning, filled only with the rustle of rumpled bedding and their stifled breathing. Wu Peng looked down at the woman beneath him: her once pale cheeks were now flushed a deep red, her lower lip caught tightly between her teeth as she tried not to make a sound, though now and then a small moan, like a stifled sob, still escaped through that gap in her lips.

This double assault on sight and sound loosened every restraint in him. He was no longer the gentle, well-mannered assistant who always knew his place; in this moment he wanted nothing but to possess her utterly.

It was an hour later before the two of them settled again.

Shasha lay limply face-down on the bed, not wanting to move so much as a finger. Wu Peng held her from behind, his chest against her back, his chin resting in the hollow of her shoulder, his breathing not yet fully steady.

“It's done for,” Shasha said weakly, though a smile hung at the corners of her mouth. “In this state, I won't have a brain left to meet Xiao Jing with later.”

Near noon, Shasha got up and took a shower. Standing before the bathroom mirror, she looked at the faintly visible red mark below her collarbone, sighed, and had no choice but to find a high-necked cashmere sweater to put on.

Before leaving, she put on careful make-up to hide the signs of fatigue on her face and resumed the brisk, decisive air of the sales director. She walked to the bed, looked at Wu Peng still lounging there, bent down, and — like a slippery little fish — pecked a kiss onto his lips.

“I'm off to meet Xiao Jing,” Shasha said, picking up her bag and giving him a wink. “If I can bring it off, the three of us'll have dinner together.”

“Be careful.” Wu Peng reclined against the headboard, watching her retreating figure disappear behind the door.
