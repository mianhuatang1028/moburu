Luwu

Chapter Twenty-Seven: Luwu

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Luwu” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Western Mountains, Book Three”), the Mound of Kunlun is presided over by the god Luwu, who has a tiger’s body and nine tails, a human face and a tiger’s claws, and governs the nine regions of heaven and the seasons of the Lord’s gardens. As the saying goes: the one who keeps the gate is the first to answer to it.

The public notice period was two weeks.

Zheng Zhiyong had known this rule since his Yuanwang days. At a unit like the Sixth Academy, bid documents easily ran to a thousand pages — technical solutions, qualification documents, price lists, proof of past performance — and stacked up in piles they could fill half a room. The evaluation panel finishing its reading wasn’t the end of it: during the notice period there was another round of compliance review, and in that window any bidder could object to the result, or anonymously submit additional materials and demand a fresh review. That was the rule out in the open, and it was also the loophole in the dark.

When Zheng Zhiyong was COO at Yuanwang, he’d seen one case where someone wrecked a notice period. It was an eight-million project with Yuanwang in first place. On the seventh day of the notice period, someone anonymously submitted a document saying that one of Yuanwang’s qualification certificates had expired three days before the bid deadline. Once the document was in, the Sixth Academy’s project office had no choice but to start a review, and with all the back-and-forth it dragged on for two months. In the end Yuanwang kept the award, but the delivery cycle was squeezed to the bone, and that year Zhao Bin’s team nearly collapsed from exhaustion.

So during these two weeks of notice, Zheng Zhiyong didn’t relax for a single day.

The first week passed without a ripple. On Tuesday of the second week, Supervisor Zhou called.

“Mr. Zheng, there’s something I want to give you a heads-up about. During the notice period we received an anonymous document questioning the procedure and qualifications by which Xinghuan was evaluated. According to procedure, we need your side to submit additional materials. This doesn’t affect the notice result, but the timing may have to be pushed back a bit.”

Zheng Zhiyong held the phone, his tone very steady. “Supervisor Zhou, our procedure and qualifications are all up to standard, aren’t they?”

Supervisor Zhou said, “Mainly it’s that your past revenue is too small. Someone may have raised questions about the project-experience part.”

When he hung up, he called Zhang Hailu and Cong Shasha into his office and told them how things stood. Zhang Hailu listened and said with a frown, “Holy shit, this one’s tough. This company really was a shell before. Where are we supposed to find revenue?”

Cong Shasha said, “But we still meet the bidding qualifications. This is obviously nitpicking.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “So you’re saying someone’s stirring things up — Yuanwang’s gotten someone involved?”

Zhang Hailu said, “I think so. Maybe someone on the inside is working hand in glove with Yuanwang.”

He paused.

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Then this person isn’t one of the evaluators. It’s risk control and auditing.”

The office was quiet for a few seconds.

Cong Shasha said, “Mr. Zheng, Ms. Diao is very close with the leaders in risk control at the Sixth Academy.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Who’s the key person on their end?”

Cong Shasha said, “I’ve never met them. Ms. Diao always handled that connection herself before.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Go. Find the leader you know well, ask around about what’s happening inside, find out who it’s stuck with.”

Cong Shasha nodded.

On Friday evening Cong Shasha met Deputy Director Li in Beijing. Zheng Zhiyong waited in Xi’an for news. He waited until after ten that night, when Cong Shasha’s call came.

“Mr. Zheng, it’s more complicated than we thought.” Cong Shasha’s voice was pitched very low, as if she were calling from a car. “Deputy Director Li says it isn’t risk control holding it up. It’s a top leader who read the complaint materials and felt our team was too new and too improvised, short on project experience, not qualified enough, and then he pushed it down onto the risk-control department and the project office. He also said there’s a problem with us even getting into the evaluation. The project office is in a very awkward spot too; Supervisor Zhou called a special meeting about it. Deputy Director Li’s exact words were: hurry up and submit the extra materials.”

Zheng Zhiyong held his phone and said nothing.

Cong Shasha went on, “Mr. Zheng, I think Yuanwang is behind this.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Does that even need saying?”

Cong Shasha said, “I think it has to be Ms. Diao.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Let’s not guess yet. Go find out which top leader stepped in to lean on it. And then you and Hailu get the extra materials in as required, fast.”

Cong Shasha said, “Okay.”

When he hung up, Zheng Zhiyong sat on the sofa in his fourth-floor dormitory room and smoked a cigarette. He remembered what Zhu Yiwen had said in his office that day: Things haven’t been easy for Gao Fei lately. This order of yours hurt him.

People who are hurting sometimes bite.

Just as much of a headache as the extra materials was money.

For this Sixth Academy project, once the award came through, they’d have to front the money to start work. Zheng Zhiyong had Zhang Hailu do a detailed funding forecast with the finance staff: up-front R&D, scheduling production in the supply chain, outsourced machining, testing and certification, expanding the team, travel and on-site costs, plus the performance bond the Sixth Academy required. All told, the money they’d have to sink into it wasn’t three million — it was six million at the bare minimum, and on a looser estimate it would reach eight million.

Xinghuan had started out with more than eight million in cash on the books; after these few months of spending, a little over five million was left. Even if they put every last cent on the books into it, it would still fall short of the six-million floor, and fall a big chunk short of the looser eight-million estimate.

Zheng Zhiyong took the forecast and made a trip to Shanghai.

Wu You’s office was in Lujiazui, with the Huangpu River right outside the floor-to-ceiling windows. When Zheng Zhiyong arrived, Wu You had just come out of a meeting on a quant strategy, and a few printed pages of model parameters lay spread on his desk. He took the forecast, read it from top to bottom, put it down and took a sip of coffee.

“Old Zheng, six to eight million in up-front funding — how accurate is that figure?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Zhang Hailu did it together with finance. I asked every supplier for their quote myself. It won’t be off by more than ten percent.”

Wu You said, “What’s the payment cycle on this Sixth Academy project?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “The first installment is paid three months after delivery and acceptance, and the rest is settled over two years.”

Wu You said, “So that means this six to eight million won’t start coming back for a year at the earliest, and won’t all be back for more than two years. In the meantime, what does the company live on?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “That’s why I’ve come to you. Mr. Wu, the authority you gave me at the start was supply-chain financing with no ceiling.”

Wu You said, “Supply-chain financing is for procurement and production scheduling against actual orders. The gap you’ve got now is operating capital you’d be fronting. Those aren’t the same thing.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “I understand. But on this Sixth Academy project, fronting the money is non-negotiable. Without it we can’t start work.”

Wu You was silent for a while, then said, “Old Zheng, when I invested in this company I agreed with the other shareholders that the money was for the company to do business with, not for the company to front costs with. Here’s what I’ll do: I’ll lend you two million in my own name, as my personal support for you. The rest, you’ll have to figure out yourself.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Mr. Wu, two million isn’t enough. There may be hidden costs too — with this qualification review going on, we’ll have to find people to figure out what’s really happening. The gap is four million at the very least.”

Wu You said, “I know it isn’t enough. That’s why I said the rest you’ll have to figure out yourself. You can take on some private-sector projects outside, ones that pay quickly, and get the cash flow moving first. A defense project — the first order’s fine for making a name, but relying on it to keep the company alive isn’t realistic. Didn’t you say that people are questioning us right now for having no revenue, like a briefcase company?”

All the way back from Shanghai, Zheng Zhiyong kept thinking about what Wu You had said. Two million, plus the five-million-odd on the books that could be freed up, came to a bit over seven million together — still a little short of the looser eight-million estimate, and only just reaching the six-million floor. But then the company would truly have not a cent left on the books; if anything went wrong along the way, there’d be no room to maneuver at all.

He needed to find a private-sector project, bring in one or two million in revenue, something that paid fast and would earn some ready cash.

As it happened, Zhang Hailu had introduced one a while back, but with the bidding period so busy they’d never gotten around to it, never even considered it. It was a company an old classmate of Zhang Hailu’s had opened in Dongguan, making fan lights. The man’s surname was Wang, and his name was Wang Renhou. These past two years Wang Renhou’s company had been selling into Africa and India, and he wanted to optimize the motor to shrink its size and cut its cost. Motors were Old Zheng’s strong suit, so Zhang Hailu had recommended Zheng Zhiyong to him.

Before, Old Zheng had felt the project had too little technical content. But now Zheng Zhiyong made a trip to Dongguan. Wang Renhou’s company was in an industrial park: three stories, the workshop on the ground floor, offices on the second, dormitories on the third. Several production lines were running in the workshop, the workers heads-down at their work, yellow zone lines painted on the floor, and on the wall the slogan “Quality Is Life.”

Wang Renhou was in his forties, a Guangdong local, not tall, dark-skinned, speaking with a Cantonese accent, not fast, every sentence plain and solid. He took Zheng Zhiyong on a lap of the workshop, explained where the product stood now, then brought out the motor solution they were currently buying and its price list.

“Mr. Zheng, my volume has taken off fast these two years. Last year two million units; this year it’ll be three million.”

Zheng Zhiyong was startled inside — the volume on this thing was actually that frightening. Cut three yuan off each one and profit goes up six to nine million! He went over the current motor’s technical parameters and winding method, watched the product actually running on the line, and went to look at Wang Renhou’s R&D lab, which had every kind of debugging and testing equipment, and engineers who were all young and full of energy. He said, “Mr. Wang, give me two technicians, and I’ll stay here a few days and have a go at it. If I can pull it off and save you two or three yuan a unit, you give me fifty fen a unit.”

Wang Renhou’s eyes lit up, and he took Zheng Zhiyong’s hand and said, “Mr. Zheng, if you can pull it off, I’ll give you twenty percent of the cost-down. You decide how much goes to your company — it can go to you personally too.”

Zheng Zhiyong was keen to bring some revenue in for the company, so he said all of it should go to his company, thinking to himself that the later years — the part in the future — he’d take personally, and talk it over with him then; anyway, they’d spell out the time period clearly when they signed the agreement. He smiled and got to work, eating and sleeping at the factory with the technicians, and he really did pull it off: the weight came down by twenty percent, it used less copper, and overall efficiency went up too.

Wang Renhou was more satisfied than he’d expected, too. He thought to himself: I’ve been in manufacturing for more than ten years, from a little workshop to a factory of two hundred-odd people, and what got me here was plain hard graft. He didn’t know much about technology, but he valued talent. So he treated Zheng Zhiyong to a big dinner with drinks, and over the meal learned that Zheng Zhiyong was really just a wage worker himself, and couldn’t help admiring him a bit more. Halfway through the drinking, Wang Renhou said, “Mr. Zheng, that company of yours in Xi’an — how much do they pay you a year?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “A base salary of ten thousand, plus ten percent technical equity.”

Wang Renhou said, “Ten thousand? Mr. Zheng, come work for me — a million a year, plus a share of the profits. My company isn’t big, but the cash flow’s good and we don’t owe anybody. Come here and you’ll be number one on the technical side. I’ll listen to you on everything.”

Zheng Zhiyong was taken aback for a moment, then laughed. “Mr. Wang, at that company in Xi’an, I’m not just a hired hand.”

Wang Renhou thought to himself, isn’t that just a hired hand all the same? So he laughed and said, “Shareholders have got to eat too. How do you live on a hundred-odd thousand a year? And besides, here you’d be a shareholder too. Think it over, no rush.”

Zheng Zhiyong didn’t take up the offer. But he knew very well that what Wang Renhou was proposing showed one thing: his technical skills were worth money on the market. He was taking ten thousand a month in salary at Xinghuan because he was betting on a bigger game. And right now, that game was stuck on money.

Though he’d landed this million-yuan order for the company, they were still a good deal short.

Triumphant from Dongguan or not, Zheng Zhiyong went through every option he could think of. Banks? Xinghuan was an asset-light company, with nothing to put up as collateral. Friends? He still hadn’t paid back Lu You’s hundred thousand; he couldn’t open his mouth. Loan sharks? He didn’t dare touch them.

In the end he thought of Zhu Yiwen.

Zhu Yiwen’s company was in Xiangcheng, so this time Old Zheng simply went straight there from Dongguan and arranged to meet him. Gaoda’s office was a five-story detached building with two ginkgo trees by the entrance, their branches bare. The receptionist led Zheng Zhiyong up to the third floor. Zhu Yiwen was sitting behind his desk reading documents. When he saw him come in he stood, came around the desk and held out his hand. “Mr. Zheng, come, sit.”

The office was large. One wall was bookcases, full of technical books and binders; on the other wall hung a remote-sensing image of a city at night, dotted with lights. Zhu Yiwen boiled the water and made the tea himself, with practiced hands.

“Mr. Zheng, have some tea. Rougui, from the Wuyi Mountains.”

Zheng Zhiyong took the cup and drank a mouthful.

Zhu Yiwen said, “You haven’t come here today just for tea, have you?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Mr. Zhu, I’ve come asking for help. This Sixth Academy project needs six to eight million fronted up front, and I don’t have enough on the books.”

Zhu Yiwen put down his cup and looked at him. “You told me before — didn’t your company give you supply-chain financing with no ceiling?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “That’s supply-chain financing for actual orders, not operating capital to front costs. Mr. Wu’s position is that fronting the money is something I’ll have to sort out myself. He’s lent me two million personally, and the rest I have to scrape together on my own.”

Zhu Yiwen said, “So you’ve come to me hoping I’ll give a little on the payment terms for the subcontract?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Mr. Zhu, on this project, Gaoda takes forty percent and carries only ten percent of the cost. You’re the one making the most. I’d like to ask you to help out on the payment schedule — hold off on the up-front subcontract payments for now, and once the Sixth Academy’s first payment arrives, I’ll pay Gaoda.”

Zhu Yiwen said nothing. Holding his teacup, he slowly took a sip and leaned back in his chair.

Zheng Zhiyong knew that this reason alone wouldn’t persuade Zhu Yiwen. He thought for a moment, put his cup down and said, “Mr. Zhu, let me tell you something else. Once you’ve heard it, decide whether you’ll help me.”

Zhu Yiwen looked at him and said nothing.

Zheng Zhiyong said, “You may laugh at me, but you know why I’m fighting Yuanwang to the death — I’ve got a score to settle with Gao Fei. I don’t know whether you know it, but just recently Wang Yan’s and her brother’s shares have all been taken off the business registry.”

Zhu Yiwen’s expression didn’t change much; only the hand holding the teacup stopped for a moment.

Zheng Zhiyong saw the change in his expression, took out his phone, opened Tianyancha and showed Zhu Yiwen the latest list of registered shareholders. Zhu Yiwen sighed and shook his head.

Old Zheng struck while the iron was hot. “After that I came to Xinghuan, and Wu You gave me a chance. I took on this Sixth Academy project half for the company and half to slap Gao Fei in the face. Now it’s Gao Fei and Diao Kexin’s Yuanwang. It isn’t Sister Yan’er and Gao Fei’s anymore.”

When Zheng Zhiyong had finished, he picked up his cup and drank the rest of his tea.

The office was quiet for a while.

Zhu Yiwen put down his cup and looked at Zheng Zhiyong, with something in his eyes that Zheng Zhiyong couldn’t make out. Not sympathy, not surprise — more like a very deep silence, of someone who’d been touched by something.

Zhu Yiwen said, “Mr. Zheng, what’s between you and Gao Fei is between you and him. I’m a businessman. Actually, there’s one more reason I’m working with you: we’re both in Xiangcheng, and I have a rough idea that their funding chain has run into huge problems. But in working with you, however the agreement was signed, I stand by it — the payment terms, the split, all of it. Adjusting an agreement after it’s been signed — that isn’t something I can just change on a whim.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “I understand. I didn’t expect you to say yes. I just wanted to tell you why I have to win, why I have to make Xinghuan a success. If you help me, you’re not helping me vent my anger; you’re helping someone who can get things done.”

Zhu Yiwen didn’t answer right away. He stood up and went over to the remote-sensing image, and stood there a while with his back to Zheng Zhiyong.

Zheng Zhiyong couldn’t see his expression. He only knew that while Zhu Yiwen stood there, the line of his shoulders had loosened a little compared with before.

After a good while, Zhu Yiwen turned around, came back to the tea table, topped up Zheng Zhiyong’s tea and said, “Mr. Zheng, on the payment terms I can’t give you an answer right now. Gaoda isn’t mine alone — there are other shareholders, and on payment terms my word alone doesn’t decide it. I hear the academy leadership hasn’t actually signed the contract over to you yet. Why don’t we talk it over again when the contract’s signed and the project’s locked in?”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Mr. Zhu, then I’ll wait to hear from you.”

Zhu Yiwen said, “Go back and have a proper talk with your company’s boss, and lay out your financial plan from beginning to end. Once you’ve got your numbers straight, this project is still very doable. Also, you can take private-sector projects, but don’t take just any project. At the stage you’re at now, the most important thing isn’t money — it’s the ability to get things done. Once you’ve built up that ability, the money will come by itself.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Mr. Zhu, thank you. Today’s trip was worth it.”

Zhu Yiwen said, “We’ve had the tea, we’ve had our talk. On the subcontract payments and the terms, I’ll have finance run the numbers again, and I’ll need to talk with the shareholders too. But don’t expect too much.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “Whatever you can spare helps.”

He got up to take his leave. When he reached the door, Zhu Yiwen suddenly called after him: “Mr. Zheng.”

Zheng Zhiyong turned his head.

Zhu Yiwen said, “As for Gao Fei — don’t push a man to the wall. Even a rabbit bites when it’s cornered. Be careful yourself.”

Zheng Zhiyong said, “I understand.”

When he came out of Zhu Yiwen’s company, dusk was already falling. The traffic on the streets of Haidian flowed without end, and the streetlights came on one after another, stretching the shadows of the plane trees long. Zheng Zhiyong stood at the roadside and lit a cigarette.

He thought of Zhu Yiwen’s back as he stood in front of that remote-sensing image. There was something in that back he couldn’t see through. Zhu Yiwen hadn’t agreed to the payment terms, but Zheng Zhiyong kept feeling that today’s trip was more than just “worth it.”

Zheng Zhiyong finished his cigarette, stubbed it out in a trash can by the road, took out his phone and sent Wu Peng a message: “Set up a meeting with Mr. Wu for me, first thing Monday morning next week. I’ll go to Shanghai and take him through the financial plan for the Sixth Academy project from beginning to end.”

Wu Peng replied: “Will do, Mr. Zheng.”

Zheng Zhiyong put his phone in his pocket, hailed a taxi, and headed for the airport.

Outside the car window, the Beijing night fell back bit by bit. In his head he went through what he had to do next: the Sixth Academy’s supplementary verification — get Zhang Hailu to make the materials solid; on the hidden front, get Cong Shasha to feel out who exactly was making trouble in the middle; with Wu You, get the numbers straight and negotiate again; Wang Renhou’s project — take a million-yuan order first to bring in some revenue; Zhu Yiwen’s payment terms — wait for news.

There was one more thing he hadn’t told anyone. The anonymous document submitted during the notice period — he knew the person behind it was Diao Kexin. This woman was no simple creature. She was deeply steeped in dealings with leaders on every side. Was this leader’s relationship with her purely about money? Or was there sex in it too? The more Old Zheng thought about it, the more lost he felt.

The taxi got onto the airport expressway. Zheng Zhiyong leaned back in the back seat and closed his eyes.

He remembered last winter, the first time he sat across from Lu You and said, “I’m just like Jia Baoyu.” Back then he thought he was a son of a family fallen on hard times, that it was just bad luck. Thinking about it now, it wasn’t bad luck at all. He had walked himself to that point, one step at a time.

The car drove on, the streetlights outside sweeping past one after another. Zheng Zhiyong opened his eyes and looked out the window, and that hatred rose up in his heart again. But this time, there was more pressed underneath the hatred than before. There were account books, there was a plan, there was a group of people working with him, there was an award notice.

He gathered all of it together, pressed it against his heart, and felt a little steadier.
