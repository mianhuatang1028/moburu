Chapter Three: Hundun

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Hundun” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Western Mountains, Book Three”), on Mount Tian there is a god shaped like a yellow sack, with six feet and four wings, a formless blur without a face, and it knows song and dance. Without a face, you cannot see where it comes from. As the saying goes: a blur without a face — fortune and misfortune cannot be told apart.

After marrying, Zheng Zhiyong worked steadily for about seven years, and then set out on the life journey of a middle-aged man who can’t stop stirring things up.

In the fourth year of their marriage, Zhang Jingyu gave birth to a son, and they named him Zheng Ruixi. The child looked very much like his mother: long arms and legs, skin white as snow, deep-set eyes, a high bridge to his nose. That year Zhang Jingyu’s parents retired too and came to Beijing to help the young couple with the baby. From the time she got pregnant, Zhang Jingyu had wanted to buy a home and a car, so before the baby was born they first bought a new BYD; Zheng Zhiyong usually drove, ferrying his pregnant wife around and handling all the trivial household errands. Zhang Jingyu, big belly and all, led the entire apartment hunt. They looked and looked for months without committing. What they had saved from these years of work could only cover the down payment on an old, run-down little place in Fengtai. There happened to be a secondhand small three-bedroom right between their two workplaces, about eighty square meters; the light, the layout, the renovation suited them in every respect — the only regret was that every room was a bit small. They had looked at it when she first got pregnant, and looked at it again when Zhang Jingyu’s belly was big. That was 2015, and from the start of the year to the end, prices kept edging up; by the time the two finally decided to buy, it cost over three hundred thousand yuan more than when they’d first seen it. Because his wife was about to give birth and her parents were coming to Beijing, they hurried to buy the place and registered it in Zhang Jingyu’s name. Because Jingyu was so close to giving birth, they didn’t gut and fully renovate it after buying; they just replaced the furniture and patched up the damaged parts here and there.

After the baby was born, the family of five finally moved into this home of their own.

Zhang Jingyu’s bedroom was very small. The couple’s double bed was set flush against one wall, leaving only enough space by the window for a small dressing table, but now the dressing stool had been tucked under the table to make room for the crib. A newborn comes with a lot of stuff, so Zheng Zhiyong put up two shelf boards at the head of the bed. The crib was pressed tight against the outer edge of the bed on Zhang Jingyu’s side and tight against the window as well; they had to wedge a thick sofa cushion against the crib’s railing to block off the radiator under the window. To get out of bed, the two of them had to climb down from the foot end.

Zhang Jingyu lay beside her husband, glanced at the baby sleeping soundly in the crib beside her, then stretched out and laid her head on Zheng Zhiyong’s chest. She said softly, “Honey, this place is really small, but it feels solid to me. I finally have a home of my own. Thank you, honey.”

A warm current welled up in Zheng Zhiyong’s heart.

Zhang Jingyu went on, “Honey, you’ll work hard and make money and move me and the baby somewhere bigger someday, won’t you?”

Zheng Zhiyong stroked Zhang Jingyu’s head and said, “I will, honey. I’ll definitely get the two of you into a bigger place. Give me a few years. I can definitely do it.”

Zheng Zhiyong began to think about how he could earn more money for the family. He’d been doing his research institute job perfectly well, his heart as calm as still water, when around 2012 the era of the internet boom suddenly arrived. One year, back in his hometown for the New Year, Zheng Zhiyong happened to hear that a high school classmate — a guy who had only managed to get into an ordinary second-tier college — had joined the founding team of a company taking internet software overseas and was now a vice president. This guy was named Liu Yang; the company was in Wangjing SOHO and its founder was a former vice president of 360. A few years in, by ’15 — the year Zheng Zhiyong’s son was born — the company’s valuation had shot up like a launched satellite to several billion US dollars. Suddenly hearing that this second-tier classmate, whose high school grades had been far below his own, was now worth over a hundred million, Zheng Zhiyong was deeply shaken and often sighed: what a man fears most is choosing the wrong trade. At this point Zheng Zhiyong once again deployed his peerless gift of the gab and went to drink with Liu Yang and catch up on old times. After a few warm, boozy dinners, Liu Yang agreed to arrange a position for him in the software company’s overseas business development.

He said, “But the company doesn’t have headcount open just any time. You’ll have to wait a few months while I find the right moment to bring you in. In the meantime you can start studying our business model.”

Sure enough, a few months later, after several rounds of interviews, Zheng Zhiyong got an offer from Liu Yang’s company, at more than double what he’d earned in the system. He joined the company, called APilot. The name happened to mean “navigator,” and though the company mainly took security software overseas, and he’d switched from aerospace to the internet, with a name like that Zheng Zhiyong always felt he was still in the business of things that fly in the sky.

Zheng Zhiyong was surrounded by young people five to ten years younger than him. Internet companies are like that, and this one especially: the average age was just twenty-six, and many of the core staff were only a few years out of college. Zheng Zhiyong suddenly realized he wasn’t young anymore; he was no longer Engineer Zheng, the freshly graduated technician, but Old Zheng, a junior operations hand. Old Zheng did overseas business development while learning operations and overseas social-media ad buying. He worked hard and used his head, taught himself Silicon Valley growth-hacking theory, even taught himself a few algorithms to optimize the ad data, and his results were good. Add to that his personal friendship with Liu Yang, and a year later Old Zheng was promoted to department manager, got another twenty percent raise, and was granted options worth several million. Less than a year after that, in 2017, the company listed on Nasdaq; on its first day of trading its market value soared by more than a hundred percent. But not long after, Liu Yang suddenly resigned and stopped showing up at the company. Growth in the overseas regions he’d been responsible for opening up had slowed over the previous year, and all kinds of rumors went around: some said Liu Yang had been sacrificed in a political struggle and had no choice but to leave; others said the falling numbers had put him under too much pressure and he’d chosen to go.

Old Zheng couldn’t hold back and went to find Liu Yang. They met again at the little bar near Liu Yang’s home where they’d first talked about the job.

Liu Yang: There’s nothing to it. I just wanted to quit.

Old Zheng: Why quit?

Liu Yang: The last few years were too exhausting, and I never spent proper time with my wife. I’m thirty-four this year. I’ve decided to travel the world with her while I get myself in shape to have a kid.

Old Zheng: Can’t you have a kid and travel while you’re working?

Liu Yang: My wife won’t have it. She says if I don’t give her proper time, and we still can’t have a kid, she’ll divorce me and find someone else to have one with.

Old Zheng: Then the wife does come first.

Liu Yang raised his glass and said to Old Zheng: I’m totally financially free. I have enough money. I just want to live a good life now.

After Liu Yang left, Old Zheng’s department was merged into the department of another vice president who had never gotten along with Liu Yang. A few months later, Old Zheng was suddenly called into a meeting room by HR, handed three months’ severance and processed out. That day was exactly twenty-seven days short of a full year since he had become department manager and been granted his million-yuan options.
