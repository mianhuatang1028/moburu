Chiru

Chapter Thirty-Six: Chiru

Translation first published: 2026-11-10 09:40 (UTC+8) First published here: 2026-11-09 21:27 (UTC+8) © 2026 Moburu · All rights reserved
“Chiru” — In the Classic of Mountains and Seas (“Southern Mountains”), the Ying River near Mount Qingqiu brings forth the chiru, shaped like a fish but with a human face, its cry like a mandarin duck’s; whoever eats its flesh will never get scabies. A human face on a fish’s body, a voice like a mandarin duck — out of the Ying River it comes, to play among people.

The day Old Zheng went to Xi’an North Station to pick her up, he drove the company’s white Toyota Prado.

He parked in the station’s underground car park and waited at the exit. Xiaoya came out in an off-white down jacket, wrapped in a scarf that made the little bit of face peeking out look even rosier, dragging her suitcase. She saw him and waved, smiling. Old Zheng took her suitcase, and the two of them walked toward the car park. When they reached the car, Xiaoya stopped.

It was a huge white Toyota Prado, the body riding high, its paint giving off a cold sheen under the car-park lights. The car wasn’t exactly new, but it was well kept — thick tires, a wide, broad front end — and parked there it had a solid, heavy weight to it. Xiaoya didn’t know cars and couldn’t have named the model, but she recognized the size of it. She’d seen this kind of car in Chengdu. It was usually the kind driven by “engineering bosses,” parked by a construction site; the door would open and out would step a man with a bag under his arm, talking very loudly.

Old Zheng put the suitcase in the trunk, opened the passenger door and said, “Get in.”

Xiaoya had to work a bit to climb in; she’d never ridden in such a tall car before. The inside was very clean, the seats dark leather. On the center console sat a half-smoked pack of cigarettes and a lighter, and from the rearview mirror hung a string of plain, unfussy charms. She fastened her seat belt, put her hands on her knees, and let her eyes sweep around the car.

Old Zheng started the engine and pulled out of the car park. Once they were on the road the view was high up, and the sedans beside them slid past the window one after another. Sitting in the passenger seat, looking out at the streets, Xiaoya suddenly realized she’d rarely seen a highway from this vantage point.

She turned her head and glanced at Old Zheng. He had one hand on the wheel and the other resting on the gearstick, eyes on the road ahead, his face showing nothing much. He’d kept the beard, and the line of his profile was a bit harder than before. She suddenly felt that this man wasn’t quite the same “big brother” she’d seen in videos and met in the hotpot restaurant in Chengdu. In Chengdu he’d been a soft-spoken middle-aged man on a trip. In Xi’an, sitting in this car with his hand on the wheel, he was like someone else — someone with things to do in this city, a car to drive, people who called him “Mr. Zheng.”

Something in her heart stirred. She couldn’t say what the feeling was. She’d seen a lot of bosses in Chengdu — some really rich, some faking it — and she could tell them apart. This car wasn’t newly bought, wasn’t rented to put on a show; it was just a car in everyday use. It smelled of cigarettes, there was a lighter, there was half a pack of smokes.

Of the big brothers who sent gifts in her livestream, some sent a gift and then messaged her right away asking to meet, and some sent a gift and vanished. After half a year of talking, she knew Old Zheng wasn’t that kind. But now, sitting in this car, watching him drive onto the Second Ring Road, watching the streets of Xi’an fall away behind them out the window, she suddenly felt this man had grown taller and more imposing.

He wasn’t the “send a gift and want a date” type. He had his own business, his own car, his own city. When he came to Chengdu he’d behaved properly, with restraint and good manners; now she’d come to Xi’an, and he was hosting her, but he hadn’t begged her to come.

The thought let the thing that had been hanging in her heart settle a little. And once it settled, something else rose up — something she couldn’t quite put into words. As if she’d thought she was reaching upward, and now found that the thing she’d reached was more solid than she’d imagined.

Old Zheng said nothing. He drove on, turned off the Second Ring Road onto Yanta Road. Xiaoya looked out the window, then tilted her head to look at Old Zheng and said, “Big brother, you haven’t shaved.”

Old Zheng said, “It’s winter. Can’t be bothered.”

Xiaoya reached out toward his chin and said, “Prickly! What a he-man.”

Old Zheng didn’t answer. At an intersection the light was red, and they stopped. Old Zheng felt in the storage compartment for the pack of cigarettes, shook one out and asked Xiaoya, “You don’t mind, do you?” Then he lit it, took a drag, held it between his fingers and rested his hand on the edge of the car window.

Xiaoya watched his profile as he smoked. She suddenly thought: this man may be more capable than I imagined. And maybe more complicated than I imagined too. She’d thought he was just “a boss who runs a company.” Now, sitting in this car, she felt there was something else beneath those words “a boss who runs a company.”

The light turned green. Old Zheng stubbed out his cigarette in the car’s ashtray, put it in gear, and the car moved on.

Xiaoya said, “Big brother, where’s your company?”

Old Zheng said, “Over in the High-Tech Zone. A small building.”

Xiaoya said, “Can I go see it tomorrow?”

Old Zheng said, “Sure. I’ll take you tomorrow.”

Xiaoya said “Mm” and said nothing more. She drew her eyes back from the window and let them fall on her knees, then raised them again and glanced at Old Zheng’s hand on the wheel. The hand wasn’t young anymore; the knuckles were a bit thick, and there was a thin layer of callus at the web of the thumb.

She thought: these hands have done real work.

The car went on south. Xi’an in winter: the sky gray, the trees along the road bare, the Qinling Mountains in the distance showing a faint outline under the murky sky. Sitting in the passenger seat, Xiaoya felt that thing in her heart sink one moment and rise up again the next. She couldn’t say what it was; she only knew it wasn’t quite the same as before she came.

In the afternoon, Old Zheng took her to the Big Wild Goose Pagoda.

He didn’t think much about it. There were only a handful of places in Xi’an to take people sightseeing, and the Big Wild Goose Pagoda was the first. He parked at the South Square and they walked over. There weren’t many tourists in winter; the square was sparsely dotted with people. Xiaoya stood at the foot of the pagoda, tipped her head back to look up and said the pagoda was so tall, much taller than it looked in videos. Old Zheng said it was from the Tang dynasty, built by Xuanzang to store scriptures. Xiaoya said she knew — Journey to the West.

Old Zheng didn’t reply.

He stood by the railing on the north side of the pagoda and lit a cigarette. Beside him Xiaoya took photos with her phone — the pagoda, the sky, the mock-antique street in the distance. After a while she turned around and said, “Big brother, stay right there, don’t move. I’ll take one of you.”

Old Zheng said, “I don’t photograph well.”

Xiaoya said, “Just stand there.”

She held up her phone, head tilted to find the angle, and said, “Smile, come on, don’t be so serious.” Old Zheng tugged up the corner of his mouth. Xiaoya pressed the shutter, ran over to show him and said, “That’s great — you look like a boss.”

Old Zheng glanced at it and said nothing. He stubbed out his cigarette on a trash can.

Walking south, they passed a little slope planted with pines. In winter the pines were still green, only a dark, dull green. Xiaoya, walking beside him, suddenly slipped her arm through his. Old Zheng’s body stiffened for a moment; he didn’t shake her off, but he didn’t hold her arm back either. Xiaoya kept hold of him, saying nothing, and they just walked like that.

After a while she said, “Big brother, you’re not used to people taking your arm, are you?”

Old Zheng said, “It’s not that. I’m just not quite used to it.”

Xiaoya said, “Then I’ll let go.”

Old Zheng said, “No need.”

So Xiaoya didn’t let go.

That evening they ate at the Muslim Quarter. Xiaoya wanted to try everything. With the paomo — mutton soup with crumbled bread — she broke the bread twice and gave up, saying it was too hard to tear, and Old Zheng took it and finished crumbling it for her. She had a mouthful, said it was delicious, then went for what was in Old Zheng’s bowl. Old Zheng watched her eat and hardly touched his own chopsticks. Halfway through, Xiaoya looked up and said, “Big brother, why aren’t you eating?” Old Zheng said, “Watching you eat fills me up.” Xiaoya said, “That doesn’t sound like a compliment!”

Old Zheng grinned. “It is.”

On the way back after dinner, Xiaoya said, “Big brother, can I ask you something?”

Old Zheng said, “Go ahead.”

Xiaoya said, “Do you think I’m annoying?”

Old Zheng said, “No.”

Xiaoya said, “Then why do you never get in touch with me first? It’s always me sending a message, and only then you reply.”

Old Zheng was silent for a few steps. Even in winter the Muslim Quarter was crowded, the lights on, steam pouring out of every shop. He said, “Xiaoya, at my age, in my situation, it’s not appropriate for me to go chasing a young girl.”

Xiaoya said, “What’s appropriate or not appropriate? I’m not asking you to take responsibility for anything.”

Old Zheng glanced at her. Xiaoya looked back at him, her eyes bright under the streetlights, not dodging. She said, “I just like you. I know you probably think I’m flighty, saying something like this when we’ve only known each other a few days. But I just think you’re a good person. If you don’t believe me, there’s nothing I can do.”

Old Zheng said, “I believe you.”

Xiaoya said, “Then kiss me.”

And with that she stopped where she was. People passed by beside them; some turned to look. Xiaoya stood turned toward him, eyes closed, face tipped up, lips pressed lightly together, waiting.

Old Zheng didn’t move. Xiaoya opened her eyes. “Ugh, no fun. Come on, come on, let’s go…”

That night he took her back to her hotel and stood downstairs a while without going up. While Xiaoya waited at the elevator she asked Old Zheng, “Do you want to come up?” Old Zheng shook his head. Xiaoya thought for a few seconds, then, quick as a chick pecking rice, pecked a kiss on Old Zheng’s cheek, dragged her suitcase into the elevator, turned around and said, “Big brother, see you tomorrow.” Before Old Zheng had even recovered from his surprise, he said, “See you tomorrow.”

As he turned to walk out, there was a very strange feeling in his heart. What it was like, he couldn’t say. Not happiness, not unhappiness either. Just the feeling that this had happened somewhere before. Where exactly, he couldn’t remember, and he didn’t try to.

The next morning Xiaoya went to the Bell Tower on her own while Old Zheng had meetings at the company.

At noon, when the meeting broke up, he called her. She said she was eating liangpi, cold noodles, over by the Muslim Quarter, and told him to come. Old Zheng drove over and picked her up, and in the afternoon took her up on the city wall. In winter the wind was strong up on the wall and there weren’t many people. The two of them walked a stretch along the wall; Xiaoya leaned on the battlements, looked down and said the city of Xi’an was so perfectly square. Old Zheng said this was the Ming city wall, more than six hundred years old. Xiaoya said six hundred years — that’s longer than America’s history.

By the time they came down off the wall, dusk was falling. The two of them found a restaurant and ate, and when they came out all the streetlights were on. Xiaoya said, “Big brother, yesterday you said you’d take me to your company, and we still haven’t gone.”

Old Zheng said, “Now? Everyone’s gone home.”

Xiaoya said, “Better that they’ve gone home — no one there, nice and quiet. I just want to see what the place you spend every day looks like.”

Old Zheng glanced at her and said, “All right. Let’s go.”

When the car pulled up at the little building in the High-Tech Zone, it was already fully dark. There was hardly anyone in the park; only a few lights were still on in the building. Old Zheng went to the door and tapped a few keys on the combination lock. They went in; the lights in the lobby came on automatically, and the glass cases in the showroom gave off a cold gleam under the light.

Upstairs on the second floor was Old Zheng’s office. He didn’t turn on the main light, only the lamp on the desk. Xiaoya stood in the doorway, looked all around and said, “This is it?”

Old Zheng said, “This is it.”

Xiaoya went over to the whiteboard, looked for a while at the symbols and arrows she couldn’t understand, turned back and said, “You spend every day in here drawing this stuff?”

Old Zheng said, “Drawing this stuff, cursing people out, smoking.”

Xiaoya laughed. She went to the window, looked out and said, “It’s nice and quiet.”

Old Zheng said, “At night there’s no one around, so it’s quiet.”

Xiaoya said, “What’s upstairs?”

Old Zheng said, “The third floor’s a meeting room and a banquet hall. The fourth floor’s the dormitory.”

Xiaoya said, “Staff dormitory?”

Old Zheng said, “My dormitory.”

Xiaoya said, “I want to see! Take me to see it.”

Old Zheng was silent for a moment, then said, “There’s nothing much to see.”

Xiaoya said, “I want to see it, come on.”

Old Zheng glanced at her, said nothing more, and took her upstairs.

As they passed the third floor, Xiaoya glanced down the corridor. The door to the banquet hall was closed, but you could tell from its brass handle and the sconce beside it that it was a fancy place. She didn’t ask, and followed Old Zheng up to the fourth floor.

The corridor was very quiet, with only the sound of their two sets of footsteps. Old Zheng went to the door and tapped a few keys on the combination lock. Standing behind him, Xiaoya saw that he’d pressed six digits, and asked offhand, “What’s the code?”

Old Zheng said, “My birthday.”

Xiaoya said, “What’s your birthday?”

Old Zheng said six digits. Xiaoya made a mental note.

The door opened. The instant he pushed it open, something stirred in Old Zheng — this lock held another code too, Cong Shasha’s birthday. After the two of them had slept together, once their relationship had reached that point, he’d added it for her. Sometimes she came over on her own. Today she was in Beijing scouting ahead for him; she wasn’t even in Xi’an. But that code seemed to proclaim that Shasha was already the mistress of this room. Xiaoya stood in the doorway, knowing nothing. He didn’t dwell on it, pushed the door open and let her go in first.

She took a few steps forward and the first thing she saw was the huge floor-to-ceiling window. Outside were the park’s man-made water features, the streetlamps’ light falling on the water and breaking into pieces. By the window stood an enormous freestanding stone bathtub, strangely shaped. Further in was a two-meter bed with dark gray bedding, made up very neatly. Between the bed and the sofa stood a slatted screen; behind the screen was a wraparound sofa set, with a few documents and an ashtray on the coffee table. Against the wall was a whole wall of bookshelves, the rightmost section standing ajar, a little light coming through.

Xiaoya went around the room a few times, looking for a good while, and exclaimed, “Big brother, you live somewhere this nice?”

Old Zheng said, “The company provides it.”

Xiaoya went to the bathing area, looked at the tub and said, “This bathtub’s bigger than my bed at home.”

Old Zheng didn’t answer. He stood there and didn’t move.

Xiaoya came back, sat down on the sofa, turned to look at him and said, “Come over here.”

Old Zheng came in and sat down on the sofa. Xiaoya pointed at the hidden door standing ajar beside the bookshelves and asked, “What’s in there?”

Old Zheng said, “The study.”

Xiaoya said, “A whole other world in there. It’s even fancier than a five-star hotel.”

Old Zheng said, “It’s the company’s.”

Xiaoya came back and sat down beside him. The sofa wasn’t big, and the two of them sat close. Looking out at the night view, she said, “Big brother, living all alone in a room this big — doesn’t it feel empty?”

Old Zheng said, “I’m used to it.”

Xiaoya said, “Look at you, showing off.”

Old Zheng said nothing.

Xiaoya said “Hmm.” She drew her eyes back and let them fall on the wine cabinet at the side of the room. She went over and took a close look. “You’ve got a wine cabinet? You’ve got this much wine? You really know how to enjoy yourself!” she exclaimed.

Old Zheng smiled and asked, “Shall we have a drink?”

Xiaoya said sure. So he got up and went over to the wine cabinet. In the cabinet was a bottle of red that had already been opened, more than half of it left. He didn’t pick that one; he chose another bottle and opened it with the corkscrew. Two chilled stemmed glasses stood in the cabinet, crystal, with a cold gleam. He took both glasses out, poured Xiaoya a glass first, using the clean one, and then poured one for himself.

The wine was deep red, and poured into the glass it swirled a ring of dark light under the floor lamp. He handed her the glass and said, “To you.”

Xiaoya took it, turned the glass to look at it and said, “This wine’s not cheap, is it?”

Old Zheng said, “Someone gave it to me. I don’t really know about it.”

Xiaoya said, “These glasses are pretty too.”

She took a sip. Old Zheng drank too. As the wine went down, the mood loosened further, and Xiaoya said, “Big brother, tonight — can I not leave?”

Old Zheng looked at her. He knew what those words meant. Yesterday in the hotel lobby she’d said “Come up for a bit,” and he’d said no. Today she’d followed him here, was standing in the room where he lived, saying “Can I not leave?” This time it was closer than yesterday, and harder to refuse.

He was silent for a few seconds. He said, “Xiaoya, have you forgotten what I said yesterday?”

Xiaoya said, “I haven’t forgotten. You said you can’t give me anything.”

Old Zheng said, “And you’re still asking today.”

Xiaoya said, “I’m asking because I want to. Whether you want to is your business.”

Old Zheng looked at her. Her eyes shone in the dim light, not dodging, not pressing either. She just looked at him, waiting for him to speak.

He said, “Xiaoya, listen to me. This room — I didn’t buy it; the company fitted it out. This building — the company rents it. Me, I’m really just an empty shell. Even if you stayed, tomorrow you’d go back to Chengdu, and I wouldn’t even dare say the words ‘I’ll take responsibility.‘”

Xiaoya said, “Have I asked you for anything, that you’d have to take responsibility? Can you stop thinking so much?”

Old Zheng said, “I’m thinking too much?”

Xiaoya looked at him and said, “Big brother, you think way too much! It’s late, I’m going back.”

Old Zheng said, “I’ll take you.”

Xiaoya said, “No! You’ve been drinking.”

Old Zheng said, “It’s fine, I’ll get a cab and take you.”

Xiaoya looked up and said, “Then you’d only have to come back again. Don’t bother, really.”

Old Zheng said, “It’s only five kilometers there and back.”

“All right, then.”

The two of them went downstairs. At the door Old Zheng called a car on his phone. While they waited, neither of them said a word, standing on the steps; the wind was a bit cool. When the car came, Old Zheng opened the back door, let Xiaoya get in first, and got in himself from the other side.

The car pulled away onto the road. Xiaoya leaned against the window, looking out at the streets. The light inside the car was off, the streetlights swept past the window one after another, and light and shadow swayed across her face.

After a while she suddenly turned her head and looked at Old Zheng. Old Zheng looked at her too.

Xiaoya leaned over and kissed him on the lips. This time it wasn’t a chick pecking rice; it was slow and earnest. Old Zheng didn’t pull away, and didn’t push her off. His hand came up and rested on her back. The two of them kissed for a while, then Xiaoya drew back, leaned against her own seat and caught her breath.

She said, “Big brother, why did you always turn me down before?”

Old Zheng said, “You’re too much like her. I’m scared.”

Xiaoya said, “But I’m not her.”

Old Zheng: “I know. But I met you later than her.”

Old Zheng said nothing more. He looked at her for a while, then reached out, put his hand around the back of her neck and pulled her head to his, and they kissed deeply — this one lasting longer than the last.

When the car reached her hotel, Xiaoya got out and came around to the door on Old Zheng’s side. Old Zheng didn’t get out but rolled down the window. Xiaoya said, “Big brother, come on, get out with me.”

Old Zheng said, “I have to go to Beijing first thing tomorrow, and I’ve still got materials to prepare.”

Xiaoya said, “Oh! Then I won’t get to see you tomorrow.”

Old Zheng said, “I can have someone take you to the train station.”

Xiaoya said, “Big brother, you go take care of your real work. I’ll sort myself out — don’t worry about it. Go on, quick.”

Old Zheng put his hand out of the car window and gave a wave in the wind, and the car drove off.
